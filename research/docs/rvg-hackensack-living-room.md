# The Living Room on Prospect Avenue

*Rudy Van Gelder's Hackensack studio — history, atmosphere, and trivia.
Workshop piece for John, written 2026-10-21. Sources: Dan Skea, "Rudy Van
Gelder in Hackensack: Defining the Jazz Sound in the 1950s" (Current
Musicology); Don Ball, "If You Build It…" (NEA, 2017, drawing on Van
Gelder's 2009 NEA interview); rvglegacy.org (Van Gelder Estate photos and
the house blueprint); Wikipedia's Van Gelder Studio article. Epistemic
marks throughout; floor dimensions are genuinely undocumented (unk).*

---

## Whose house — and the surprise in the answer

The house belonged to Rudy Van Gelder's parents, at **25 Prospect Avenue, on
the corner of Thompson Street, Hackensack, New Jersey**. But here is the
twist that makes the whole story make sense: **the house was designed to be
a studio before it was built** (obs). Around 1945–46 — while Rudy was
finishing optometry college in Philadelphia — his parents were building a
new home, a one-story California-style ranch in cinder block with central
air conditioning, and Rudy convinced them to build recording in from the
blueprint. Where a spare bedroom or study would have gone, architect
Sidney Schenker drew a **purpose-built control room**, separated from the
living room by a large double-paned window — one pane angled slightly off
vertical to kill reflective glare — with a hole through the concrete-block
wall beneath it for microphone cables (obs, Skea). The Estate still has the
construction photos, dated October 27, 1945, and the blueprint.

So: yes, it was actually the living room — but a living room that had never
once been only a living room.

## The room itself

The living room was the live room; the musicians played in it (obs). What
made it sing, per Skea's account: a **ten-foot ceiling** — high enough to be
noticeable from the street, and "designed to improve the room's acoustics"
— plus a **wide archway into the adjoining dining room** that let sound
bloom, and corridors running off toward the kitchen and bedrooms that acted
as air columns of varying proportions. Van Gelder's own description, from
the 2009 NEA interview, is the one to quote (obs):

> "It was a U-shaped house, and there was a large center section. The
> kitchen would go off on one leg and the bedrooms would go off on the
> other leg and the studio was right in between. The kitchen was a hallway
> away from where I used to put Kenny Clarke."

Read that again: **Kenny Clarke's drums went in the hallway by the kitchen.**
And Rudy's verdict, in Ben Sidran's *Talking Jazz* (obs): "Acoustically it
sounded nice… it had little hallways and little nooks and crannies going
off. It was really nice. Nice place to record. I made some good records
there."

How big was the room in feet and inches? **unk** — no source I found gives
floor dimensions; the blueprint exists but its scale isn't published in
these accounts. The documented facts are the ten-foot ceiling, which "made
the room seem more spacious than it actually was," and the musicians'
consistent memory of being *close* — Savoy producer Ozzie Cadena (obs):
"They were all tight. Everybody was like close together. He just did it
electronically."

## The day job, and the atmosphere

Until July 1959, all of it happened around an optometry practice. Rudy kept
his office at **447 Cedar Lane in Teaneck**, ten minutes away, and recorded
at night, on weekends, and on **Wednesdays — his regular day off** (obs,
Skea). The atmosphere in the early years was exactly what "living room"
promises: musician friends paid nothing to record and a dollar an acetate;
drummer Sonny Igoe remembered "all those wax disks… going on the floor"
during late-night jams; pianist Billy Taylor brought Tatum records over so
the two of them could develop, by ear and trial, the piano miking that
became Van Gelder's signature (obs, Skea's interviews). The guinea-pig
honor roll of the late '40s living room: Lennie Tristano, Lee Konitz, Warne
Marsh, Zoot Sims, Gerry Mulligan.

The first commercial release out of the room wasn't jazz at all — blind
Paterson accordionist Joe Mooney singing "We'll Be Together Again"
(Carousel, 1951), rescued from obscurity when WNEW's Al "Jazzbo" Collins
played it daily on "Collins on a Cloud" (obs). The Blue Note door opened in
1952 when a Gil Mellé session Rudy made was sold to Alfred Lion — and when
Lion asked one of his own engineers to duplicate that sound, the engineer
told him to go back to the man who made it (obs, NEA). Thus the ten-year,
hundreds-of-records marriage.

## Trivia worth keeping

- The disk-cutting lathe shed lacquer chips, so **Leon Van Gelder** (Rudy's
  brother) mounted an **Electrolux vacuum** to the basement wall under the
  control room, drilled a hole through the floor, and ran auto-parts-store
  hose adapters up to the cutting head to suck the chips away (obs — Leon's
  own account to Skea). Their father, who had played cornet in a Navy band
  in WWI, built the control room space with his own hands.
- Van Gelder had the **second or third Telefunken U-47 condenser microphone
  in America** — and when close-miking distorted it, an Estonian engineer
  named **Rein Narma**, settled in nearby Bergenfield, modified the mic's
  amplifier circuit for him (obs, Skea).
- Miles Davis's icy muted-trumpet sound was captured with the U-47 placed
  **almost touching the Harmon mute** — intensity without volume (obs, Skea;
  critic Martin Williams called the resulting tone "one of the most personal
  and effective musical sonorities in jazz history").
- He spliced tape with a razor blade on the diagonal; he overdubbed as early
  as 1951 (Tristano stacking pianos; Teo Macero's five-part "Explorations,"
  1953; Bobby Sherwood playing all fourteen passes of a one-man-band single
  in 1954).
- His methods were so guarded that **the microphones were moved when
  photographers came in**, to disguise how he was recording (obs, Wikipedia).
- The last Hackensack session and the first Englewood Cliffs session were
  both led by **Ike Quebec**, in July 1959 — preserved as the singles
  collection *From Hackensack to Englewood Cliffs* (obs). And the very first
  session recorded in the famous Englewood Cliffs chapel was not a jazz date
  at all: **the West Point Glee Club** (obs, NEA).
- The house is gone. The neighborhood was subsumed by the Hackensack
  University Medical Center (obs, Wikipedia). Englewood Cliffs, by contrast,
  is on the National Register of Historic Places.

## Why the canon keeps two rooms

The canon's place vocabulary treats the living room and the chapel as the
separate rooms they were — and the counts make the point by themselves
(obs, `_jazzcanon`): **47 canon sessions at Hackensack (1954–1959), 137 at
Englewood Cliffs (1959–1971)**. Every methodology conversation about "don't
locate a session by its release year" (the *Workin'* trap) is really a
conversation about July 1959: the month an optometrist quit his day job,
moved four miles, and left behind the most consequential living room in the
history of recorded music.
