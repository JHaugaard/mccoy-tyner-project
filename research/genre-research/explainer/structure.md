# Proposed structure for the explainer page

**For John to accept, reject or rewrite. This is a shape, not copy.** No prose here is
meant to be published as written.

Working title: **Inside / Outside** — *a plain guide to the shapes jazz has taken*

---

## Part 0 — What you're actually hearing
*Target: 100 words. The reader should feel oriented, not taught.*

Three things the reader needs before any genre name means anything:

1. Most jazz songs are **known** — not newly composed each time, but standards with a fixed
   melody and a fixed set of chords.
2. A small group is **improvising** over those known chords, making up the melody as they go.
3. A rhythm section — usually bass and drums — **keeps a steady pulse** underneath.

Everything that follows is a variation on: *what happens when players stop, or don't stop,
staying inside that fixed chord pattern?*

---

## Part 1 — Inside and outside
*The thesis. The longest section. No genre names in it at all.*

**The idea, in two sentences:** most jazz songs have chords everyone knows. Players can
play *on* those chords — that is called playing **inside** — or *off* them, breaking the
pattern. That is called playing **outside**. Jazz musicians have used those two words since
the 1940s, and most of the great arguments in the music since have been about which one
counts.

**Then three demonstrations, in increasing order of difficulty to hear:**
- Two players on the same well-known song. One stays with the chords. One leaves them and
  comes back. Both are playing the same tune. *(The listener needs to hear this once before
  they can hear anything else in the page.)*
- Why this is a live question and not a technicality: the inside player can play all night
  and never play the same version twice, because the improvisation happens *within* a fixed
  frame.
- **The twist, and the best thing on the page:** the line moves. Charlie Parker — whose fast,
  angular playing terrified musicians in the 1940s — was considered *outside* by many of
  them. The music that is now the safe, central, "inside" tradition was once the radical
  music that the establishment complained about. **Nobody is permanently outside.**

**Why this belongs first:** it needs no music theory, it makes every later section
intelligible, and it earns the page's title.

---

## Part 2 — The inside line, by ear
*Six sections. Each opens with how it sounds, and only then gives the name.*

| Order | Feels like | Name introduced | One-line hook |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Fast, angular, urgent; the rhythm section never stops | **Bebop** | "It sounds like the song is being argued with." |
| 2 | Calm, spacious, lots of air | **Cool jazz** | "The same job, done slowly." |
| 3 | Heavy, bluesy, swinging hard again | **Hard bop** | "Cool jazz answered by turning the volume and the feeling back up." |
| 4 | Less like songs, more like pieces | **Post-bop** | "The improvisation stops being the point and starts being the material." |
| 5 | The chords stop moving | **Modal** | "The song stops going somewhere, and the melody has room." |
| 6 | A groove you can nod to; an organ enters | **Soul jazz** | "Gospel and rhythm-and-blues underneath a jazz frame." |

Each section gets, in this order: **a description you can hear** → the name → one record →
one "here's what to listen for" cue.

> **Hooks are not cleared for publication.** All six are original, not borrowed — but hooks 3
> (hard bop) and 4 (post-bop) overreach, and hook 6 needs rewording or a credit. See
> **Appendix A** for the full provenance audit before any of these lines is reused.

**The "listen for" cue is the most important element and is easy to under-weight.** A
non-musician cannot follow "it uses the bop scale." They can follow "the horn keeps going
*up* instead of coming back down." Every cue must be physical, audible and immediate.

---

## Part 3 — The outside
*One section, two clearly marked subsections. Honest.*

**3a. It stopped being a tune — free jazz**
The chords go away entirely. The players are building the music as they go, from
improvisation, with no skeleton to return to. Ornette Coleman's *Free Jazz* (1960) named both
the style and, accidentally, everything around it.

**3b. It became music you stand up for — avant-garde jazz**
A different lineage: bigger structures, more composed, often loud. Not the same thing as 3a,
despite the two being constantly merged.

**The honest paragraph, which must be present:** this music is genuinely harder for some
ears, and the reason is worth saying plainly — *it removes the orientation cues a listener
has been relying on without noticing*. You have been hearing a melody and a set of chords
for the last ten records. Those are the handrail. Here the handrail is gone. That is not a
defect; it is a different kind of music. But a reader deserves to be told why they are
finding it hard rather than left to assume something is wrong with them.

**The gentlest door:** *The Shape of Jazz to Come* (1959) — inside melodies, outside solos.

---

## Part 4 — The electric turn
*Short. Its own axis, and it says so.*

Fusion is often filed with the outside branch, and that is misleading. Fusion is **loud and
electric**, not harmonically free — the players are still working inside chords, mostly on
repeating patterns called vamps. What changed was the volume, the instruments and the
audience. It is a different axis of change, not a different side of the inside/outside line.

**Bitches Brew** (1970) is the landmark. It is often described as wild, and it is — but the
wildness is in the sound and the studio, not in the harmony.

---

## Part 5 — Why the boxes don't hold
*What earns the reader's trust.*

**5a. The hinge records.** Two records, side by side:
- John Coltrane, *A Love Supreme* (December 1964) and *Ascension* (June 1965). Same
  musician, six months apart. The first is inside-modal; the second abandons the structure
  entirely. **The line is a door, not a wall.**
- Eric Dolphy, *Out to Lunch!* (1964) — sounds free, is tightly written down.

**5b. The traps.** A short list of the things people confidently get wrong:
*Kind of Blue* is not bebop. *Birth of the Cool* is a reaction *away* from bebop.
*Bitches Brew* is not free jazz. "Avant-garde" is not a synonym for "free jazz." ECM is a
record label, not a genre.

**5c. A small timeline.** Optional, and here rather than at the front, so it rewards rather
than gates.

---

## Part 6 — Where to start
*A short list, deliberately short.*

The temptation is to give a list per genre. I'd resist. For someone who has just read the
page, **four or five records is plenty**, and the selection principle should be: *one that is
hard to put down, one that is calm, one that is the best argument for the outside branch, and
one that is simply the most beautiful record in the set.* Not a taxonomy — a start.

**This section is blocked on the open task in `README.md`:** the current candidates were
selected by what's in the canon, which is the wrong criterion and, for bebop specifically,
would give a beginner a genuinely bad recommendation.

---

**What to cut if it runs long**

In order: Part 4 (compress to three sentences), the Part 2 "listen for" cues down to one
each, then Part 5b. **Never cut Part 1** — it is the page.

# Appendix A — Provenance and audit of the Part 2 one-line hooks

*Added after John asked whether the six hooks were borrowed or original. This is a working
note, not copy. Nothing here is written for the reader.*

## The short answer

**All six hooks are original coinage**, drafted whole-cloth into this file when the outline was
written. None is a quote, and none is a paraphrase of a quote.

Verified by searching all 51 raw source files under `genre-research/raw/` for each phrase. The
only hit was *"argued with"*, in a ResearchGate excerpt (`raw/pod-book-cooks-lee.txt`) about
debating an editorial rule — unrelated to bebop. Pure coincidence.

**What this means practically:** the sentences themselves are free to use, with no attribution
owed. What cannot be done is presenting them as a critic's judgment. They are Space Bunny's
critical judgment, and in two cases they assert more than the research supports.

**Overall verdict per hook:** 1, 2, 5 are safe and good. 6 needs rewording or a credit. 3 and 4
overreach, and in both cases the sources already contain something better than what was drafted
here.

---

## Hook-by-hook

### 1. Bebop — "It sounds like the song is being argued with."

**Status: original, safe.** The idea behind it (the familiar tune reduced to a frame) is well
grounded in the sources.

**A real quote is available if authority is wanted instead of flourish:** Kenny Clarke, one of
the players who invented the style — *"'Bebop' was a label that certain journalists later gave
it, but we never labeled the music. It was just modern music, we would call it. It wouldn't be
anything, really, just music."* (quoted in the Bebop Wikipedia article, which is our source;
see `long-form/genres/bebop.md`).

**Caution:** the hook cuts against a point worth making. The players themselves rejected the
framing. Fine as a listener-facing image; misleading if it implies the boppers thought of it that
way.

### 2. Cool jazz — "The same job, done slowly."

**Status: original, and the tightest of the six.** A fair compression of what the sources say.
Britannica's cool-jazz entry has *"understatement and a more relaxed expression replaced
extroversion and high-tension virtuosity."*

**Real quotable alternative — and a gift for Part 5:** Gerry Mulligan himself, on why his band
was called cool — the idea *"was not to get away from bebop, but just to try to get a good
little rehearsal band together. Something to write for… As far as the 'Cool Jazz' part of it, all
of that comes after the fact"* (Mulligan quoting Feather, via Wikipedia; see
`long-form/genres/cool-jazz.md`). The genre named itself backwards — a strong beat for the
"boxes don't hold" section.

### 3. Hard bop — "Cool jazz answered by turning the volume and the feeling back up."

**Status: original, but the weakest claim on the page. Do not publish as written.**

Half true, and it flattens the best material we have:

- **The Rosenthal account locates the roots elsewhere.** David Rosenthal's account, treated as
  the standard one in `long-form/genres/hard-bop.md`, roots hard bop primarily in the R&B scene,
  not in an anti-cool reaction — it grew on Black pop music *"at a time when bebop seemed to
  have lost both its direction and its audience."*
- **Racial history is erased.** The sources are blunt that cool was mostly white and West Coast,
  hard bop mostly Black and East Coast, and that the reaction reading itself carries racial
  politics. A hook that reduces hard bop to a volume knob cannot carry that.

**Better construction:** keep "the volume and the feeling back up" as the *sound* description,
but pair it with Scott Yanow's plain formulation — tempos could be just as blazing as bebop while
*"the melodies were generally simpler, the musicians… tended to be familiar with (and open to the
influence of) rhythm & blues"* (AllMusic, quoted on Wikipedia: Hard bop). Honest, sourced, and
the R&B point is the interesting one.

**Structural note:** the R&B-roots story is better writing *and* better for the reader, but it
puts the Civil Rights era inside a genre section. If that story is wanted, the structure needs a
small adjustment — flagged for John's decision, not made unilaterally.

### 4. Post-bop — "The improvisation stops being the point and starts being the material."

**Status: original, but overreaches.** See `long-form/genres/post-bop.md` and its contested
section.

- **Waters's account has post-bop *retaining* the machinery**, not abandoning it: head-solos-head
  format, walking bass, the blues inflections, the 32-bar song form. The bass gets a
  *compositional* role; the chords widen, they don't disappear.
- **Baker's phrase is the outlier, not the definition.** His "abandonment… of underlying chorus
  structure beneath improvisation" describes the extreme case, not the style as a whole.
- **The category itself is contested.** Waters treats post-bop as a tendency its practitioners
  did not use or recognize. The file records that no source was found adjudicating this.

**Options:** soften the hook to something about the harmony loosening rather than the
improvisation taking over; or lean into the contestation openly as a feature of the section.

### 5. Modal — "The song stops going somewhere, and the melody has room."

**Status: original, genuinely good.** The second clause does real work and matches the sources:
Miles Davis's reported attraction to *"slower, deliberate music that allowed the creation of solos
from static harmony rather than constant changing chords"*, and Peter Spitzer's vertical
(rapidly moving harmonies) vs. horizontal (modal forms running many measures) distinction.

**Caution:** the hook implies invention. Modal material is older than jazz, and there was a named
1950s "modalism" movement — Tristano, Thornhill — well before 1959. `long-form/genres/modal-jazz.md`
already states "it is not a new idea invented in 1959." Either say so in the page, or the hook
sets up a claim the page then has to walk back.

### 6. Soul jazz — "Gospel and rhythm-and-blues underneath a jazz frame."

**Status: original in wording, but the closest to borrowed — it is a compression of a standard
genre description.**

- **Carnegie Hall's timeline** has soul jazz digging *"deeply into the spirit and flavor of
  gospel"* and *"emphasiz[ing] melodic hooks and repetitive rhythmic grooves, including a bass
  line played in the syncopated tradition of rhythm and blues."*
- It also reads as a genre summary because it *is* one — this is close to how the style is
  conventionally described, which is why it feels borrowed rather than written.

**Options:** credit Carnegie Hall explicitly, or re-voice it enough that it is clearly John's own
compression. Recommendation: **re-voice.** The hook duplicates the work of the "how it sounds"
line that already precedes it in the section template, so a credit costs more than it buys.
