# The explainer path

**Audience:** John's friends and others who do not know jazz, and who have probably never
heard the words *bebop*, *modal* or *post-bop*. They are not trying to learn jazz history.
They want to be able to follow a conversation and not feel lost.

**Deliverable John writes:** one simple page that orients without intimidating.

---

## The core problem with a genre page

The obvious structure — a section per genre, in order — is **the wrong entry point for this
audience**, and it fails for a specific reason.

A reader who has never heard of these categories does not have a taxonomy to navigate. If
the page opens with "Bebop, 1945–48," the reader's first experience is a wall of labels
attached to nothing. The labels are *retrospective*: critics named these things after the
music existed. **The page has to hand over the experience first and the name second**, or the
names have nothing to attach to.

There is a second, related trap. Our own research organised itself genre-by-genre, because
that is how the scholarship and the canon are organised. Importing that structure into a
beginner page would launder our source material's shape into the reader's first contact with
the music.

## The principle

**Experience first, names second. Chronology third, if at all.**

The reader has already heard jazz, or something adjacent. They can already hear that some
jazz is fast and frantic, some is calm and spacious, some is heavy and bluesy, some is loud
and electric, and some has apparently stopped being a tune. That perception is the actual
entry point. The genre names are then introduced as *handy labels for things you have already
heard* — which is what they are.

This also has a payoff for the page title. **"Inside/outside" is not a genre, it is a single
distinction, and it is the one distinction that can be explained to a beginner in two
sentences with no music theory at all:**

> Most jazz songs have a fixed set of chords everyone knows. You can play *on* those chords
> or *off* them. Musicians have called those two things "inside" and "outside" since the
> 1940s, and almost every argument in jazz history since is an argument about which one
> matters.

That is a complete, true, load-bearing idea. If the reader leaves with only that, the page
has done its job.

## Structure — my recommendation

Full detail in `structure.md`. In outline:

| Part | Job | Rough weight |
|---|---|---|
| 0. What you're actually hearing | 60 seconds of orientation. Improvised melody over a known chord pattern, small group, steady pulse. Two sentences that make everything else possible. | short |
| 1. The one distinction | Inside / outside, plainly, with two or three records. Includes the twist: the line moves, and Parker was once the rebel. | **longest — this is the thesis** |
| 2. The inside line, by ear | Six sections named for how the music *feels*, not for its genre. Genre name introduced inside each, after the description. | long |
| 3. The outside | Free jazz and the avant-garde. Honest about the difficulty, with the gentlest available door. | medium |
| 4. The electric turn | Fusion, on its own axis, opening by saying it is a *different* argument. | short |
| 5. Why the boxes don't hold | The seams, the hinge records, the mislabel traps. This is what makes the reader trust the rest. | medium |
| 6. Where to start | A short list. Fewer records than you think. | short |

### The two structural decisions worth arguing about

**By-feel or by-chronology for Part 2?** I recommend **by-feel**, with a small timeline near
the end. Chronology is a narrative and narratives are easy to follow — but only if you
already know where you are in the story, and a beginner does not. A reader who meets
"hard bop" before they know that cool jazz preceded it and was overtaken by it has no
frame for the word. By-feel lets each genre be introduced by contrast with the one before
it, which is how people actually learn. **The cost:** the page loses the satisfying
before-and-after that a chronology gives. Worth a small timeline in Part 5 to recover some of
it.

**Should the outside be one section or two?** I recommend **one section, two clearly marked
subsections**, with one sentence saying plainly that free jazz and avant-garde jazz are
different things. Two separate top-level sections would over-promise; one undifferentiated
section would teach the error. This is also Open Question 3 from the orientation document,
still unanswered.

## Rules for this path

1. **No genre name until the reader has heard the thing.** Describe first, name second.
2. **Every technical term gets an immediate plain translation** in the same sentence. No
   glossary-only hand-offs. `structure.md` and `language.md` carry the working translations.
3. **Recommend the best record, not the owned record.** See the open task below.
4. **Be honest about difficulty.** Free jazz is genuinely hard for some ears. Say so; do not
   condescend, and do not pretend otherwise.
5. **Do not advocate.** John curates past his own taste and has said free jazz does not
   currently resonate with him. The page describes; it does not recruit.
6. **Short.** If it runs long it has become a history, and that is the long-form path's job.

## The open task this path inherits

**Every entry point in the existing corpus was selected from the canon, not from
listening.** mccoy nominated the gateways, the hinge albums and the mislabel-trap records
with the instruction "single album, all in canon." That is a defensible shortcut for a
collector and the wrong basis for a universal recommendation.

Concretely, this is now a live problem rather than a theoretical one. The canon's entire
bebop holdings are five Savoy and Dial records from 1945–48 — a set of period transfers and
compilations. Handing that to a beginner as *the* gateway into bebop would be bad advice.
The bebop entry points need re-deriving from listening and critic recommendation, with no
reference to holdings.

This is the first thing to commission, and it is a good fit for **researcher**, which is
working again as of 2026-09-29.
