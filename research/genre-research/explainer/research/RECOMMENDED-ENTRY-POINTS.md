## The editorial test

Every recommendation below was selected using a rule that survived being argued with by three
independent agents. My original rule — that good on-ramps are *era-accessible and melodic* —
was found wanting. What replaced it:

> **Is there a line the listener could hum, over a pulse they could tap, arranged in a shape
> they can follow?**

Three terms, from two independent refinements: **hummable line** and **tappable pulse**
(researcher), **followable shape** (the transparent/opaque distinction). All three agents
agreed era-accessibility is a *proxy* for this, not the mechanism — and a bad one, since
*Giant Steps* was available and famous and is still a poor first record.

A 259-listener study in the outside dossier supports the physical half: listeners reliably
agree on *where* texture, volume and register change, and "the effect of musical training was
quite small." So the "listen for this" cues the page uses are not a stylistic whim.

## The page's short list

Per `explainer/structure.md` Part 6, deliberately short. Four records, chosen for function
rather than taxonomy — and **none of them was chosen because it is in the canon.**

1. **Moanin'** (Blakey, 1958) — the most physical, most immediately grooving thing here, and
   the one that converts hardest. mccoy's judgement from the original advisory: *"if he
   converts no one, nothing will."*
2. **Kind of Blue** (Miles, 1959) — the most beautiful and most welcoming. Note it is also
   the page's biggest *trap* in a different role: fine as a first modal record, misleading as
   a first hard bop record.
3. **The Shape of Jazz to Come** (Ornette, 1959) — the gentlest honest door into the outside
   branch, and the proof that "outside" can be melodic.
4. **In a Silent Way** (Miles, 1969) — the electric turn without the shock. *Bitches Brew* is
   the famous version and the wrong first one; the reception evidence is that it is widely
   treated as a date, not a first record.

**If the page can hold one more:** *Waltz for Debby* (Evans, 1961), which `researcher`
supplied unprompted and which is arguably the most listenable record in the whole set for
someone with no jazz background at all.

## The finding that changes the bebop recommendation outright

This is the most practically useful thing the whole exercise produced, and it is
retrieved, not recalled.

**The canonical bebop and cool records are physically compromised, and the standard
compilations are the worst place to start.**

- The AFM/Petrillo recording ban ran July 1942 to November 1944, and wartime shellac
  shortages followed. The classic records are 78s gathered onto LPs afterwards.
- **Savoy's metal masters were lost.** Every bebop reissue therefore descends from a
  disc-to-tape transfer made around 70 years ago. There is a counter-argument in the sources
  — that a modern transfer from a commercial pressing is more musically transparent than the
  tape — so this is a live technical question, not a settled one.
- **Mosaic's definitive 10-CD bebop set deliberately excludes Charlie Parker** (JazzTimes).

And the two records everyone is told to start with are both *session logs*:

- ***The Charlie Parker Story*** is the complete 26 November 1945 session, all takes, and its
  most famous track has an unresolved pianist credit — Gillespie under the pseudonym "Hen
  Gates," or Sadik Hakim, with Lubinsky denying Hakim played at all. Miles's own
  autobiography says it was Dizzy, not Miles, on "Ko-Ko."
- ***Groovin' High*** spans three years with 37 credited players — five drummers, four
  bassists, four pianists. Scott Yanow's AllMusic note calls it "so-so" for brevity and
  incompleteness, while conceding the music is classic.

**The recommendation this produces: hand a beginner one 1945 Guild track** — Gillespie's All-
Stars, "Shaw 'Nuff," and on the same record "Salt Peanuts." One band, one session, one room,
one drummer.

The reasoning, which is the part worth keeping: a beginner's actual problem with bebop is
**not enough pulse to orient against.** A single quintet playing at one tempo supplies that.
A 37-player, three-year cross-section supplies it three times over and then abandons it.

This is a genuinely counterintuitive recommendation and it is the strongest argument for the
re-derivation John asked for: **the canon's bebop entry point, *The Charlie Parker Story*, is
the single worst candidate on this list.**
