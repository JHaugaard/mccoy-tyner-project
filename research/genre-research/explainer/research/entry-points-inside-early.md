## 1. The finding that should lead: compilation or original?

This is the strongest single result of the research, and it changes what a beginner should actually be handed.

### 1.1 What the canonical records physically are

The canonical bebop and cool jazz recordings are not albums. They are **mono studio sessions made between the mid-1940s and the mid-1950s**, originally issued on 78 rpm discs in 3–4 minute pieces, and only later gathered onto LPs by a producer or a label. — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool) "Most of them were originally released in the 10-inch 78-rpm format and are all approximately three minutes long."

The compilation is the native form, not an afterthought. The two most-cited bebop starting points are both Teddy Reig / Savoy constructions: — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Charlie_Parker_Story) released 1956, Savoy MG 12079, producer Teddy Reig, recorded November 26, 1945, described as "the first album that chronicles the entire session, recorded November 26, 1945, including all takes of all pieces." — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin%27_High_(Dizzy_Gillespie_album)) 1955 Savoy compilation, recorded 1945 – December 11, 1947, 13 tracks, 36:38.

`Groovin' High` spans nearly three years of sessions and credits **37 musicians** — 5 drummers (Big Sid Catlett, Kenny Clarke, Cozy Cole, Joe Harris, Shelly Manne), 4 bassists (Ray Brown, Slam Stewart, Curly Russell, Murray Shipinski), 4 pianists (Al Haig, Clyde Hart, John Lewis, Frank Paparelli). — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin%27_High_(Dizzy_Gillespie_album)) personnel list.

Catalogue number MG 12020 for `Groovin' High` — RECALLED (not confirmed in the captures read in this pass; the capture shows label Savoy, year 1955, and a Van Gelder/Yujiro Kasai remastering credit).

### 1.2 The sonic problem is documented, not alleged

Three compounding physical facts:

1. **The AFM/Petrillo recording ban, 1942–44.** James C. Petrillo "enforced a ban on recording for all union musicians starting July 31, 1942"; it ended piecemeal as labels capitulated, with Victor and Columbia doing so "in November 1944." — RETRIEVED (https://midfidelity.com/2023/09/27/why-bebop-doesnt-sound-good/) The same source states bebop's "recorded legacy of this new genre was truncated by" the ban.

2. **Poor shellac.** "Because World War II cut off access to India (where high-quality shellac could be imported) records made especially during and sometimes after the war suffer from poor, gritty and noisy surfaces. Sadly, Savoy's commercial pressings were made from that era's poor stock." — RETRIEVED (https://midfidelity.com/2023/09/27/why-bebop-doesnt-sound-good/)

3. **The metal masters are gone.** "Like some record companies' masters, unfortunately, Savoy's no longer exist. It's not known what happened to the metal parts… they were once transferred to tape." The source adds: "When the master source is missing, the true fidelity — however good or bad in the first place — is obscured to some degree." — RETRIEVED (https://midfidelity.com/2023/09/27/why-bebop-doesnt-sound-good/) The same article says the Savoy catalog was purchased by Concord Music Group in 2019. — RETRIEVED (same URL)

**Consequence — INFERENCE:** every bebop reissue a beginner can buy descends from a disc-to-tape transfer made decades ago from a 78 whose original lacquer master no longer exists. The sonic floor on the whole genre's catalogue is therefore a fixed property of the archive, not a mastering choice of any given reissue. This is the single most important thing to say to a reader who wonders why the genre sounds like it does.

**And a genuine counter-argument, in print:** the same source took a commercial pressing of Tiny Grimes's "Romance Without Finance" (Bird on sax), cleaned it, and reports "The result is of course more noisy than the tape transfer from metal parts. It's also complete, which strangely can't be said for the tape: the ending was cut off. In my opinion, we were able to achieve something that's more musically transparent, closer to, and does more justice to the original performance than any previous mastering." — RETRIEVED (https://midfidelity.com/2023/09/27/why-bebop-doesnt-sound-good/) That is one engineer's opinion stated in a magazine, not a measurement. INFERENCE: it is nonetheless the correct shape of the argument for a beginner — the defect is in the *master*, so no remaster can fully repair it, and a noisier source can beat a truncated one.

### 1.3 The definitive bebop box set deliberately omits the headline artist

Mosaic's 10-CD *Classic Savoy Be-Bop Sessions 1945-49*: "The only thing that keeps _Classic Savoy Be-Bop Sessions_ from being _Complete Savoy Be-Bop Sessions_ is that it excludes Charlie Parker's recordings for the label." The same review calls it "10 CDs of bebop in its ascendant era, featuring more than 20 headliners." — RETRIEVED (https://www.jazztimes.com/reviews/albums/various-artists-classic-savoy-bop-sessions-1945-49/)

Catalogue number MD 10-264 — RECALLED (not confirmed in the capture; the capture confirms 10 CDs and the Parker exclusion).

**Why this matters for a beginner, stated plainly (INFERENCE):** the label's own high-water mark for this music, assembled when superior tape and disc sources were available and when the sessions were still being actively researched, declined to include the most famous bebop musician in existence. Anyone building a first listening path is therefore building a path the archivists themselves did not build. That is a fact to disclose, not a problem to solve.

### 1.4 The two most-cited starting points, and their documented defects

**`The Charlie Parker Story` (Savoy MG 12079, 1956).** The pianist on the session is unidentified, and the credit has changed across pressings. On the earliest 78 releases "the pianist is listed as 'Hen Gates', a pseudonym known to be used by Dizzy Gillespie," used because he was under contract to Musicraft. — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Charlie_Parker_Story) Liner-note author John Mehegan "is under the impression that Powell was present, adding to the controversy over piano players." — RETRIEVED (same URL) Mehegan also records: "As a final irony, a pianist by the name of Argonne Thornton claims he played the date or at least part of it, although this is denied by Herman Lubinsky, who conducted the session." — RETRIEVED (same URL) Wikipedia's summary of the state of play: "It is also controversial, in that to this day it is unclear who the pianist and trumpet player are on all of the tracks." — RETRIEVED (same URL)

The trumpet on "Ko-Ko" is disputed too. "In his autobiography, Miles says it was Gillespie, and not him, who played on this track: 'I remember Bird wanting me to play "Ko-Ko," a tune that was based on the changes of "Cherokee." Now Bird knew I was having trouble playing "Cherokee" back then. So when he said that that was the tune he wanted me to play, I just said no, I wasn't going to do it. That's why Dizzy's playing trumpet on "Ko-Ko," "Warmin' up a Riff," and "Meandering" on Charlie Parker's Reboppers, because I wasn't going to get out there and embarrass myself.' —Miles Davis, _Miles: The Autobiography_" — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Charlie_Parker_Story) The source also notes "Some sources additionally state that a) Gillespie played both trumpet and piano on this track or b) it is impossible for Gillespie to have played both." — RETRIEVED (same URL)

**`Groovin' High` (Savoy, 1955).** "Jazz critic Scott Yanow concedes that the music included is classic, but dismisses the compilation over-all as 'so-so' because of its brevity, because of the outdated and lightweight liner notes and because the material presented does not represent the complete sessions at which the material was played." — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin%27_High_(Dizzy_Gillespie_album), citing AllMusic) The *Rough Guide to Jazz* describes it as "some of the key bebop small-group and big band recordings." — RETRIEVED (same URL)

### 1.5 So does that make them bad first experiences? — my answer

**No, but they are bad *first records*, and the reason is instructive rather than disqualifying.** This is INFERENCE throughout, resting on the retrieved facts above.

- `The Charlie Parker Story` is a *session log*, not a listening sequence. A beginner meets Bird's most famous tune, "Ko-Ko," surrounded by false starts and a disputed credit. That is a researcher's object. The audible result on first hearing is a performance that seems to wander without announcing where it is going. (INFERENCE)
- `Groovin' High` is a 37-player, three-year cross-section whose players change underneath you. A listener who cannot yet hold a single rhythm section in the ear has no continuity to hold on to. (INFERENCE) Yanow's "so-so" is a bibliographic verdict, not a verdict on the music — and the source itself concedes "the music included is classic." (RETRIEVED, above)

**Which edition I would actually hand a beginner, and why — INFERENCE, with the retrieved facts as the warrant:**

- **Bebop:** a single **track** from the 1945 Guild sessions, specifically Dizzy Gillespie and his All-Stars / Sextet, "Shaw 'Nuff" (Guild/Musicraft, 1945), and on that record "Salt Peanuts." Personnel for the Guild date: Dizzy Gillespie (trumpet), Charlie Parker (alto), Clyde Hart (piano), Remo Palmieri (guitar), Slam Stewart (bass), Cozy Cole (drums) — all RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/bebop-jazz/). It is **one band, one session, one room, one drummer** — the exact thing `Groovin' High` is not — and it is first commercial documentation of Parker and Gillespie together. (RETRIEVED, same URL) Rationale: the beginner's real problem with bebop is *not enough pulse to orient against*, and a single quintet playing at one tempo supplies it. A 13-track, 37-player, three-year compilation supplies it three times over and then abandons it.
- **Cool:** *Birth of the Cool* is already a single coherent compilation, so no substitution is needed — but see the note on which edition, in section 2.
- **Hard bop:** no compilation problem. These are original sessions on tape-to-disc masters, engineered by Rudy Van Gelder, and the beginner simply plays the record. (RETRIEVED, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Train_(album); https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/hard-bop/)

## 2. Bebop

**What the beginner is up against, in the sources' own words.** Bebop is "defined by fast tempos (typically 180-300+ BPM), complex chromatic harmony, and virtuosic improvisation over chord changes rather than melody." — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/bebop-jazz/) The Herbie Hancock Institute's teaching notes are blunter and better: bebop harmonies are more complex, "temperos are often much faster," "bebop heads are more intricate and difficult to play than regular melodies," and bebop "requires musical virtuosity and artistry to play it." The same document frames the social shift: "Whereas Big Band Swing was considered entertainment (i.e., dance music), Bebop was considered *art* music (like classical music, i.e., bebop was for listening only)." — RETRIEVED (https://jazzinamerica.org/ — Herbie Hancock Institute, *Jazz in America*, Grades 7–9 unit 6 PDF, captured in full at `hancock-pdf.md`)

One structural fact that makes bebop more navigable than its reputation suggests: "Bebop combo arrangements are simple and rarely written. The basic format is simply the head played in unison by the horns for the first and last chorus with a lot of improvised solos in between. The head serves as a 'frame' for the most important part." — RETRIEVED (Herbie Hancock Institute, URL above) **INFERENCE:** for a beginner this is good news — the underlying architecture is a plain frame-and-fill, not a through-composed form. What is dense is the material *inside* the frame.

### GENTLE — Dizzy Gillespie, "Shaw 'Nuff" / "Salt Peanuts" (Guild/Musicraft, 1945)

- **Artist / album / year / label:** Dizzy Gillespie and His All-Stars / Sextet; Guild Records (reissued on Musicraft); 1945. — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/bebop-jazz/)
- **Audible reason it works first:** the source itself nominates it: "'Salt Peanuts' is as good an entry point as exists: it's fast, funny, and structurally transparent enough that you can hear exactly what the bebop rhythmic feel means." — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/bebop-jazz/) It is also "one of the first fully realized bebop performances on a commercial label." — RETRIEVED (same URL)
- **Band / who it suits:** absolutely no prior knowledge. This is the one bebop item in the research that is defensible as a first-ever jazz record.
- **Listen for this:** the title phrase of "Salt Peanuts" *is* the bebop rhythmic figure — the guide notes the syllable appears in song titles as early as 1945 and that in this title the scat syllable is "literalized in music." — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/bebop-jazz/) So you can hear the genre's name and its groove in the same three seconds. Concretely: the accents land off the beat, hard, and the guitar and piano lock to that accent rather than to the ride pattern.
- **Note (RETRIEVED, same URL):** the source's stated recommendation for a first bebop *listen* is a slower or mid-tempo standard — "Round Midnight" (Monk), "A Night in Tunisia" (Gillespie), "Ornithology" (Parker).

### MODERATE — Charlie Parker, *The Immortal Charlie Parker* (Savoy MG 12001, 1955/56)

- **Artist / album / year / label:** Charlie Parker; *The Immortal Charlie Parker*; released 1956 (Savoy catalogue MG 12001), producer Teddy Reig; recorded September 15, 1944; November 26, 1945; May 8 & August 14, 1947; and September 24, 1948; 46:15. — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Immortal_Charlie_Parker)
- **Audible reason it works:** it is five *whole* sessions spanning the style's first four years, so a listener gets to hear the same musician change rather than hearing one night's worth of intensity stretched thin. — INFERENCE, from the retrieved session dates. Every track is a self-contained performance with a beginning and an end, and the disc is 46 minutes — a sit-down, not an endurance test. (INFERENCE)
- **Band / who it suits:** a beginner who has already had one *date* inside bebop (the Guild record above) and now wants to know what Parker actually sounds like over several years.
- **Listen for this:** the alto saxophone on the 1944 and 1945 tracks is thinner, faster, and more angular than anything you have heard; by the 1947–48 tracks it has weight and sustain. Five dates, one voice, visibly changing. (INFERENCE)
- **Caveat (RETRIEVED, same URL):** sessions are separated by years, so personnel and acoustic character change; the record is a curated selection, not a session log. Whether it presents master takes plus alternates — RECALLED (not confirmed in the capture read in this pass).

### MODERATE (second option) — Charlie Parker and Dizzy Gillespie, *Bird and Diz* (Clef/Verve, 1952)

- **Artist / album / year / label:** Charlie Parker and Dizzy Gillespie; *Bird and Diz*; released July/August 1952; Clef/Verve, producer Norman Granz; recorded February–May 5, 1949, and June 6, 1950; 24:58. — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird_and_Diz)
- **Audible reason it works:** the source states it "was recorded primarily on June 6, 1950" — one session, one band, one date. — RETRIEVED (same URL) Two tracks, "Passport" and "Visa," are Parker without Gillespie, from March and May 1949. — RETRIEVED (same URL) **INFERENCE:** for a beginner the great advantage is that the two most famous bebop soloists are on one disc trading the same tunes; you hear the idea rather than a personality.
- **Band / who it suits:** a beginner who finds solo *voices* easier to follow than rapid harmonic motion. This is the shortest record in the research at 24:58.
- **Listen for this:** on "Leap Frog," "An Oscar for Treadwell," "Mohawk," and "Relaxin' With Lee," you can hear two horns answering the same chorus in turn. (INFERENCE; track titles and durations RETRIEVED, same URL)
- **Warning — this is the edition trap within a record:** the CD edition appends alternates, breakdowns and false starts, several of them very short — "Relaxin' With Lee (Take 3 False Start)" at 0:04, "Leap Frog (Take 4 Breakdown Take)" at 0:13, "Leap Frog (Take 7 Breakdown Take)" at 0:14, "Leap Frog (Take 1 Breakdown Take)" at 0:26. — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird_and_Diz) **INFERENCE:** for a first listen, take the original 10-inch-era programme and skip the bonus takes; a four-second false start is not "more complete," it is an obstacle. (The page's track list shows 24 tracks including the 13th onward, which are the added takes.)

### DEMANDING — the *Charlie Parker on Dial* route, or the honest version of Parker

- **Status:** the eJazzNews essential-bebop canon lists "Charlie Parker on Dial, *The Complete Dial Sessions* (Dial Records, 1946-47)" as entry #1. — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/bebop-jazz/) Catalogue detail and audible description — RECALLED (not in the captures read in this pass; **do not quote specifics until verified**).
- **Band / who it suits:** a reader who has a steady pulse already and wants the chords.
- **Why it is harder, honestly:** the Herbie Hancock material names the mechanism precisely — "improvisation over chord changes rather than melody," tempos "typically 180-300+ BPM," and heads "more intricate and difficult to play." — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/bebop-jazz/; Herbie Hancock Institute PDF as above) A beginner at this stage has no reference melody to fall back on when the line stops being a tune. That is the whole difficulty, and it is structural rather than a matter of taste. (INFERENCE)

### TRAP — *The Charlie Parker Story* (Savoy MG 12079, 1956) and *Groovin' High* (Savoy, 1955)

- **Artist / album / year / label:** Charlie Parker; *The Charlie Parker Story*; 1956; Savoy MG 12079, produced by Teddy Reig; recorded November 26, 1945. — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Charlie_Parker_Story) Dizzy Gillespie; *Groovin' High*; 1955; Savoy compilation; recorded 1945 – December 11, 1947. — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin%27_High_(Dizzy_Gillespie_album))
- **Why a beginner should not start here:** these are the two records most often named as bebop's front door, and both are archives rather than experiences. `The Charlie Parker Story` "chronicles the entire session… including all takes of all pieces" — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Charlie_Parker_Story) — so the beginner meets the genre's most famous theme embedded in a document whose own personnel credits are unresolved, the pianist being unidentified to this day and the "Ko-Ko" trumpet attributed differently by different sources. — RETRIEVED (same URL, including Miles Davis's autobiography quotation) `Groovin' High` is 13 tracks drawn from 37 credited players across nearly three years. — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin%27_High_(Dizzy_Gillespie_album)) Yanow calls it "so-so." — RETRIEVED (same URL, citing AllMusic)
- **The honest counterweight:** neither record is bad music — Yanow "concedes that the music included is classic" — RETRIEVED (same URL) and neither is a *wrong* recommendation, only a wrongly *ordered* one. They belong after the Guild single-date record, when a listener has a rhythm section in the ear to attach to.
- **Listen for this (once you are ready):** on *The Charlie Parker Story*, listen for the difference between the master take of "Ko-Ko" and the false starts before it — same tune, same players, a widening-out process you can hear happening. (INFERENCE)

## 3. Cool jazz

**What the beginner is up against.** The Herbie Hancock Institute's unit 6 defines cool jazz structurally: "Whereas Bebop was 'hot,' i.e., loud, exciting, and loose, Cool Jazz was 'cool,' i.e., soft, more reserved, and controlled"; cool groups "ranged in size from trios to nonets"; "Classical" instruments "such as flute, French horn, tuba, and vibraphone (vibes) were often found in cool jazz groups"; cool jazz "was a blending of jazz and classical music" and "often included counterpoint, that is, two or more melodic lines occurring at the same time… this was different from Bebop which had its focus on one melodic line at a time." The document names Miles Davis as "the most important Cool Jazz pioneer" and *Birth of the Cool* as "the first important Cool Jazz album." — RETRIEVED (Herbie Hancock Institute, *Jazz in America*, Grades 7–9 unit 6 PDF, `hancock-pdf.md`; https://jazzinamerica.org/)

**The naming itself is unstable — and that is a caution for the writer, not just the reader.** Mark C. Gridley, in the *All Music Guide to Jazz*, "identifies four overlapping sub-categories of cool jazz," the first being "Soft variants of bebop, including the Miles Davis recordings that constitute *Birth of the Cool*; the complete works of the Modern Jazz Quartet; the output of Gerry Mulligan, especially his work with Chet Baker and Bob Brookmeyer; the music of Stan Kenton's sidemen during the late 1940s through the 1950s; and the works of George Shearing and Stan Getz." — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cool_jazz) The same page notes "The term *cool* started being applied to this music around 1953, when Capitol Records released the album *Classics in Jazz: Cool and Quiet*." — RETRIEVED (same URL)

### GENTLE — Chet Baker, *Chet Baker Sings* (Pacific Jazz, 1954)

- **Artist / album / year / label:** Chet Baker; *Chet Baker Sings*; 1954; Pacific Jazz Records, producer Richard Bock. — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chet_Baker_Sings)
- **Audible reason it works first:** it is **singing**. A lyric is a melodic line the listener already knows how to follow, and Baker's vocal line is described in the source as carried by a near-whispered delivery over familiar standards. — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/cool-jazz/) "Chet Baker's trumpet tone is one of the most immediately recognizable sounds in jazz: soft, conversational, near-vocal in quality, with minimal vibrato and an intimacy that felt almost private." — RETRIEVED (same URL) The same source says the record "brought cool jazz's emotional restraint to vocal performance" and "brought cool jazz to listeners who might never have sought out an instrumental record." — RETRIEVED (same URL) **INFERENCE:** a lyric removes the single largest barrier — having no melody to hold — without requiring the listener to give up the genre's sound.
- **Band / who it suits:** a reader who wants to enter cool jazz without meeting an instrumental head-solos-head form cold.
- **Listen for this:** Baker's trumpet comes in behind and around the words; where the voice is nearly a horn, you are hearing exactly what "cool" means in this style. The source's own reading: the album "highlights the relationship between Baker's voice and his horn." — RETRIEVED (https://www.allaboutjazz.com/chet-baker-sings-by-mark-barnett)
- **Provenance bonus:** "In 2001, the album received the Grammy Hall of Fame Award." — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chet_Baker_Sings)
- **Caution:** the eJazzNews artist profile gives the year as 1954 and the label as Pacific Jazz — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/cool-jazz/) — but a discography listing in the same capture file dates the album 1956. See [Contested and Uncertain](#contested-and-uncertain).

### MODERATE — Miles Davis, *Birth of the Cool* (Capitol T-762, February/March 1957)

- **Artist / album / year / label:** Miles Davis; *Birth of the Cool*; released February or March 1957; Capitol Jazz T-762, producers Walter Rivers and Pete Rugolo; recorded January 21 and April 22, 1949, and March 9, 1950, at WOR Studio, New York; 35:29. — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool)
- **Audible reason it works:** it is one band on one set of sessions, and the band plays with restraint the beginner can hear as space rather than as difficulty. The eJazzNews essential list calls it "The genre's founding document. Gil Evans's orchestration set the template for ensemble restraint, with French horn and tuba replacing conventional brass register instruments and every player instructed to pull back from bebop's assertive attack," with key tracks "Boplicity," "Jeru," "Move." — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/cool-jazz/)
- **Band / who it suits:** a beginner who has had some instrumental jazz and is ready for a *sound* rather than a solo. Nine players, not five — which is part of the point.
- **Listen for this:** tuba and French horn in the bottom of the texture, and lines played *under* the melody rather than over it. The Hancock material's description of counterpoint — "two or more melodic lines occurring at the same time" — is literally what you are hearing. — RETRIEVED (Herbie Hancock Institute PDF) and INFERENCE for the specific instruments on this record.
- **The contemporaneous reaction is worth giving the reader.** Winthrop Sargeant, classical music critic at *The New Yorker*, compared the band to "an impressionist composer with a great sense of aural poetry and a very fastidious feeling for tone color… The music sounds more like that of a new Maurice Ravel than it does like jazz … it is not really jazz." — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool, citing Gioia, p. 283) Yet: "Though he did not recognize the record as jazz, Sargeant said he found the record 'charming and exciting.'" — RETRIEVED (same URL) Count Basie, the Royal Roost headliner during the nonet's tenure: "Those slow things sounded strange and good. I didn't always know what they were doing, but I listened, and I liked it." — RETRIEVED (same URL) **INFERENCE:** the two quotes together are a usable teaching pair. "Strange and good… I didn't always know what they were doing, but I listened, and I liked it" is a near-exact description of the intended first-listening experience, from a musician in the room.

### MODERATE (second option) — the recorded state of the record matters more than usual here

- **Which edition, and why — INFERENCE:** prefer **The Complete Birth of the Cool** (Capitol, 1998, remastered by Mark Levinson), which "collected the nonet's live Royal Roost and studio tracks onto a single disc." — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool) Reason: the beginner's first problem with cool is *texture*, and the 10-inch 78s that all of this music originally appeared on cannot carry a nine-piece ensemble's texture at all.
- **One retrievable fact that constrains this advice:** most of the material "were originally released in the 10-inch 78-rpm format and are all approximately three minutes long." — RETRIEVED (same URL) So on the original issues the band is heard in three-minute fragments, and the identity of a "cool nonet" is never fully stated. That is a real defect in the *original artefact*, not in the music. (INFERENCE)
- **Full release history, retrieved:** the January 1949 tracks were released as two 78 singles in 1949; "Israel" and "Boplicity" were doubled onto a 78 and released in 1949; "Venus de Milo" and "Darn That Dream" followed in 1950; in 1954 Capitol put eight tracks on a 10-inch LP, *Classics in Jazz—Miles Davis* (Capitol H-459); in 1957 eleven tracks (all but "Darn That Dream") became *Birth of the Cool*; the twelve-track version arrived in 1972 as *Capitol Jazz Classics, Vol. 1: The Complete Birth Of The Cool* (M-11026), and "Subsequent releases have used this 1972 compilation as the template." — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool)
- **A caution about the completeness claim, INFERENCE and worth flagging:** the Wikipedia page records the 2001 reissue producer Michael Cuscuna's note beginning "All previous reis…" — RETRIEVED but **truncated in the capture**; the full text was not recovered. Do not characterise the completeness of the 1998/2001 editions without reading that note. See [Contested and Uncertain](#contested-and-uncertain).
- CM Blue Note A92 as a carrier of the Royal Roost live tracks — RECALLED (the capture attributes the Royal Roost live material to *Cool Boppin'* in 1991 and to the 1998 Capitol complete edition; it does not confirm an A92 attribution). Do not state it without a fresh check.

### DEMANDING — the nonet as an exercise in hearing more than one line

- **What it is:** the cool record at its most extreme, and the point at which a beginner's ear has to hold counterpoint without a melody to fall back on.
- **Why it is harder, honestly:** the Hancock curriculum identifies the two mechanics cool jazz adds to bebop — written arrangement ("Unlike Bebop, much of Cool Jazz was arranged (written) ahead of time") and simultaneous melody ("Cool Jazz often included counterpoint… different from Bebop which had its focus on one melodic line at a time"). — RETRIEVED (Herbie Hancock Institute PDF) **INFERENCE:** a beginner trained on bebop solos is being asked to stop listening for "the one who is improvising" and start hearing a texture. That is a genuine change of listening task, not a harder version of the same task.
- **The intellectual extreme in the same territory:** Lennie Tristano, whose work "included freely improvised tracks that predated free jazz by several years, a remarkable fact that positions Tristano as a bridge between cool's formal structures and the freedom that would follow." — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/cool-jazz/) **Not recommended as a beginner record under any circumstances.** (INFERENCE)
- **Listen for this (on the demanding material):** count how many distinct lines are going at once in any bar. If you can hear two, you are doing the thing cool jazz asks of you. (INFERENCE)

### TRAP — *Groovin' High* as a cool-jazz misunderstanding, and the general trap of the *Greatest Hits* package

- The specific trap inside this territory: **`Groovin' High` is not a cool record at all and is often reached for as one.** It is bebop, 1945–47. — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin%27_High_(Dizzy_Gillespie_album)) A reader who grabs the most-cited "classic jazz compilation" as a first stop gets bebop's speed and density without bebop's continuity. (INFERENCE)
- **Listen for this:** nothing to listen *for* — that is the point. The failure mode is audible: the personnel under you changes without warning, and there is no groove that persists to carry you across the change. (INFERENCE)

## 4. Hard bop

**What the beginner is up against.** The Hancock unit calls hard bop "in part, a reaction to Cool Jazz": "Many jazz musicians felt that with Cool Jazz, the music had become too 'classical' in nature, that is, too European (not enough 'blues')." — RETRIEVED (Herbie Hancock Institute PDF) It then splits the genre in two: "There were two factions of Hard Bop: *Funky Jazz* and *Mainstream*. *Funky Jazz* was relatively simple (simple melodies, simple chord progressions, simple forms); its essence was one of groove and feeling and was heavily influenced by blues and gospel music. *Mainstream Jazz* (AKA *Straight-Ahead Jazz* or simply *Hard Bop*) was far more complex… it was still about groove and feeling but added the complexities of Bebop." — RETRIEVED (same source) It names the central figures as Cannonball Adderley, Art Blakey, John Coltrane, Sonny Rollins and Horace Silver. — RETRIEVED (same source)

**This split is the most useful thing in the section for a beginner,** because it means "hard bop" is not one difficulty level. (INFERENCE) The Funky/Mainstream split gives a beginner permission to enter at the simpler pole and to know that the harder pole exists rather than concluding they have failed.

### GENTLE — Art Blakey and the Jazz Messengers, *Moanin'* (Blue Note BLP 4003, recorded 30 October 1958, released late 1958)

- **Artist / album / year / label:** Art Blakey and the Jazz Messengers; *Moanin'*; recorded October 30, 1958 at Van Gelder Studio, Hackensack, New Jersey, released late 1958; Blue Note BLP 4003, produced by Alfred Lion; 40:16. — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moanin%27)
- **Audible reason it works first:** the opening track is a **four-bar loop that does not stop.** The eJazzNews hard bop guide describes Bobby Timmons's "Moanin'" as "a gospel piano vamp so irresistible it practically demands a physical response, followed by a simple but perfectly constructed melody." — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/hard-bop/) It also records that the LP was originally self-titled, and that "the instant popularity of the bluesy opening track, 'Moanin''" led to it taking the song's name. — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moanin%27) **INFERENCE:** for a beginner the vamp is the whole gift. It gives a physical reference — you can feel where you are in the track without tracking a melody — and every solo on the album sits on top of a groove that keeps reasserting itself.
- **Band / who it suits:** a first hard bop record, full stop. Even the general beginner list calls it: "*Moanin' is one of the most accessible hard bop albums ever, built on blues, gospel influences and strong, driving rhythms*… its energetic-but-approachable sound has made it a perfect gateway for many newcomers." — RETRIEVED (classical-music.com beginner list, captured at `cm-essentials.md`)
- **Listen for this:** on "Moanin'," a call-and-response riff that repeats — described in the same general list as "one of jazz's great earworms." — RETRIEVED (classical-music.com) On "Blues March," the same source's list of essential hard bop tunes includes it from the *Moanin'* album. — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/hard-bop/)
- **Third-party corroboration of the ranking:** the eJazzNews hard bop guide's own answer to "What is the best hard bop album to start with?" is *Moanin'*, "the most common recommendation for first-time listeners, and for good reason. It's immediately accessible, rhythmically compelling, and features some of the genre's most memorable compositions." — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/hard-bop/) The classical-music.com list calls it "the genre's defining statement." — RETRIEVED (https://www.classical-music.com/, `cm-essentials.md`)

### MODERATE — John Coltrane, *Blue Train* (Blue Note, recorded 15 September 1957, released January 1958)

- **Artist / album / year / label:** John Coltrane; *Blue Train*; recorded September 15, 1957 at Van Gelder Studio, Hackensack, New Jersey, released January 1958; Blue Note, produced by Alfred Lion; 42:05. — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Train_(album))
- **Audible reason it works:** four of the five tracks are Coltrane's own, and "at this point his compositions used conventional diatonic harmonies" — RETRIEVED (same URL) — so the *writing* is not the obstacle. The obstacle, and the appeal, is the saxophone. NPR's assessment: "*Blue Train* has a beautiful blues feel that's absolutely infectious." — RETRIEVED (https://www.npr.org/2010/01/25/99865218/a-hard-look-at-hard-bop) The same source notes Coltrane "chose all of the musicians for this date, including Lee Morgan on trumpet and Philly 'Joe' Jones on drums." — RETRIEVED (same URL)
- **A production fact that matters to a beginner's first impression, and it is audible:** "Unlike Coltrane's previous label, Blue Note paid the musicians to rehearse the music before the recording session." — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Train_(album)) **INFERENCE:** rehearsed, hard bop is where the beat is placed. Bebop, by contrast, was developed largely in jam sessions where "there [is] very little rehearsal, if any." — RETRIEVED (Herbie Hancock Institute PDF) That single production decision is a large part of why hard bop sounds more like a groove and less like a scramble, and it is why this genre is the easiest of the three to enter physically.
- **Band / who it suits:** a beginner who can feel blues/gospel pulse and now wants to meet the more demanding pole of hard bop. It is Coltrane's only session as leader for the label. — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Train_(album))
- **Listen for this:** the title track's opening — a slow, heavy, harmonically unremarkable warm-up in which nothing happens for a while. It is the record showing you its hand before showing its trick. (INFERENCE)
- Also: gold certification from the RIAA in 2001. — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Train_(album))

### DEMANDING — Sonny Rollins, *Saxophone Colossus* (Prestige PRLP 7079, recorded 22 June 1956)

- **Artist / album / year / label:** Sonny Rollins; *Saxophone Colossus*; recorded June 22, 1956; Prestige PRLP 7079; personnel Tommy Flanagan (piano), Doug Watkins (bass), Max Roach (drums). Added to the National Recording Registry in 2016, with an essay by Hugh Wyatt. — RETRIEVED (https://www.loc.gov/static/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/documents/SaxophoneColussus.pdf)
- **Audible reason it works for this reader:** it is the record where you stop hearing notes and start hearing *motivic development* — the same short cell, reworked. The eJazzNews guide calls Rollins's approach "building an entire solo from a single rhythmic or melodic cell" and says the *Saxophone Colossus* material "remains the clearest demonstration of this approach in the recorded canon." — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/hard-bop/)
- **Band / who it suits:** the eJazzNews guide's own division of labour: *Saxophone Colossus* "is the better choice for listeners who want to understand hard bop improvisation at its most sophisticated." — RETRIEVED (same URL) Put it after *Moanin'*, never before.
- **Why it is harder, honestly:** the LOC essay notes Rollins "performs five lengthy songs" — RETRIEVED (https://www.loc.gov/static/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/documents/SaxophoneColussus.pdf) — i.e. solos that last, with no vocal hook and no recurring riff to re-find you. A beginner who is still using the four-bar vamp as a map will lose the map. (INFERENCE)
- **Listen for this:** on "St. Thomas," the same tiny rhythmic figure — the essay says Rollins gives the song "the kind of rhythm that makes the listener feel as if they are on the island" — RETRIEVED (https://www.loc.gov/static/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/documents/SaxophoneColussus.pdf) — returns again and again, inverted and re-timed, for the length of the track. If you can hear one cell going for four minutes, you have heard what this record is for. (INFERENCE)

### TRAP — *Kind of Blue* (Columbia, 1959)

- **Artist / album / year / label:** Miles Davis; *Kind of Blue*; 1959; Columbia. Recorded spring of 1959 over two sessions. — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/bebop-jazz/) for label/year; the spring-1959 two-session detail is RETRIEVED from the JazzTimes piece captured at `kindofblue-trap.md`.
- **Why beginners are pointed at it:** it tops the general beginner list, which describes it as "an album you can sink into immediately, yet it rewards deeper listening for years," whose "clarity, warmth and unhurried confidence make it the ultimate beginner's companion," and instructs the reader "Start here: 'So What'." — RETRIEVED (https://www.classical-music.com/, `cm-essentials.md`) It is entry #1 in the eJazzNews beginner list ("Absolute first listen"). — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/best-jazz-albums-for-beginners/)
- **Why a beginner should not start there — the trap is documented, twice, in print.** A JazzTimes column: "for the non-hardcore Davis people, *Kind of Blue* can function as a sort of trap, much like *The Great Gatsby* does for F. Scott Fitzgerald or *Citizen Kane* for Orson Welles. Items billed as THE BEST EVER can stop us cold, and even cause us to take them for granted, never reassessing them. Instead we gesture, often without thought, to where they sit in the corner, under a halo and backdrop of blue ribbons." — RETRIEVED (https://jazztimes.com/… — the column captured at `kindofblue-trap.md`) And from the *Los Angeles Review of Books*: "If Miles Davis has been the entry point into jazz for so many (the 101, if you will), *Kind of Blue*, his opus, is the first line of the syllabus. It's also, as Miles might've said, a cliché. And Miles abhorred clichés. Yes, it's *Catcher in the Rye*, *The Great Gatsby*, *On The Road*. It's so exceptional, it's unexceptional." — RETRIEVED (https://lareviewofbooks.org/… — captured at `kindofblue-trap.md`)
- **The structural reason (INFERENCE):** *Kind of Blue* is a **later** record than any of the three genres this file covers. A reader who enters at 1959 and works backwards will meet the earlier styles as pastiche — as the thing the modal record was reacting to — rather than as music that was once contemporary and urgent. The sequence has to run forward.
- **Second, independent reason (INFERENCE):** it is modal. It is *deliberately* harmonically static, and static harmony removes precisely the thing a beginner uses to tell one bar from the next. It is superb music and a poor first draft of a curriculum.
- **Listen for this:** nothing to listen for, on the first pass. The cue is behavioural — if you find yourself unable to say whether the music has ended, you are hearing modality, not a defect. (INFERENCE)

## 5. The seams

### 5.1 Where the three genres actually divide

The captures give three definitions that are consistent with each other and can be set side by side. This table is assembled entirely from retrieved text; the "what a beginner feels" column is INFERENCE.

| | Bebop | Cool jazz | Hard bop |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Core identity** | Improvisation over chord changes, not melody; focus on the soloist; arrangements simple and rarely written | "A blending of jazz and classical music"; counterpoint, two or more melodic lines at once; much of it arranged ahead of time | Blues- and gospel-based, Afro-centric; explicitly a reaction to cool's European-ness; split into simpler "Funky Jazz" and more complex "Mainstream" |
| **Source** | RETRIEVED — Herbie Hancock Institute unit 6 PDF (https://jazzinamerica.org/) and eJazzNews (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/bebop-jazz/) | RETRIEVED — Herbie Hancock Institute unit 6 PDF | RETRIEVED — Herbie Hancock Institute unit 6 PDF |
| **Ensemble** | "simple and rarely written," head in unison bookending improvised solos; 4–5 pieces typical | "ranged in size from trios to nonets (nine-piece band)"; flute, French horn, tuba and vibes common | "usually two or three horns plus… rhythm section" |
| **What a beginner feels** (INFERENCE) | *Where am I?* — no melody to hold | *Where is the one doing the thing?* — no single soloist | *I can feel it* — pulse is the ground floor |

**The single sentence that describes the axis (INFERENCE, resting on the three retrieved definitions):** the seam between bebop and cool is **who is heard** — one soloist at a time versus a texture — and the seam between cool and hard bop is **what the ground is made of** — a classical, arranged, counterpointed ground versus a blues-and-gospel ground. Cool is the point of maximum removal of the beat's physicality; hard bop is its restoration.

**The educational source agrees on the middle seam and puts it in print:** "Hard Bop was, in part, a reaction to Cool Jazz… too 'classical' in nature, that is, too European (not enough 'blues')." — RETRIEVED (Herbie Hancock Institute unit 6 PDF)

### 5.2 The best single record showing a transition in progress: *Saxophone Colossus*

**This is the one recommendation in the file that rests on a citable claim rather than on my judgement.**

The Library of Congress National Recording Registry essay by Hugh Wyatt, on the album added to the Registry in 2016, states: "**'Saxophone Colossus' remains, after more than six decades, one of the most awe-inspiring jazz albums of all time. It straddles the divide from classical bebop to hard bop and its newer manifestations.**" — RETRIEVED, verbatim (https://www.loc.gov/static/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/documents/SaxophoneColussus.pdf)

The essay makes the same point structurally: "The album was also important because it showed that Sonny had successfully made the transition from bebop to post-bebop styles on his own—freeing himself of the influences of the founding fathers of the tenor saxophone, like Coleman Hawkins, Chu Berry, and Lester Young." — RETRIEVED (same URL)

**Why this beats an editorial choice.** A Milestones-style pick is a curator's argument about which record best represents a transition; the reader has to take the curator's ear on trust. The LOC claim is different in kind: an institution's registry essay says in print that the record *straddles* the divide, which means the record itself exhibits both sides, and the reader can go and check that claim against their own ears. (INFERENCE) That is the property this file needs from a seam record — not "an expert recommends this," but "here is the evidence, and you will be able to hear it."

**What the reader should listen for, given the claim — INFERENCE:** the essay names specific features on each side of the line. "You Don't Know What Love Is" "shows the romantic side of Sonny. He proves to opponents of hard bop that the music can be delicate, while at the same time being straight ahead." — RETRIEVED (same URL) So: the softness is not cool's classical distance, it is hard bop's claim on delicacy. And on "St. Thomas" the essay describes a Caribbean rhythm strong enough to "make the listener feel as if they are on the island" — RETRIEVED (same URL) — which is a *groove* source, a hard bop source, arriving on a bebop-form record. If the reader hears one bar of Caribbean pulse inside a bebop form, the LOC sentence is being demonstrated to them.

**Recommended sequence (INFERENCE):** *Moanin'* first, so the hard bop pole has an anchor, then *Saxophone Colossus* to see the bebop end of the bridge.

### 5.3 The caution: the bebop/cool opposition is partly an artefact of presentation

- **The term is unstable by the sources' own account.** Cool jazz is not one thing: Gridley identifies "four overlapping sub-categories," the first of which is explicitly "Soft variants of bebop, including the Miles Davis recordings that constitute *Birth of the Cool*." — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cool_jazz) **INFERENCE:** if the first sub-category of cool is bebop that has been slowed down, then "bebop vs cool" is a difference of degree and presentation, not of kind.
- **The label arrived after the music.** "The term *cool* started being applied to this music around 1953, when Capitol Records released the album *Classics in Jazz: Cool and Quiet*." — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cool_jazz) **INFERENCE:** musicians in 1949 were not playing a different genre because a word existed; the word was attached to music that was already being made. This is the clearest single piece of evidence that the seam is a naming seam.
- **And the two styles were presented as a set, not as rivals.** Norman Granz's Jazz at the Philharmonic, 1944–1983, was "a series of jazz concerts, tours and recordings produced by Norman Granz" whose programme "featured many of the era's preeminent musicians." — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz_at_the_Philharmonic) **INFERENCE (marked as such because the capture does not make the point explicitly):** JATP billeted bebop and the West Coast players on the same concerts and the same records, on the same bill, for two decades. A division that survives being billed as a single package all evening is a division of marketing more than of music. *I did not recover a passage in the captures stating this in so many words — treat the argument as inference and do not source it to the JATP page as a claim the page makes.*
- The eJazzNews cool jazz guide's inclusion of Tristano, whose freely improvised work "positions Tristano as a bridge between cool's formal structures and the freedom that would follow," — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/cool-jazz/) is a further reminder that the cool category is a convenience, not a boundary. (INFERENCE)

### 5.4 The other seams worth showing a reader

- **Bebop → cool:** *Birth of the Cool* itself. The eJazzNews account is that "Gil Evans's orchestration set the template for ensemble restraint, with French horn and tuba replacing conventional brass register instruments and every player instructed to pull back from bebop's assertive attack." — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/cool-jazz/) That is a transition *in instrumentation*, which a beginner can see and count, not just hear. (INFERENCE)
- **The documentary detail that makes the nonet's own status visible:** Pete Rugolo signed the group to Capitol after hearing them at the Royal Roost in September 1948; the nonet played a two-week engagement there, supported Count Basie, and the recordings "were released over the next few years as a series of influential—but not best-selling—78s. Their initial impact was relegated to jazz critics and musicians." — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool) **INFERENCE:** this is the best available evidence for the "accessible within its own era" hypothesis — this band was accessible *and* influential to exactly the wrong audience at the time. (Which does not, on its own, settle the hypothesis. See below.)

## 6. The pattern: three positions, reported honestly

The original hypothesis (mine, the commissioning agent's): **the best on-ramps are the records accessible within their own era and melodic, rather than the most radical or harmonically adventurous.**

Two other agents have partially demolished it, in two different ways. All three positions are reported below. No synthesis is offered as settled fact.

### Position 1 — the original hypothesis

Records that a listener of the era could readily hear are better first records than the radical ones. Formulated in terms of *melody* as well as accessibility. **Status:** the hypothesis under test. Not independently supported by any retrieved source in this research. Partially supported, in one instance, by the retrieved fact that *Birth of the Cool* was "influential—but not best-selling" and its "initial impact was relegated to jazz critics and musicians." — RETRIEVED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool) That fact supports *influential-but-not-widely-heard*, which is not the same as *accessible*.

### Position 2 — the "researcher" refinement: singable line over a steady, perceptible pulse

Claim: the hypothesis is mostly right but wrongly formulated. The real predictor is **a singable line over a steady, perceptible pulse**, and that combination survives considerable harmonic or formal radicalism.

Counterexamples offered, both of which are radical and both of which the refinement predicts should work:
- **John Coltrane, *A Love Supreme*** — harmonically advanced, but carrying a chant-like theme a child could sing. **Status of the record's existence and dates:** the album is referenced in a captured beginner list alongside the note that "*Blue Train* is a slightly more accessible entry point with its hard bop energy." — RETRIEVED (classical-music.com list, `cm-essentials.md`) **The description of its theme and its harmonic content is RECALLED** — carried from the parallel agent's argument; **no capture in this file was located that describes the theme as chant-like.** Do not publish that characterisation without a fresh check.
- **Ornette Coleman, *The Shape of Jazz to Come*** — formally radical, yet more melodic than most bebop. **Status: entirely RECALLED.** No capture in this file covers this record. Do not publish a description of it from this file.

### Position 3 — the parallel agent's refinement, from the later styles: transparent vs opaque

Claim: "accessible within its era" is **not even sufficient**. The counterexample offered is *Giant Steps*, which was a hit — accessible to its own audience and still difficult. The real variable is **TRANSPARENT versus OPAQUE**, not adventurous versus simple: modal harmony is theoretically fancy but transparent.

**Status:** *Giant Steps* is **entirely RECALLED** — no capture in this file covers it. The "transparent vs opaque" framing is a borrowed distinction from timbral and textural legibility, not from anything in the retrieved sources here. Treat it as a hypothesis imported from another style's research, not as a finding about bebop, cool or hard bop.

### Are positions 2 and 3 compatible, or in tension?

**I cannot settle this, and I think the captures cannot settle it either — there is no retrieved evidence in this file bearing on it directly.** What I can say is the following, and the first point is the only one I would put weight on.

1. **The two refinements are not obviously in tension, because they are asking about different records.** (INFERENCE) Position 2's counterexamples are records that are *melodically* open despite being harmonically or formally radical. Position 3's counterexample is a record that was *socially* accessible — a hit, an audience favourite — and was still hard. These are compatible if the predictor is perceptual (can I follow a line? is the texture legible?) rather than social (was it popular with people like me?) or harmonic (are the chords simple?). A modal record with a clear pulse passes both tests. So does a harmonically radical record with a chant. So far the two refinements are *complementary*: each supplies a necessary condition the other lacks.
2. **Where they could come into tension (INFERENCE, and this is a real tension, not a hedge):** Position 2 makes *melody* load-bearing — "a singable line." Position 3 does not require melody at all; it requires that the *texture* be transparent, which a purely instrumental, melodic-free but very clear texture could satisfy. If a beginner is given a piece of hard bop with no singable line but an extremely clear, steady, audible groove, position 2 says it fails and position 3 says it passes. *Moanin'* is close to the test case and sits on the favourable side of it — it has both. The captures contain no record in this territory that cleanly separates the two, so the tension is currently unfalsified in either direction.
3. **Both refinements are weaker than the original hypothesis's own claim, and neither rescues it.** (INFERENCE) Both agents moved the predictor from a *social/historical* property (was it accessible to its own audience) toward a *perceptual* property (can I follow it). That is a real improvement, because the perceptual property is the one that actually governs a first listening experience, and because it is testable by the reader. But it means the original hypothesis's most interesting content — the historical claim that a record's contemporaneity is what makes it a good on-ramp — has been quietly dropped by both refinements rather than refuted. Nobody has shown it is wrong. It has been replaced.
4. **My own view, marked as INFERENCE and explicitly not a retrieved finding:** the three positions converge on something narrower and more useful than any of them, and the captures do support it. The retrieved eJazzNews material for *Saxophone Colossus* describes hard bop improvisation as "building an entire solo from a single rhythmic or melodic cell." — RETRIEVED (https://ejazznews.com/jazz-education/hard-bop/) That is a **cell** — a repeated, physically audible unit — carrying a five-minute solo with no melody in the traditional sense. Under position 2, that record should fail (no singable line). Under position 3, it should pass (the cell is transparent). The researcher's own guide calls it "the better choice for listeners who want to understand hard bop improvisation at its most sophisticated," — RETRIEVED (same URL) — i.e. it is a *later* record, not a first one. The record does not adjudicate the two positions because it is not an entry point under either of them. **The captures contain no entry-point record that tests the disagreement.** That is the honest state of the question.

**What would settle it, stated so a later pass can do it:** find, within bebop, cool or hard bop only, two records that a beginner-list source actually recommends to newcomers, one with a singable line over a steady pulse but no repeated rhythmic cell, and one with a repeated cell but no singable line. If the two recommendations diverge in how they are described as beginner material, that is evidence. Nothing in the current banked material does this.
