`researcher` cross-check, and the existing corpus. Written after

## 1. The pattern question — three answers, and why I think two of them are compatible

I put a hypothesis to every agent: *the best on-ramps are the records accessible within
their own era and melodic, rather than the most radical.* All three came back having partly
demolished it, in different language.

**Position A — mine:** era-accessibility plus melodic writing.

**Position B — `researcher`:** "mostly right, wrong formulation." The operative variable is
**a singable line over a steady, perceptible pulse** — and that quality survives considerable
harmonic and formal radicalism. Counterexamples offered: *A Love Supreme* (harmonically
advanced, but a chant-like theme a child could sing) and *The Shape of Jazz to Come*
(formally radical, yet more melodic than most bebop).

**Position C — the later-styles agent:** "accessible within its era" is not even
*sufficient* — *Giant Steps* was a hit and still a bad first record. The real variable is
**transparent versus opaque**, not adventurous versus simple. Modal harmony is theoretically
fancy but transparent, which is why *Kind of Blue* beats *Maiden Voyage* as a first record.

### My reading: B and C are compatible, and together they demote my original to a proxy

*(This paragraph is my inference, not a retrieved finding — flagging it as such.)*

B and C are not competing descriptions of the same thing. They diagnose **different failure
modes**, and a record can fail either:

- **C is about form.** Can the listener tell what is going on — is there a shape, a repeat, a
  return? A record can be formally followable and still be a poor door because its line is
  unmemorable.
- **B is about line and pulse.** Is there something to hold onto melodically and
  physically? A record can have a beautiful line over no perceptible pulse, and it will lose
  the listener anyway.

Both **agree on the thing that matters most**: era-accessibility is a *proxy*, not a
mechanism, and it is a poor one. That is the real convergence, and it is the finding I would
put weight on.

**The practical form for the page**, and this is the useful output: an editorial test the
page can actually apply to any candidate record —

> **Is there a line the listener could hum, over a pulse they could tap, arranged in a shape
> they can follow?**

All three terms, from two independent agents. A beginner-facing page that applied only to
"accessible in its era" would have put *Giant Steps* in front of someone. It would not have
put *Shape of Jazz to Come* or *A Love Supreme* there — which is the case the other two
positions exist to make.

**A 259-listener empirical study, retrieved in the outside dossier**, backs the physical half
of this: listeners reliably agree on *where* texture, volume and register change, and "the
effect of musical training was quite small." So the perceptual-cue strategy the explainer's
structure relies on has empirical support, which I did not expect and am glad of.

## 2. A disagreement that turned out to be a non-disagreement

**researcher** nominated **Keith Jarrett, *The Köln Concert* (1975)** as the single gentlest
door into free jazz / avant-garde, reasoning that "the listener never has to abandon
orientation to enjoy it."

**The outside dossier** nominates *Shape of Jazz to Come* as the gentle door into **free
jazz**, and *Köln Concert* as the gentle door into the **avant-garde** wing.

These look like competing answers and are not. The dossier is answering a *finer* question —
which record for *which* branch — and the two agents independently converged on the same
record for the same branch. **Treat the convergence as meaningful and the conflict as
dissolved.**

**RESOLVED 2026-09-29 — see [`RESOLUTION-koln-concert.md`](RESOLUTION-koln-concert.md).**
The *Köln* recommendation is **dropped**. The one accessibility-screened beginner list
explicitly excludes it; both beginner starter kits point at *Shape of Jazz to Come* instead;
and the sources that do nominate the Köln are answering *how do I start with Keith Jarrett*,
which is a different question. *Shape of Jazz to Come* now carries the whole outside branch
with genuinely convergent provenance.

## 3. An open question the page must settle: what is Alice Coltrane's doorway record?

Three sources in this corpus describe *Journey in Satchidananda* differently, and they cannot
all be right.

| Source | Says it is |
|---|---|
| **The canon export** (`style: Modal Jazz`) | modal |
| **mccoy's advisory** | "the spiritual-jazz door" |
| **The outside dossier** | **the avant-garde/outside door, not free jazz** — citing an Apple Music editorial that the record "represents a watershed for her as she forged an identity apart from free jazz" |

The dossier's point is a good one and it cuts both ways usefully: **her actual free jazz album
is the inaccessible one.** So a page that recommends the record for free jazz may be pointing
at the wrong door.

This is a small factual question with a real structural consequence, and it is **mccoy's to
settle** — it touches the canon's own filing. Worth raising with him directly.

## 4. The best single finding for the fusion axis

From the outside dossier, via a Bay Honors analysis:

> **Chameleon** — the record almost everyone hears as the definitive free-jazz statement — is
> **a 32-bar A-A-B-A over two preplanned changes.**

It is *inside*. Not "sort of inside." Formally conventional, returning, over chords that
somebody wrote down in advance.

This is the strongest available ammunition for the page's central correction, because it
comes from the most-cited free jazz record and the evidence is structural rather than
critical. A reader who has been told free jazz means formlessness can be shown the opposite
in one sentence.

**The same dossier makes the parallel point about fusion from the other side:** *Bitches
Brew*'s structure is a recurring fanfare over a bass vamp — theme and returns, not
formlessness. So *Bitches Brew* is **both** a bad first fusion record **and** the record most
commonly misread as free jazz when it is not. One error, caught twice.

## 5. The mechanism, now empirically grounded

My hypothesis was that beginners fail at free jazz because it removes orientation cues they
had been relying on without noticing. **Both agents independently confirmed it**, and the
outside dossier found peer-reviewed support I did not expect:

- **Music Theory Online 20.3**, on the 2,400-annotation SALAMI dataset: avant-garde jazz
  inspired far more listener disagreement than song-form material, and the predictor is
  "differences in the commitments made at the outset of a piece regarding what constitutes a
  fundamental structural unit."
- **Corbett, *A Listener's Guide to Free Improvisation***, the sharpest statement of the
  failure mode: "absence of a steady beat can be a deal breaker for listeners raised on a
  diet of 4/4 backbeats." Notably, **Corbett declines to name a first record at all** — which
  is its own kind of honesty.
- **KNKX** (public-radio education): free jazz "narrows that already limited audience to a
  select few… for most, free jazz is a non-starter," and the decision takes "about ten
  seconds."

**And the answer to "does naming it help?" is a qualified yes** — worth having, because the
page needs to know whether explaining the mechanism is worth the space. Naming the removed
cues converts "is this even music?" into "what am I hearing instead?" That reframing
"genuinely moves some listeners. **It does not move most, but it moves some.**"

That is enough to justify the space, and it sets the right expectation for the reader.

**The framing that does the most work**, from `researcher`, unverified as a quotation but
sound as reasoning: **"hearing noise is an accurate perception, not an error."** It lets the
page be honest without being apologetic.

## 6. A gap I could not close, in two independent attempts

**Is there any real figure for the proportion of listeners who never get free jazz?**

- `researcher` offered "70–85% of adults." **Unsourced.** A guess in a percentage's clothing.
- The outside dossier went looking properly and found **nothing** — no study, no survey, no
  critic willing to put a number on it.

Both failures are informative, and the second is the more useful one: **there appears to be
no such figure in the literature.** The page should therefore argue qualitatively — "a large
fraction, and that is not a failure on your part" — and **must not print a percentage.** If a
number is ever wanted, it has to come from somewhere new.

## 8. Honest status of the entry-point work

- Three dossiers: two written, one in progress. Together roughly 100k characters.
- The **compilation-versus-original** question — the strongest practical finding in the whole
  exercise — belongs to the early-inside file and is not yet resolved. Preliminary finding:
  **Savoy's metal masters were lost**, so every bebop reissue descends from a 70-year-old
  disc-to-tape transfer, and Mosaic's definitive 10-CD bebop set deliberately **excludes
  Parker**. If that holds up it changes the bebop recommendation outright, and it is the
  single most practically useful thing this research has produced.
- `entry-points-inside-late.md` states candidly that **no education programme nominates a
  post-bop first record**, so that list rests on criticism rather than pedagogy. Worth
  carrying forward as a known weakness.
- The late-styles dossier's weakest pick is *Unity* (Larry Young), which it flags as
  effectively uncited. Treat that one as a suggestion, not a recommendation.
