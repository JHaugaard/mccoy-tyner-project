# The listener's guide: where to enter each genre

**Purpose.** John writes the page; this is the practical scaffolding underneath it — what to
put in front of a reader at each moment, and an honest account of what will happen.

**A note on tone.** Every entry below is written for someone who likes jazz well enough to be
curious but does not have the vocabulary yet. None of it assumes the reader is stupid, and
none of it pretends the hard entries are easy. Where something is genuinely hard, it says so.

**Gateway records** are taken from mccoy's advisory of 2026-09-28
(`../raw/consultation-mccoy-2026-09-28.txt`) and cross-checked against the live canon export
for actual presence. The "in canon" column is mechanically derived, not asserted.

---

## The one piece of advice that matters most

**Do not start with bebop.**

Bebop is where the history starts, and it is a genuinely bad place to start *listening*. Its
harmonic language was, at the time, a refusal to be background music; the melodies move fast
and the chords move under them. A reader who opens the page at 1945 and thinks "I don't
understand this" has learned something false about jazz and about their own ear.

The canon makes the same point structurally: **the collection's first two albums are 1950** —
*Birth of the Cool* and *Subconscious-Lee* — not 1945. The reaction came before the canon
began.

If the page needs one instruction for the whole reader, make it this: **these styles are
conversations that were happening at the same time, and you are allowed to walk into the
middle of one.**

## The inside line

### Bebop — 1945–48
**Enter here:** Charlie Parker, *The Charlie Parker Story* (1945, Savoy) — in canon.
*Bebop is hard because it is fast and harmonically dense, and because its beauty is
structural rather than emotional on first hearing.* The advice: listen for the rhythm
section's drive, and let the melody be a texture at first. Come back to it in a year.

### Cool jazz — 1949–56
**Enter here:** Miles Davis, *Birth of the Cool* (1950) — in canon.
*The gentlest door in the whole canon, and the one the collection itself chose to open with.*
Good first stop. Long unhurried lines, room to breathe. If the page wants a reader to feel
welcome on arrival, this is the record.

### Hard bop — 1955–65
**Enter here:** Art Blakey, *Moanin'* (1958, Blue Note) — in canon.
mccoy's verdict, worth taking seriously: **"if he converts no one, nothing will."** This is
the spine of the canon and the most direct, most physical music on the page. Blues-based,
blues-inflected, and it swings hardest. A reader who likes *Moanin'* will like most of the
collection.

### Post-bop — 1964–70
**Enter here:** Wayne Shorter, *Speak No Evil* (1964, Blue Note) — in canon.
*The gentler on-ramp if modal feels like a wall* — mccoy suggests *Maiden Voyage* (1965) as
the softer alternate. Post-bop is where the music stops being a vehicle for solos and
starts being composition.

### Modal — 1959 onward
**Enter here:** Miles Davis, *Kind of Blue* (1959) — in canon.
mccoy: "no contest." It is the most approachable important record in jazz, and the most
misfiled — it is not bebop and it is not cool jazz. That is the point worth making on the
page.

### Soul jazz — 1958–70
**Enter here:** Lee Morgan, *The Sidewinder* (1963, Blue Note) — in canon.
*Cannonball Adderley's* **Mercy, Mercy, Mercy! Live at "The Club"** *(1966)* is the
live-party alternate, and if the page has any space for a "this is what the music sounds
like in a room full of people," that is the record.*
**Steady, groove-driven, blues-and-gospel-inflected.** Soul jazz is the genre most at risk
of being dismissed as "lesser hard bop" — mccoy notes John defends it against exactly that
sneer. It is not lesser; it is a different job (the groove leads) rather than a lesser
version of the same one.

## The outside line

### Free jazz — 1960 onward
**Enter here:** Ornette Coleman, *The Shape of Jazz to Come* (1959) — in canon.
**The melodic door.** mccoy's reasoning: "inside melodic heads, outside solos — the door
opening." It is the right choice for a first free-jazz record precisely because it is so
tuneful. **Alice Coltrane, *Journey in Satchidananda*** (1970) is the spiritual-jazz door,
and is the most accessible route into the territory.
*A small irony worth using:* the canon files *The Shape of Jazz to Come* as **Post-Bop**,
not free jazz, while filing *Ascension* as Free Jazz. Both defensible; together they are a
neat demonstration that the labels do not settle the question. **Alice Coltrane's record is
filed Modal Jazz** — which is mccoy's own "modal continuity is what carries spiritual jazz"
point, visible in the data.
**What will put a newcomer off:** the leap from "I can follow the tune" to "there is no tune
I can follow." That is a real wall and the page should say so rather than pretend otherwise.

### Avant-garde jazz
**Enter here, if at all, via the composed wing** — AACM lineage. The Art Ensemble of Chicago
is mccoy's note as the most *legible* wing for a listener coming from the composed
tradition, and therefore disproportionately valuable.

**Important definitional point the page should make explicitly:** "avant-garde" is **not** a
synonym for free jazz. In the house taxonomy, avant-garde is the AACM compositional wing;
free jazz is the Coleman/Trane/Ayler line. Popular writing merges them constantly, and the
merge is a real historical error — Gridley's point that the era's categories "led some
outsiders to assume … that the different musicians of this period had more in common than
they actually did."

### Fusion — 1968 onward
**Enter here:** Miles Davis, *Bitches Brew* (1970) as the landmark; **Herbie Hancock,
*Head Hunters* (1973)** as the groove-first listener's route — both in canon.
**But the page must open this section by saying fusion is a different axis, not a louder
version of the same one.** Fusion is electric and groove-driven but overwhelmingly
*harmonically inside* — vamps, riffs, ostinatos. It does not belong in a bucket with free
jazz, and putting it there teaches the wrong lesson. mccoy's proposed frame: "The Electric
Turn."

## The hinges — the records that make the whole page click

These are the highest-value listening suggestions in this file, because they turn an
abstraction into an experience the reader can have. All in canon.

| Pair / record | What it demonstrates |
|---|---|
| **Coltrane, *A Love Supreme* (Dec 1964) → *Ascension* (June 1965)** | Same leader, six months apart: the most inside-modal record in the canon, then a full outside statement. "The line is a door, not a wall." |
| **Dolphy, *Out to Lunch!* (1964)** | Sounds free, is tightly composed. Proves free-sounding ≠ free. |
| **McLean, *Destination Out* (1963)** | A hard bopper stepping out; the title is the concept. |
| **Hill, *Point of Departure* (1964)** | Dolphy inside Hill's structures; again, the title tells the story. |
| **Rollins, *East Broadway Run Down* (1966)** | The most inside tenor giant of his generation, with Cherry, Haden and Blackwell. |
| **Coleman, *The Shape of Jazz to Come* (1959)** | Inside heads, outside solos. The door opening. |
| **Mingus, *Pithecanthropus Erectus* (1956)** | Inside tradition, avant-garde gestures — years before the labels existed. |
| **Davis, *Bitches Brew* (1970)** | Outside texture over inside vamps. The fusion hinge precisely because it fools people. |
| **Old and New Dreams, *Playing* (1980, ECM)** | Filed Free Jazz; actually a Miles sidemen band. Our own categories are a convenience, not a fact. |

## What a reader is actually being asked to accept

Worth a short section on the page, in plain words:

- That the genres overlap, always have, and were named afterwards by critics rather than
  declared by musicians.
- That "outside" is a **relative** term. Levine's formulation, sourced in
  `../06-inside-outside-thesis/thesis-and-page-architecture.md` §3: what one listener hears
  as outside, another hears as inside — and **Charlie Parker was considered "out" by many
  musicians in the 1940s.** The inside tradition was once the radical one.
- That "outside" does **not** mean marginal. *The Shape of Jazz to Come* was famous on
  arrival; plenty of inside records were obscure. **[mccoy]**
- That fusion is not free jazz, and avant-garde is not free jazz.
- That ECM is a record label, not a genre — a "sound" that is largely a marketing category.
  Say it gently; it is a house rule but readers will not know that.
