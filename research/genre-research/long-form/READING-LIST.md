# A reading list for the long-form work

**For John. 2026-09-29.**

This is **not a bibliography.** It is a path — an order, with reasons, and honest notes about
what will be hard. Nothing here is required. You do not need to read all of it, and you
should not read any of it to sound like something you are not.

**Verification note.** Items marked **[verified]** were confirmed by search this session; the
free resources all were, and I have linked them. Items marked **[recalled]** are from my own
knowledge and unconfirmed — treat their titles and attributions as things to check before you
cite them. `raw/reading-list-verification.json` holds the search results.

---

## The short version

If you read nothing else, read these three, in this order:

1. **Ted Gioia, *How to Listen to Jazz*** — because you are writing for beginners and this is
   the book about how beginners actually hear. Full text is **already in `raw/`**
   (`gioia-howtolisten.txt`, 384KB). You can start tonight.
2. **Mark Gridley, *Jazz Styles*, any edition** — the reference book you will keep open
   forever. Every genre definition in this project traces back to it, and it is genuinely
   readable rather than a doorstop.
3. **Scott DeVeaux & Gary Giddins, *Jazz*** — the standard textbook. Full text **in `raw/`**
   (`dg-jazz-woxx8.txt`). This is the one that will give you vocabulary you can actually
   defend, because you will have read the argument behind each term.

## Tier 1 — the on-ramp

### Ted Gioia, *How to Listen to Jazz* **[recalled]**
The book for your specific job. Gioia's whole project is bridging the gap between "I like
this" and "I can hear what's happening," and he does it without condescension — which is the
register your explainer needs. **Already in `raw/`.** Start here.

### Mark Gridley, *Jazz Styles* **[recalled]**
The reference. Gridley is where "out music" and the loose-category caution come from, and he
is the source for the observation that Armstrong, Parker and Tristano could all once have been
called avant-garde. Use it as a lookup, not a read-through. Older editions are fine and cheap.

### Scott DeVeaux & Gary Giddins, *Jazz*** **[verified in `raw/`]**
The standard undergraduate text, and unusually well written. The chapter on the 1940s–50s
criticism is the best short treatment of the bebop/cool split as a *political and economic*
story rather than a purely musical one — directly relevant to the long-form's first research
front.

### John Szwed, *Jazz 101*** **[verified]**
Gioia's near-twin. Useful mainly as a second opinion; if you like one you'll tolerate the
other. Szwed is a little more historical, Gioia a little more about listening.

## Tier 2 — the free and permanent tools

These are better than most books for what you actually need, and they cost nothing.

### Library of Congress — National Recording Registry **[verified]**
<https://www.loc.gov/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/recording-registry/descriptions-and-essays/>

Free, well-written essays on individual landmark recordings, written for a general reader. This
is the closest thing to "a jazz book that is also free" and several are excellent. Two already
in `raw/` informed this project: the essay on *Saxophone Colossus* (which supplied the citable
"straddles the divide from classical bebop to hard bop" line) and the *Köln Concert* essay.

**Why this matters for the anti-poser problem:** these are short, attributable, and written in
plain English. If you base a paragraph on a LoC essay, you can say exactly where it came from
and nobody will think you are straining.

### Jazz Discography Project (jazzdisco) **[verified]**
<https://www.jazzdisco.org/> — Dave Gregson's session-by-session discographies.

The primary record for "who was actually in the room, when, on what date." This is what
settled the bebop personnel disputes in this project. Use it to *check*, never to narrate.

### Discography of American Historical Recordings (DAHR) **[verified]**
<https://adp.library.ucsb.edu/> — the pre-1925 US commercial recordings, with dates and
label numbers.

### Herbie Hancock Institute of Jazz — Jazz in America **[verified]**
<https://hancockinstitute.org/curriculum/> — free classroom curriculum.

Genuinely useful here for a specific reason: **it is teaching material written for
non-musicians**, and the two entry-point dossiers leaned on it. Its Grade 7–9 hard bop unit
is the best single plain-language account of hard bop as a reaction to cool. Captures already
in `raw/` (`hancock-pdf.md`, `hancock-jiam-gr7-9-freejazz-fusion.md`).

### Open Music Theory / Second Texts **[verified]**
<https://open.library.okstate.edu/sandbox/part/jazz-2/> and the Open Textbook Library entry.

Free, peer-reviewed, and readable if you want the actual theory behind modal harmony and
bebop scales. **Read this only when you need it** — see the lane note at the end.

## Tier 3 — for the two hard problems

### Free jazz: the problem you named

**John Corbett, *A Listener's Guide to Free Improvisation*** (University of Chicago Press,
2016) **[verified]** — <https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/L/bo22955331.html>

This is the most important book on this list for you specifically. It is written by someone
who understands both the music and the listener's problem, and it does something unusual: it
**declines to hand you a first record.** One of the subagents flagged that as its own kind of
honesty, and it is. It will also give you the sharpest sentence anyone has written about why
beginners fail — that the absence of a steady beat can be a dealbreaker for someone raised on
4/4 backbeats.

**George E. Lewis, *A Power Stronger Than Itself: The AACM and American Experimental Music*** **[verified]** — <https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/P/bo5504497.html>

The scholarly book on the composed avant-garde wing. This is the volume that will let you
separate "avant-garde" from "free jazz" with actual authority rather than assertion — which is
one of your three open decisions.

**Ingrid Monson, *Saying Something: Jazz Improvisation and Interaction*** (Oxford, 1996) **[recalled]**

The book that changed how free improvisation is understood — it is about musicians *listening to
each other*, not about notes. Useful if you want to describe what free jazz players are
actually doing rather than that it is chaotic.

### The politics of the categories

This is the long-form's first research front and the biggest gap in what we have.

**Eric Porter, *What Is This Thing Called Jazz?*** **[verified]** — <https://www.ucpress.edu/books/what-is-this-thing-called-jazz/paper>

The best single book on how jazz got *made* — the musicians, the labels, the money, the
racial politics, and who got called avant-garde versus sellout. Directly on the question you
most need to answer and least have material for.

**Keith Waters, *Postbop Jazz in the Sixties*** (Oxford, 2019) **[recalled]**

The scholarly treatment of the term whose contestedness is one of your open questions. You do
not need to agree with it. Read the back half of
`long-form/genres/post-bop.md` first so you know what question to ask it.

**Scott DeVeaux, "The Birth of Bebop"** **[verified in `raw/`]**
`raw/deveaux-birthofbebop2.txt` (411KB). The definitive account of how bebop actually
happened — which is *not* the story of Parker inventing it alone. Good corrective material.

### The one essay to start with

**Justin Duhaime, "Eric Dolphy & Outness"** (Carleton University, 2011) **[verified]**

<https://justinduhaime.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Eric-Dolphy-Outness-Final-revised.pdf>
— also in `raw/`, and the single most valuable source in this entire project.

Free, about 20 pages, and it is the documentary foundation for the name you stumbled on with
mccoy. If you read one thing from this list besides Gioia, read this. It will tell you exactly
how strong and how shaky your page title is, and it contains the Dolphy quote that undercuts
the easy version of the argument.

## What I would skip, and why

- **Grove/Oxford Music Online articles.** Excellent, authoritative, and written to be
  impenetrable on first contact. Fine as a last resort; wrong as an on-ramp.
- **Academic journal articles before the books.** Same problem, worse.
- **Anything that promises to give you "the definitive list of jazz genres."** There isn't
  one, and the promise is a tell.

## How to read so you can say what you actually know

You said you want to work in a serious, measured, defensible manner, and that you don't want
to come across as a poser. Here is the practical part, and it is mostly about attribution
rather than about knowledge.

**Three registers. Keep them visibly separate.**

| Register | How it sounds | What it costs you |
|---|---|---|
| *I read this* | "the Library of Congress essay on *Saxophone Colossus* argues that…" | Nothing. It is a strength, not a weakness. |
| *A critic said* | "Sargeant wrote in *The New Yorker* that it didn't sound like jazz to him at all." | Nothing, and it is often more interesting than the record. |
| *I think* | "which is why this record is the better door for a beginner — though the reception was mixed." | Nothing. **This is the register that is actually yours, and it is the one people trust.** |

The failure mode is not saying anything wrong. It is **fluency without a register** — a
sentence that could have come from a textbook, with nothing marking it as yours.

**Three habits that will do most of the work:**

1. **Prefer one source you read over three you skimmed.** You can honestly say "Gioia's
   argument is that…" and that is worth more than three unattributed gestures at a consensus.
2. **Name the disagreement rather than smoothing it.** This project has a working example:
   the *dust jacket* hypothesis about "post-bop" turns out to be something critics
   *conceded the premise of*, not something anyone actually argued. That is a genuinely
   interesting, genuinely defensible sentence — and it is only available to you because the
   uncertainty was tracked rather than hidden. An enthusiast who says "I've seen this
   repeated but I couldn't find anyone actually arguing it" is *more* credible than a scholar
   citing it carelessly.
3. **Don't use a term you can't explain.** Not a style rule — a safety rule. It is very easy
   to write "modal vamps" and it is very easy for a reader to notice you don't know what they
   are. The explainer's own `language.md` exists partly to keep you out of that trap.

**What I will do, and what I won't.** I will keep flagging when a claim in the corpus
outruns its sources, and I have done so repeatedly — the "dust jacket," the "Chamber of Jazz"
scare, the two unsourced Miles/Monk quotes, the Köln Concert recommendation. That is the
service. What I will not do is write in a voice designed to sound like yours, or hand you
sentences that borrow authority the research doesn't have. If a paragraph of yours is going
to be strong on the page, it should be strong because you checked it, and the corpus exists so
that you can check it.

## A suggested first fortnight

Not a schedule — permission to stop.

- **One evening:** Gioia's introduction, then put on *Moanin'* and *Kind of Blue* back to
  back and notice whether the two really do feel like different jobs to the ear.
- **One evening:** Dehaime's essay, with the terminology file open alongside
  (`long-form/thesis/inside-outside-terminology.md`).
- **One sitting, deliberately:** read your own explainer outline
  (`explainer/structure.md`) and mark every sentence you could not defend from something you
  have read. That list is the actual to-do.
