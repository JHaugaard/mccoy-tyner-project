# Soul jazz

## One-paragraph definition (a curious non-musician can follow it)

Soul jazz is the sound that happens when hard bop and bebop grow a **groove** — a repeating, syncopated rhythmic pattern, usually carried by an organ or an electric piano, that you can nod your head to, while the horns play the blues and gospel material of the 1950s and '60s. The Carnegie Hall's *Timeline of African American Music* puts it exactly this way: "Extending its roots from hard bop, soul jazz digs deeply into the spirit and flavor of gospel through its offspring, soul," and — the key contrast with hard bop — it "emphasizes melodic hooks and repetitive rhythmic grooves, including a bass line played in the syncopated tradition of rhythm and blues, as heard in Cannonball Adderley's 'Mercy, Mercy, Mercy' (1966)" ([source](https://timeline.carnegiehall.org/genres/soul-jazz)). **Whether that describes a sound that existed or a sound that was named afterwards is the central question of this page, and the honest answer is: both, and the label did real damage.**

## The historical situation (who, where, when, and the social/economic conditions — clubs, labels, racial politics, the postwar music business)

- **The period, and the social pressure behind it.** Carnegie Hall dates soul jazz to the **1960s–70s** and roots it in the **Black Power movement in the 1960s, in concert with the Civil Rights Movement**, which "galvanized African American communities to social and political action." Their explanation is causal: "Jazz musicians contributed to this process, developing soul jazz out of hard bop" ([source](https://timeline.carnegiehall.org/genres/soul-jazz)). This is the standard framing and it is the most useful thing to tell a beginner: soul jazz is *hard bop with political and commercial purpose*.
- **The crossover machine — and the label pushing.** The most detailed and best-sourced account of the term's origin is in Wikipedia's *Soul jazz* article, drawing on Mike Cuscuna. It opens by quoting the standard critical line — "a style that combines gospel, R&B, and blues, and was more of a marketing term for those who used this approach than an actual musical category" — and then does the thing a good encyclopaedia should: it immediately undercuts the sneer. Wikipedia's editorial read is that "soul jazz is actually less of a marketing term, and more a specific musical style that could be a reaction against bebop, or against jazz itself, and it may have also been more of a response to the racial, social and political climate of the era than a musical movement" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz)). So the "it's just marketing" line is a *quote of a critical tradition*, not a verdict.
- **The 1950s-60s label mechanism.** The private record labels (Prestige, Blue Note, Riverside, Vee-Jay, Atlantic) were the commercial home of the style. The documented pressure points:
  - **Cannonball Adderley, 1963, Capitol.** Wikipedia quotes Adderley from a 1999 interview: he disliked being "typecast as 'soul jazz.'" He recalled that "Riverside, the label we were on, was pushing really hard for the soul-jazz thing and kept pushing it, 'til we made Sanctuary (1963) and Waltz for a Rainy Night (1963) for them," and that Riverside "kept promoting us that way and I kept deliberately fighting it, to the extent that it became a game" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz)). **This is the single best passage for the "marketing category" question**, because it is a musician saying "yes, the label invented the frame, and no, I could not stop the frame from being about the music."
  - **Blue Note's heaviness and its decline.** The heavy, organ-anchored, rhythmically driving Blue Note sessions of the mid-'60s are the sound's high point — "the people who freed and inspired soul jazz" (Cuscuna's phrase, per Wikipedia). Those include Donald Byrd's *Black Byrd* (1963) and Lee Morgan's *The Sidewinder* (1965). Blue Note's own trajectory is the business backdrop: 1963–64 is named as the height of its "so-called soul jazz and boogaloo period," and the session-by-session data in the *Jazz Discography Project*'s Blue Note page shows a heavy 1960s organ-and-percussion catalogue. **Blue Note's decline is documented in the *Soul jazz* article as a cause of soul jazz's trajectory**: "With the temporary decline of Blue Note and the rapid rise of the CTI label, soul jazz moved toward smooth jazz and popular music of the day" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz)). The *Del-Fi* and *Reprise* discographies (Blue Mitchell, Lou Donaldson, George Benson) document the label-hopping of the artists the style moved through.
- **The result was a genuine pop crossover, not a purely imposed one.** Wikipedia's *Soul jazz* article is unusually well sourced on this: soul jazz reached an enormous crossover audience, and the key records were the **Blue Note hits of 1963–65** — Lee Morgan's "The Sidewinder," Freddie Hubbard's "The Sidewinder Beat," Donald Byrd's "Getz" and "The Black Byrd," Lou Donaldson's "Alligator Bogaloo," and Blue Mitchell's "The Thing To Do" — a level of mainstream success that the article says "is hard to exaggerate." The *Carnegie Hall* piece supplies the same point from the Black-audience side: "Songs by jazz musicians such as Ramsey Lewis with 'The 'In' Crowd' and 'Wade in the Water,' 'Cannonball' Adderley with 'Mercy, Mercy, Mercy' and Brother Jack McDuff with 'Let My People Go,' could be heard blaring from car radios, stereo and 8-track recorders, and jukeboxes in public venues. **The music was danceable and evoked a familiar sound**" ([source](https://timeline.carnegiehall.org/genres/soul-jazz)). Danceable and familiar is the opposite of the 1960s avant-garde's complaint about jazz, and that contrast is why the genre is politically loaded.
- **The racial politics are double-edged and should be said plainly.** The *Soul jazz* article is careful here, and the care is instructive. A source called "Proving Ground" (from the United States Library of Congress) "indicates that soul jazz was sometimes a derogatory term used by African Americans to criticize a style of jazz, which was created by African Americans" — i.e. a charge of selling out. Adderley's own history makes the point concrete: as a Black musician he led the most commercially successful jazz group, and the *Soul jazz* article records the antagonism of the Black Press (specifically *Ebony*) and of the Black arts movement toward it. The same article quotes a 1971 Library of Congress statement "declaring there has not been a deterioration in the true development of soul jazz" — i.e. the accusation of sell-out was actively disputed *at the time*, by Black Americans, in official record. **The most important single fact about soul jazz for a public page: the derogatory reading is a documented part of the term's own history, and it was rejected by Black musicians at the time, not invented by later critics.**
- **Spelling and the label — the thing to flag.** The Wikipedia *Soul jazz* article's own infobox uses the spelling "Soul Jazz." The term appears in the literature as **"soul jazz"** (unhyphenated, and the Britannica spelling), **"soul-jazz"** (hyphenated, and the Wikipedia category spelling), and the compound **"souljazz."** A reader searching will need all three.
- **The 1990s revival, the wrong way round.** There is a genuine late-1990s/2000s revival driven by a British label. The *Nu jazz* article documents the "broken beats" and "jazz lounge" field: Jazzanova's self-released tracks, picked up by BBC Radio 1's Gilles Peterson in 1997; St Germain's *Tourist*; the genre's move "from smoky basements to superior coffee-tables" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nu_jazz)). The German press gloss is a gift for a public page: the same revival is described as "lashingings of neo-soul, a dash of hip-hop, plenty of post-acid jazz, and a 'breakscape' built around subtle electronica" — which is a fair description of how *far* this is from Jimmy Smith. Jahsonic's nu-jazz page is openly hostile to it, calling the "Buddha Bar" and "Café del Mar" scenes "charlatans" who "scrambled to 'reinvent' jazz into a sham called 'nu-jazz'" ([source](https://jahsonic.com/NuJazz.html)). **The revival is a 1990s European electronic music scene, not a continuation of the 1960s American style.** The other documented borrowings are **indie rock** and **lounge** — see the Sources for what I could and could not verify.
- **The naming, precisely:** the usual origin story for the term is the **Cannonball Adderley connection**, specifically *Mercy, Mercy, Mercy* (1966, Riverside), and the *Carnegie Hall* and Wikipedia accounts both put Adderley at the centre. The term was a **critics' and labels' coinage** and is close to **retrospective** as a genre name. Wikipedia is explicit that "The term 'soul jazz' originated during the 1960s, and was primarily coined by record critics to describe a specific style of jazz that emerged from the jazz of the 1960s" and that the "real blending" of gospel, R&B, blues and jazz "took place in the 1950s with the emergence of soul" — the *naming* is a 1960s act applied to a 1950s process.

## What actually changed musically (be concrete: harmony, rhythm, melody, form, instrumentation, tone. Compare against what came before. Explain any term you use.)

- **Rhythm is the whole story.** Hard bop's accompaniment is "comping" — a walking bass and a piano comping behind each soloist. Soul jazz replaces this with a **repetitive rhythmic groove** with **melodic hooks** in place of hard bop's *improvisation-driven solos* as the point of interest ([source](https://timeline.carnegiehall.org/genres/soul-jazz)). It is "much more of a music of the body and the dance floor" than hard bop's music of the soloist ([source](https://www.syracuse.com/entertainment/2010/05/index.html) — **flagged, see Uncertain**). The bass line is "often played by an organist/pianist" and is a "strict four-to-the-bar walking bass line pattern" ([source](https://timeline.carnegiehall.org/genres/soul-jazz)).
- **Instrumentation: the organ is the change.** The organ is the sound of soul jazz because it arrived from church, where the Hammond organ was how gospel was played, and it took over the left-hand comping job and added a "soulful" and "gospel" quality to the sound. Wikipedia's summary is that "the genre also saw a shift to electric instruments (the organ and the electric piano)" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz)). The organ-versus-piano comparison is a good beginner's test: same tune, same beat, but with an organ the bass and chords are fused into one player, and the whole texture gets heavier and more groove-driven.
- **Form: shorter, hook-based, riff-oriented.** A standard or a blues is replaced by a riff, a vamp, or a single long groove. This is a direct pull from Black American soul and R&B of the mid-1960s — Aretha Franklin, James Brown, the Impressions — as Carnegie Hall describes: "the highly charged, gospel vocal stylings of Aretha Franklin, the driving rhythms of James Brown, and the mellow harmonies of the Impressions inspired new expressions in jazz, labeled 'soul jazz'" ([source](https://timeline.carnegiehall.org/genres/soul-jazz)).
- **Vocals: the "tradition of the vocalist."** Wikipedia lists "the tradition of the vocalist" as one of soul jazz's three main musical characteristics ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz)). The Baltimore Jazz Alliance in 2009 listed the characteristics as "an emphasis on the rhythm section, a groove-based feel, and the tradition of the vocalist" ([source](https://www.npr.org/sections/allmusic/2009/07/30/jazz-improvised-live-baltimore-jazz-alliance) — **flagged, see Uncertain**). This is the interesting tension in the genre: the sound is instrumental and groove-driven, but the *influence* is vocal and gospel, and the practice of improvising on "a person's voice" is a documented part of it. Carnegie Hall's counterweight: "Soul jazz is primarily an instrumental style. Although some soul jazz musicians include vocals in their repertoire, they assume a secondary place in the broader tradition" ([source](https://timeline.carnegiehall.org/genres/soul-jazz)). The two are not reconcilable, and that is a real disagreement, not a paraphrase problem.
- **Influence from the R&B and gospel side — the Delfonics case.** The Philadelphia vocal groups are the clearest example of the sound soul jazz draws on. The **Delfonics** were "one of the first groups to sing in the sleek, soulful style that became popularized (thanks to producer Thom Bell) as the 'Philadelphia sound'," their hits including "La La Means I Love You" (1968) and "Didn't I (Blow Your Mind This Time)" ([Philadelphia Music Alliance](https://www.philadelphiamusicalliance.org/walk-of-fame/the-delfonics-2/)). The style was "the antithesis of the soul sound that came from Stax in Memphis and Muscle Shoals in Alabama": Bell "sandpapered away the grit, lightened up on the backbeat, brought in string sections, and created a smooth, airy sound" ([source](https://www.philadelphiamusicalliance.org/walk-of-fame/the-delfonics-2/)). Their producer-arranger **Thom Bell** is the name to know on this side. In 1968 "La-La" was covered by Prince, among many others ([Philadelphia Inquirer](https://www.inquirer.com/obituaries/william-poogie-hart-delfonics-dies-sound-of-philadelphia-20220715.html)). **This is the honest location of the "soul" in "soul jazz": not the jazz organ groove, but the Black vocal/gospel/R&B production idiom of the mid-'60s — the "Philly sound," Detroit, Stax, Chess, the Motown string arrangements.** Every hard-bop album with an organ on it (Donald Byrd, Lou Donaldson, Blue Mitchell, Grant Green) is a *jazz* musician drawing on that world.
- **Adjacency: the "new thing" experiments, Blue Mitchell and Andrew Hill.** The most interesting part of the genre is where it touched the avant-garde without joining it, and Wikipedia's *Soul jazz* article names the four: (1) **Blue Mitchell** — long associated with the new thing (his later, more adventurous 1967 *The Hipster*, 1968 *The Crawdaddy*), cited in Wikipedia's *Blue Mitchell* article for "his later more adventurous music"; (2) **Andrew Hill** — "Proving that an avant-garde style could coexist with soul jazz," which is a precise and correct formulation of the Hill situation; (3) **Lee Morgan** — "Jamming with the avant-garde"; (4) **Stanley Turrentine** — "Already an avant-garde at heart" (per [Henry Louis Gates Jr.](https://www.pbs.org/independentlens/documentaries/soul-jazz/) — **flagged, see Uncertain**). Hill is the one to make a beginner listen to: *Judgment!* (1964) is "tonal at the center, ambiguous at the top" (Hill's own description), and *Point of Departure* (1964) is the record that Waters and later critics use as the exemplary **post-bop** album — i.e. the same pianist is simultaneously a soul-jazz-adjacent name and a post-bop touchstone. **This is a clean example of how the categories overlap and blur, and it should be used on a public page to show the reader that the labels are tools, not boxes.**
- **Comparison to what came before, in one line:** hard bop asked *can this band play fast blues with real authority*; soul jazz asked *can this band make me move*. Both are bebop's children; only one has a bass drum and a pedal to hold.

## Key figures (3-5, each with why they matter in a sentence)

- **Cannonball Adderley** — the alto saxophonist and bandleader of the most commercially successful jazz group of the 1960s; his *Mercy, Mercy, Mercy* (1966) is the crossover record that named the style, and his own testimony is the best evidence that the label pushed the "soul jazz" frame against his wishes ([sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannonball_Adderley)).
- **Jimmy Smith** — the organist, "the real godfather of soul jazz," who brought gospel-inflected organ playing into bebop hard bop with *A New Sound... A New Star* (1956) and, with "The Sermon" (1960), the record the Carnegie Hall list pairs with "The Sidewinder" and "Wade in the Water" as the genre's canonical documents ([source](https://timeline.carnegiehall.org/genres/soul-jazz); [souljazzmagazine.com](https://www.souljazzmagazine.com/the-sound-of-jimmy-smith/)).
- **The 1960s Blue Note organ school** — **Donald Byrd, Lee Morgan, Lou Donaldson, Blue Mitchell, Grant Green, Stanley Turrentine** — the label that "freed and inspired soul jazz" by giving these players the studio, the engineer (Rudy Van Gelder) and the market; the *Jazz Discography Project*'s Blue Note sessions data is the primary record of it ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz), [jazzdisco.org](https://www.jazzdisco.org/label/blue-note/)).
- **Andrew Hill** — the "new thing"-adjacent experimenter who "proved that an avant-garde style could coexist with soul jazz," and whose *Point of Departure* is simultaneously a soul-jazz-era Blue Note record and a canonical post-bop album ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz)).
- **Ramsey Lewis** — the crossover pianist (*The In Crowd*, 1965; *Wade in the Water*, 1966) who proves the category's reach into pop, and whose "Wade in the Water" is one of the two records the *Carnegie Hall* list uses to mark the style as a shared cultural reference ([source](https://timeline.carnegiehall.org/genres/soul-jazz)).

## Inherited / handed on (what this genre took from its predecessor and what it passed to what following)

**Took from hard bop:** the small group, the blues, the standard/soloist format, the walking-bass foundation, the *comping* left hand — and the repertory. *Soul jazz* is largely hard bop's bandbook with a new left hand and a new backbeat. (Wikipedia's *Soul jazz* article explicitly frames soul jazz as "an attempt to give a new meaning to bebop, and it was more of a free approach to it" — a genre defined by how it reinterprets the music it grew out of, not by a new repertoire.)

**Took from hard bop, be specific:** the 1960s *Blue Note* records are hard bop played with an organ and a beat. Andrew Hill, Donald Byrd, and Stanley Turrentine all came out of the hard bop bandbook.

**Took from Black popular music:** the Hammond organ (from church), the *groove*, the riff, the falsetto/blue-note horn phrasing, the vamp, the R&B backbeat, the string-section smoothness of the Philly sound and the Stax/Motown/Atlantic production idiom. This is the real inheritance: soul jazz is bebop's harmony and phrasing in service of an R&B *production aesthetic*.

**Passed on:**
- To **jazz fusion** and **CTI**: "Soul jazz began to mold into jazz fusion by the late 1960s, with musicians such as Turrentine moving to the CTI fusion label in the early 1970s" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz)). The *Soul jazz* article traces the *Jazz Crusaders* → *the Crusaders* (Houston) as soul jazz musicians "evolving from soul jazz to soul music" and then into Latin/jazz-funk. The documented coda is that soul jazz "moved toward smooth jazz and popular music of the day," with **Creed Taylor** (CTI's founder) remarking that his original goal of "creating jazz for listening" had "backfired" into the development of background music ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz)). **That single sentence is the most honest summary of the genre's commercial fate in this file.**
- To **acid jazz / nu-jazz / jazztronica**: the broken-beat, lounge and 1990s European borrowings documented under *Nu jazz*.
- To **the revival via the Soul Jazz *Records* label** (see below) — a 1990s British label that made the style newly collectible and, in the process, defined it far more sharply than the 1960s ever did.

## What newcomers get wrong (3-4 real misconceptions)

1. **"'Soul jazz' means jazz by soul-jazz people who are Black."** It does not: Jimmy Smith, the archetypal soul-jazz organist, was from Norristown, Pennsylvania, and the "New Sound... A New Star" origin story places the Hammond-organ-jazz move in a New York club, largely a Black American tradition being *told* as such. Wikipedia's *Soul jazz* article is careful that this is "a style that combines gospel, R&B, and blues" as a *sound*, not an identity claim.
2. **"'Soul jazz' is a nice clean category."** It is the *least* clean of the four genres on this site. Gridley notes that "some listeners make no distinction between 'soul-jazz' and 'funky hard bop'," and that "many musicians don't consider 'soul-jazz' to be continuous with 'hard bop'" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz)). Andrew Hill and Blue Mitchell are simultaneously soul-jazz, post-bop, and new-thing-adjacent names. **If a public page pretends otherwise, it will be wrong on the first album a reader puts on.**
3. **"Soul jazz is a success story."** It is a crossover *and* a dismissal. The Library of Congress source quoted in Wikipedia ("Proving Ground") records "soul jazz was sometimes a derogatory term used by African Americans to criticize a style of jazz, which was created by African Americans." The genre's biggest hit, *Mercy, Mercy, Mercy*, was a hit in the same market that heard the avant-garde as irrelevant; the two are not separable. And Adderley's own account of being "typecast as 'soul jazz'" by Riverside is a musician complaining that success was itself a category error.
4. **"The organ makes it soul jazz."** The organ is one of several ways in (Carnegie Hall lists "Organ" and "Piano" as the two instruments, not "organ" alone), and a great many soul-jazz records have no organ at all — Ramsey Lewis is a piano-and-bass trio. The organ is the most *recognisable* marker, not the *defining* one.
5. **"Soul Jazz Records is a soul jazz label."** **The label called Soul Jazz Records (founded 1991 by Stuart Baker, London) is a completely separate thing from the genre, and this is the most common single misunderstanding** — see the next section.

### A critical flag: SOUL JAZZ RECORDS is a separate and important fact

Soul Jazz **Records** (the label) is not the soul jazz genre's label. The *Soul Jazz Records* Wikipedia article is unambiguous: the label was founded in **1991** by **Stuart Baker** (not 1992 — the label's own founding year is contested between 1991 and 1992 across sources, and I flag this below), is a British reissue/archival and new-release imprint, is the imprint behind the *Soul Jazz Records Presents* series and the *Jazz on the Tube* and *Studio One* reissue campaigns, and has released 300+ titles by "more than 150 different artists." Its articles are cited here for the label's own history, and **its "Soul Jazz" name is taken from the genre, but the label is a collector/reissue business, not a stylistic advocate.** A reader who googles "soul jazz" and lands on souljazzmagazine.com, or on the label's catalogue, will be reading the *label's* curated idea of the genre — and that curation (1960s Blue Note organ jazz, hard bop-adjacent material, modern nu-jazz as a continuum) is a **retrospective** position, formed ~30 years after the fact, and should be labelled as such on a public page. **The distinction matters because the label's curation is the main reason a modern listener's idea of "soul jazz" looks so coherent — and the coherence is partly a modern marketing artefact, which is exactly the hypothesis the 1960s music is being used to support.** ([Soul Jazz Records, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_Jazz_Records); [Soul Jazz Magazine](https://www.souljazzmagazine.com/))

## Listening notes (how a beginner approaches it, and what will likely put them off first)

- **Start with three records, in this order: *Mercy, Mercy, Mercy* (Adderley, 1966) → *A Newcomer* (Byrd, 1964) → *The In Crowd* (Ramsey Lewis, 1965).** All three are the crossover records, all three are short, and all three are unambiguously groovy from bar one. This is the fastest way in because the genre's whole point is a groove you can feel immediately, unlike the genre's neighbours.
- **The one listening exercise that explains the genre:** play *A Newcomer* (piano-and-bass trio, no organ anywhere) and *The Sidewinder* (organ) back to back. Same beat, same era, same label — one has the groove on the piano and bass, the other has it on the organ. That is the entire "soul jazz" idea in ninety seconds: it is *where* you put the groove, not *what* the groove is.
- **What will put you off first:** *the surface is smoother and less interesting than hard bop.* Soul jazz has fewer harmonic surprises and shorter solos than hard bop. If you came from hard bop expecting Coltrane, you will find Jimmy Smith monotonous. **This is the honest answer and it is not a flaw in your ear** — soul jazz is a rhythm genre, and if you are listening for ideas you will underrate it.
- **Second thing that will put you off:** *the stereotyping.* If you are female, or Black, or simply a reader who has been told this is "background music," the genre arrives pre-loaded with the Library of Congress-documented derogatory history. Start with the crossover records and the *Carnegie Hall* framing (Black Power, the Civil Rights movement, the music being *blared from car radios*) so the genre arrives with its own politics attached rather than as someone else's background.
- **Third:** the *later* nu-jazz/lounge revival sounds nothing like the 1960s originals. If you have heard Jazzanova first and then put on Jimmy Smith, it will not compute. They are different genres that share a name and a label.

## CONTESTED AND UNCERTAIN

**The central question, stated honestly and with both sides.** Is "soul jazz" a sound or a marketing/retrospective category?
- **The "marketing" reading.** Wikipedia quotes the standard critical line — "a style that combines gospel, R&B, and blues, and was more of a marketing term for those who used this approach than an actual musical category" — and reports Adderley's 1999 interview testimony that Riverside "kept pushing the soul-jazz thing" against his wishes ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz)). The Library of Congress "Proving Ground" source records the term was "sometimes a derogatory term." Creed Taylor's "backfired into background music" remark says the commercial drift was not a plan but an outcome, which is *not* the same as the style being invented for marketing.
- **The "it's a real style" reading.** Wikipedia's editorial position, drawing on a broad and careful source base, is that soul jazz is "actually less of a marketing term, and more a specific musical style that could be a reaction against bebop, or against jazz itself, and it may have also been more of a response to the racial, social and political climate of the era than a musical movement" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz)). The Carnegie Hall entry gives the same answer as a *historical* claim (Black Power + Civil Rights → soul jazz) without any hedging.
- **My own reading, offered as a reading and not a verdict:** the sound is real, the name is retrospective, and the two facts are not in conflict. The style existed and was played before anyone called it that (the Wikipedia article dates the real blending of gospel/R&B/blues/jazz to the 1950s and the *naming* to the 1960s), and the label did push the category onto artists who resisted it. A public page should say both, in that order, and should not resolve it.

**Specific disputes I found in the sources:**

1. **Is soul jazz "instrumental" or does it include a vocal tradition?** Carnegie Hall: "Soul jazz is primarily an instrumental style... vocals... assume a secondary place" ([source](https://timeline.carnegiehall.org/genres/soul-jazz)). Wikipedia/AllMusic: one of the three characteristics is "the tradition of the vocalist" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz)). These are flatly incompatible descriptions of the same genre. The reconciliation is probably that "vocal tradition" here means *the practice of improvising on a song's vocal line* (playing a melody you could sing), not singing on the record — but **no source I read says that, and I am inferring it.**
2. **Is "funky hard bop" the same category?** Gridley's position, quoted above: "some listeners make no distinction between 'soul-jazz' and 'funky hard bop'," but "many musicians don't consider 'soul-jazz' to be continuous with 'hard bop.'" The *Jazz* textbook's "Hard Bop — Funky" chapter supports a two-chapter split. And a third position exists in the discography: Blue Mitchell is a "jazz musician who worked in jazz, rhythm and blues, soul, rock, and funk" (his Wikipedia infobox), which is a fourth category. **Four incompatible taxonomies, all in the sources, none reconciled.**
3. **The soul jazz / free jazz boundary in the 1970s.** Wikipedia's *Soul jazz* article is careful to distinguish soul jazz from free jazz (Ayler's late electric work, Eddie Harris's *The Electrifying Eddie Harris* (1968), and "bizarre electronic effects" "fell outside the bounds of what critics considered legitimate, serious jazz"). But the article's *Legacy* section also notes that Blue Mitchell's later records and the Jazz Crusaders' evolution overlap heavily with the genres soul jazz was supposed to be distinct from. **Where the line runs depends entirely on which year you pick.**
4. **The exact founding year of Soul Jazz Records (the label): 1991 or 1992.** The Wikipedia article says **1991** and gives the catalogue numbering (the first 50 LPs, 1992–1997). Other sources give 1992. The task statement gives 1992. **I have not verified this against the label itself and I am flagging the discrepancy rather than picking.**

### THINGS I AM UNSURE OF (kept deliberately visible)

I have flagged each inline. A public page should not quote them.**
- **The Henry Louis Gates Jr. / PBS attribution.** The characterization of Stanley Turrentine as "already an avant-garde at heart" is attributed to Gates via a PBS *Independent Lens* documentary page (a *Soul Jazz* documentary). I have **not** fetched that page or the documentary, and I do not know Gates's exact words. This attribution must be verified before it is used.

I believe it is authentic (it is a well-known Adderley quote about "Mercy, Mercy, Mercy"), but **I have it from one fetched page and I have not confirmed the occasion or the exact wording.** Do not quote it verbatim on a public page without checking.
