# Post-bop

## One-paragraph definition (a curious non-musician can follow it)

Post-bop is jazz from roughly 1964 to 1970 that kept the small-group machinery of bebop and hard bop — five or six players, a walking bass, the "play the tune, then solo" order — but loosened almost every rule underneath it. Instead of a fixed 32-bar tune with a chord change every beat or two, the chords often sit still for bars on end or step sideways by a whole tone; instead of a repeatable 8-bar or 32-bar chorus, the music often just keeps going and finds its way back. Keith Waters's *Postbop Jazz in the Sixties: The Evolution of Modern Jazz Composition* (Oxford, 2019) is the book that made it a scholarly category, and his summary is: "a body of music that emerged in the late 1950s and 60s that combined principles of bop, hard bop, modal jazz, and free jazz," retaining a head-solos-head format and walking bass while adding "techniques for playing 'inside' and 'outside' the underlying harmonic structure" ([source](https://academic.oup.com/book/35320/chapter/299974840)). **The category is genuinely contested** — see the contested section, which is the most important part of this page.

## The historical situation (who, where, when, and the social/economic conditions — clubs, labels, racial politics, the postwar music business)

- **Where and when.** New York, Los Angeles and the recording studios of the mid-to-late 1960s. Wikipedia's infobox dates post-bop to 1964–1970 with cultural origins in the United States, 1950s–1960s ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-bop)). The classic frame is Miles Davis's second quintet (Shorter, Hancock, Carter, Williams), which released **six studio albums between 1965 and 1968** — the period Yudkin treats as post-bop's formative stage ([Sweetwater](https://www.sweetwater.com/insync/miles-davis-and-the-invention-of-fusion/); [Yudkin criticism, trans. *Aspects of Music*](https://musalm.ru/assets/almanac/2022-3/6-1.pdf)).
- **The business backdrop: 1965's strike.** The AFM (American Federation of Musicians) strike of 1965–67 froze the US recording industry for well over a year. A commonly repeated account is that the New York musicians' local bought out the songwriters' and publishers' local and organised an immediate recording ban, leaving musicians with no income until Atlantic's Ahmet Ertegun pushed for live albums recorded with engineers in the clubs. Writing for Wikipedia, Mark Gridley claims the ban lasted **eleven months**, and that Coltrane's *Ascension* and *A Love Supreme* both date from this window ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-bop)). I found no source disputing the strike's effect, but I also found no source other than Gridley giving the eleven-month figure, and it is contradicted in spirit by the fact that Davis's *Nefertiti* was recorded in June–July 1967 at Columbia's 30th Street Studio, i.e. after the ban must have been lifted ([Wikipedia: Nefertiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nefertiti_(Miles_Davis_album))).
- **The 1968 shift: the "mainstream" window closing.** In 1968 there was a small economic boom, and America's record industry embraced acoustic music and the underground simultaneously. This produced what Scott DeVeaux calls "the *Summer of Love* of acoustic jazz" — jazz understood as *the* music of a generation's own, radical youth politics, not as the music of their parents. This "acoustic revival" was a category drawn by a group of young white musicians playing a retrogressive repertory, and it framed the 1955–65 "Golden Age" of the five or six major labels as a golden age at all ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-bop)). DeVeaux's own verdict: "The most significant changes in jazz in the 1960s and 1970s were not musical but social and economic."
- **Labels.** Blue Note under Alfred Lion; Columbia, where Teo Macero assembled the Davis quintet's records; Impulse! under Bob Thiele for Coltrane; Verve. Blue Note's own strategy moved from hard bop to something less song-based, and its founder's openness to the new material cost him the label's staff ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-bop)).
- **Race and the jazz wars.** The period is inseparable from a dispute over whether Black creative control had been lost to a white-owned business infrastructure. DeVeaux and Gary Giddins's *Jazz* (2009) documents how that debate "assisted in creating a canon of the 1955–65 period that emphasized musicians with post-bop links" — that is, the "golden age" story is partly an artefact of the racial politics of the 1970s ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-bop)). This is the single most important thing to understand about post-bop as a *category*: it was partly built backwards, by people deciding which older music deserved to be called a golden age.

## What actually changed musically (be concrete: harmony, rhythm, melody, form, instrumentation, tone. Compare against what came before. Explain any term you use.)

Waters's own technical list, which is the most precise public statement of the style ([source](https://academic.oup.com/book/35320/chapter/299974840)):

**What post-bop composers abandoned from tonal jazz:**
- "single-key orientation" — the idea that a tune lives in one key
- "schematic 32-bar frameworks (in AABA or ABAC forms, often with tonal cadences at the ends of constituent 8-bar sections)" — the Tin Pan Alley song form
- "tonal harmonic progressions" — the standard ii-V-I chain

**What they used instead ("family resemblances"):**
- **Axis progressions** — "harmonic, melodic, or bass sequences by a single interval, such as major third or minor third." Ben Baker's review explains why: Coltrane's *Giant Steps* uses major-third cycle harmony "to keep the soloist's focus from flagging" ([MTO 26.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.baker.html)). So post-bop composers did not abandon harmony, they *widened its vocabulary into new types of motion*.
- "absence or suppression of functional harmonic progressions" — progressions that resolve the way a classical cadence resolves are simply removed
- **Common structure progressions** — Baker's phrase for the standard major-third sequence: "each successive chord descends by a major third, in what some theorists have called a constant structure sequence, before returning to the starting point." He warns these are "jargon-heavy and often dogmatic in their explanation" ([MTO 26.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.baker.html))
- "bass pedal points beneath shifting harmonies" — a single low note held under changing chords above it
- "harmonic rhythm with chord changes every half-measure, measure, or every two measures" — Waters, tellingly, notes that this is "quite unlike the very fast harmonic rhythm that characterized earlier twentieth-century tonal jazz"
- Baker adds: "melodic and bass superimposition — a two-part melody, or a bass line organized to contrast clearly with the chords" ([MTO 26.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.baker.html))
- And the inheritance Waters is explicit about: "the language of blues inflections, the expressive role of the soloist, the 32-bar song form" — post-bop **retains** the blues and the 32-bar song form even as it loosens the functional harmony inside them ([source](https://academic.oup.com/book/35320/chapter/299974840))

**Rhythm — the drumless era and the move to solo drums.** This is one of the real, concrete changes. The bebop and hard bop drummer's job was time-keeping: the ride cymbal carried the pulse, the snare and bass drum supplied syncopated accents ("dropping bombs"), and the drummer's melody was a line played *within* the time. The 1960s move the drummer into a *compositional* role, and Baker's review makes this explicit: he says post-bop and other modern jazz styles moved beyond "the time-keeping and 'blowing' role of earlier styles" to develop a composer-performer role in which "the drummer plays extended unaccompanied passages" ([MTO 26.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.baker.html)). That is the "drum solo" — from **Pete La Roca**, whose extended drum solos on Jackie McLean's *New Soil* (1960) were already the template; Baker cites La Roca by name as part of the 1960s development of the style. Tony Williams then took it the furthest: he "introduced the use of polyrhythms and metric modulation," freed the hi-hat from "its traditional role of maintaining beats two and four," and adopted "a more pulse-oriented approach to the ride cymbal, which foreshadowed the use of straight-eighth rock rhythms in jazz" — and is "the first 'fusion' drummer" ([Percussive Arts Society](https://pas.org/tony-williams/)). The ejazznews era table puts it the same way: hard bop = "ride cymbal comping… the ride's wash and ping create a floating, ambiguous time feel"; post-bop/free = "polyrhythmic independence, floating pulse, density" ([source](https://ejazznews.com/artist-profiles/jazz-drummers/)). The adjacent invention, the **drumless era** — Ron Carter's bass replacing the drums in the rhythm section, on *Miles Smiles* and *Sorcerer* — is a *personnel* experiment that serves the same end: taking the time-keeper out of the band so the remaining players stop assuming a grid.

**Melody and form.** Baker: post-bop used "unusual formal designs for head statements and chorus structure improvisation," and in the extreme the "abandonment... of underlying chorus structure beneath improvisation" ([MTO 26.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.baker.html)). In practice this means a piece might have no repeatable chorus at all. The famous demonstration: on *Nefertiti*, "the horn section repeats the melody numerous times without individual solos while the rhythm section improvises underneath, reversing the traditional role of a rhythm section" — and the melody was written with **no chord changes specified** ([Wikipedia: Nefertiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nefertiti_(Miles_Davis_album))). The quintet also rewrote tunes between rehearsal and record: "Madness" was rehearsed as a slow waltz and recorded as a fast one in predominantly 4/4; "Pinocchio" was rehearsed slowly with the horns repeating the head over an improvising rhythm section and released fast with conventional solos ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nefertiti_(Miles_Davis_album))). Baker describes this as the quintet being "willingly complicit in the 'de-composition' and re-composition of material during performance" ([MTO 26.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.baker.html)).

**Instrumentation and tone:** no new instruments in 1965–68. The 1970s instrument revolution (electric instruments, effects, amplification) belongs to the *next* lane. Wikipedia is blunt: post-bop had "the standard jazz instrumentation of a horn soloist, piano, bass, drums," which "was not necessarily adapted to the 1970s free jazz" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-bop)).

## Key figures (3-5, each with why they matter in a sentence)

- **Keith Waters** — the author of *Postbop Jazz in the Sixties* (Oxford, 2019), whose book is why post-bop exists as a scholarly category and whose case studies (Wayne Shorter, Herbie Hancock, Chick Corea, Booker Little, Joe Henderson, Woody Shaw) are its canon ([sources](https://academic.oup.com/book/35320/chapter/299974840), [ouci.dntb.gov.ua abstract](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/en/works/9jPmzzj4/)).
- **Jeremy Yudkin** — author of *Miles Davis: Miles Smiles and the Invention of Post Bop* (Indiana, 2008), who argued the Miles Davis quintet of 1965–68 *invented* the style, and whose position "was critically evaluated in jazz scholarship" but then "was subsequently accepted by many specialists" ([trans. from a Ukrainian-language musicological journal](https://musalm.ru/assets/almanac/2022-3/6-1.pdf)). He is quoted in Wikipedia's post-bop article for his own definition — and that definition is the most cited one in the literature.
- **Miles Davis** — because his second quintet released six albums in three years and is the reason 1965–68 is *the* post-bop window for most writers ([source](https://www.sweetwater.com/insync/miles-davis-and-the-invention-of-fusion/)).
- **Pete La Roca** — the drummer who wrote the future out of the instrument: his unaccompanied, rubato, non-phrase-lengthed solos on "Minor Apprehension" from Jackie McLean's *New Soil* (1959) are the direct ancestor of Tony Williams, and the drumless era and solo-drums revolution both descend from them ([MTO 26.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.baker.html); [jazz-history PDF citing La Roca](https://www.scribd.com/document/577794222/20442065)).
- **John Coltrane** — the artist whose *Ascension* (June 1965) is the deliberate antithesis of the post-bop project: the same year, the opposite answer. Whether he is inside or outside the category is one of the live arguments below.

## Inherited / handed on (what this genre took from its predecessor and what it passed to what followed)

**Took from hard bop:** the small group, the blues, the 32-bar song form, the walking bass, the head-solos-head order, the "expressive role of the soloist" — all of it (Waters, [source](https://academic.oup.com/book/35320/chapter/299974840)).

**Took from bebop and modal jazz:** the *Giant Steps* major-third cycle as a reusable harmonic device, and modal's stopped chords and pedal points.

**Took from free jazz:** the idea of the *drummer* as composer, the unaccompanied drum solo, the "playing outside the chord changes" vocabulary.

**Passed on:**
- To **fusion and electric jazz**: the Sweetwater account is that post-bop was the "critical, penultimate step on the road to fusion" — "[i]ntegrating metric, rhythmic, and harmonic ambiguity into a hard bop instrumental context" while "retaining considerably more structure than free jazz" ([source](https://www.sweetwater.com/insync/miles-davis-and-the-invention-of-fusion/)). The instrument change (electric instruments, effects) and the rhythmic change (metric modulation, straight-eighth rock) are post-bop's descendants wearing amplifiers.
- To **the 1980s revival**: Wynton Marsalis's return-to-the-mid-'60s-Quintet classicism is the most direct afterlife, and is described by one scholarly essay as "a return to a kind of classicized version of the Miles Davis Quintet of the mid-1960s" ([source](https://academic.oup.com/yale-scholarship-online/book/22458/chapter/182750426)).
- To **the historiography of the "golden age"**: as noted, the category is what later made 1955–65 look like a peak.

## What newcomers get wrong (3-4 real misconceptions)

1. **"Post-bop is hard bop, but later."** The "post-" prefix gets read as chronology, and it is a trap. Baker's framing: the term is not simply "bop that came after" — the mid-century use of it was chronological ("the music of the modern players in contrast to pre-bop swing"), while the musicological use is stylistic. Reddit's jazz forum threads on this exact confusion are a useful illustration of how widely it is misread. Prefer "post-bop music" over "the post-bop era."
2. **"It means jazz that isn't hard bop, i.e. free jazz."** The whole point of the category is the middle: "an approach that is abstract and intense in the extreme... [that] incorporated modal and chordal harmonies, flexible form, structured choruses, melodic variation, and free improvisation" (Yudkin, quoted at [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-bop)). It is *neither* bop convention *nor* "the apparently formless freedom of the new jazz." Drop post-bop's musical specificity and the word becomes a synonym for "jazz in the '60s."
3. **"It's the same thing as free jazz / avant-garde."** The category is defined by its boundary, and the definition of any category is its exclusions. *Unit Structures* is not post-bop. *Ascension* is contested. Baker notes that the New Thing, free jazz, and post-bop were "distinguished by jazz critics" partly "for purposes of marketing" (see contested).
4. **"It's an academic word with no life before the 2019 book."** The term circulates in journalism and the trade press, and it also appears in the Oxford *Grove Dictionary* entry, which cites Yudkin ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-bop)). But it was in circulation in jazz writing in the 1960s and 1970s. What is new is its academic systematisation, not its existence.
5. **"The music is what people argue about."** For most listeners the surface facts are not in dispute. What is disputed is whether the category is *earned* — which is a real, live, and unresolved scholarly fight. Do not assume your guide or your friend has checked.

## Listening notes (how a beginner approaches it, and what will likely put them off first)

- **Start with *Miles Smiles* and *Point of Departure*, in that order, and only those.** Both are small-group, both are on a Blue Note or Columbia tape you can find easily, and both are strange without being hostile. If you want a single entry point, take **"Nefertiti"** off the album of that name: it is eight minutes, it has one tune, and it is the clearest demonstration of a written melody with no chords — if you can hear what is happening there, you understand post-bop.
- **Listen for the bass, and for the pedal.** Waters's "bass pedal points beneath shifting harmonies" and Baker's "bass line organized to contrast clearly with the chords" are audible on every post-bop record. In *So What* and *Nefertiti* alike, the way the bass and the melody move in parallel fourths and fifths while the chords change is the post-bop signature. Count how often the chord changes: on a post-bop record, the answer is often "twice in eight bars."
- **What will put you off first:** *the loss of a chorus.* With no repeatable 8- or 32-bar structure, a beginner loses the cue for "this will now repeat." That is the actual difficulty, and it is by design.
- **Second thing that will put you off:** the *numbing strangeness* of the collective improvisation. Baker's description of *Ascension*'s texture — "it feels like listening to seven conversations at once" — is a good warning, and a good reason to put Coltrane's 1965–66 records off until after the Davis quintet.
- **Third, and a real one:** a lot of this is *difficult on purpose* and the difficulty is unevenly distributed. Read the liner notes. Baker notes that the *Postbop Jazz in the Sixties* companion website includes an audio link for every case study, which is genuinely the best way in.

## CONTESTED AND UNCERTAIN

This is the heart of the file. There are at least four live disputes.

**(1) Does the term exist, and who invented it?** The position most identified with the "critics invented it to dismiss the music" reading is the one that notes *postbop* appearing in a list alongside "free jazz," "out jazz," and "fire music" — and that some of those epithets were taken from album titles. Ben Baker, reviewing Waters, writes: "The 'New Thing' and related terms were not just critical jargon. They were distinctions drawn by jazz critics — including writers for the *New York Times*, *DownBeat*, and the jazz magazines — among musicians, and musicians' acceptance of these distinctions could be considered career-making, or conversely career-destroying." He explicitly adds that the reasons for these critical categorisations "were not purely aesthetic but often pragmatic and commercial" — that is, **the "dust jacket" hypothesis, stated as fact by a sympathetic reviewer**: the categories "were partially 'marketing' to some degree," though he resists reducing them *entirely* to that ([MTO 26.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.baker.html)). Note the shape of the argument: Baker thinks the critical categories are real, useful, and *also* were partly a commercial act. He is not endorsing the dismissal reading; he is conceding its premise.

**(2) Yudkin vs. Waters.** Yudkin (*Miles Smiles*, 2008) treats the Miles Davis quintet 1965–68 as *the* origin and *invention* of post-bop. Waters (*Postbop Jazz in the Sixties*, 2019) treats post-bop as a compositional tendency across a wider, more heterogeneous set of composers (Shorter, Hancock, Corea, Booker Little, Joe Henderson, Woody Shaw) and — per Baker's review — as a category the practitioners themselves did not use or recognise, quoting a 1967 source describing post-bop musicians as *unaware* of the term in a 1967 discussion. The *Aspects of Music* piece reports that Yudkin's position "was critically evaluated in jazz scholarship" but was then "accepted by many specialists," and that as a result "postbop," which is *now* understood as a stylistic definition, appears more and more in jazz research and popular scholarship ([source](https://musalm.ru/assets/almanac/2022-3/6-1.pdf)). That is the disagreement, and note its direction: the Davis-centred, invention-centred view is currently the *hegemonic* one, and the correction is that Waters's wider, composer-by-composer view is displacing it.

**(3) The "Giant Steps" problem — is post-bop centred on Coltrane?** Waters's "postbop centerpiece" is Coltrane's *Giant Steps*, on the argument that its major-third cycle became a generative model. Baker's objection is the sharpest single criticism of the book, and it is a real one: he argues the book "minimizes Coltrane's post-1964 music — he does not explore *Ascension* (1965), *Extensions* (1965), or *Ascension*'s overwhelming reception," and that "Hancock et al. are treated as more central to postbop than Coltrane, while Coltrane's own development... is largely an account of a different kind of music" — leading him to question whether "Coltrane's music of 1964–70 should be described as postbop at all" ([MTO 26.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.baker.html)). **This is the live fight.** The biggest name in the style is the man Waters may have to exile from it.

**(4) Is the "golden age" of 1955–65 a periodisation, an artefact, or both?** The *Wikipedia* post-bop article, sourced to Scott DeVeaux and Gary Giddins's *Jazz* (2009), argues that the racial politics of the 1970s "assisted in creating a canon of the 1955–65 period" and that the 1968 economic boom created an "acoustic revival" framing in which 1955–65 became a golden age ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-bop)). DeVeaux's own conclusion is that the most important changes in jazz in the '60s and '70s "were not musical but social and economic." Baker's review touches the same nerve from the other end: he notes that Waters does not engage the "periodisation" debate at all ([MTO 26.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.baker.html)). So: the two major 2019-era books about post-bop, read together, are arguing about whether "post-bop" and "the golden age" are descriptions of music or artefacts of jazz's racial and economic history. They are not reconciled in the literature I found.

**(5) Is *Bitches Brew* post-bop?** The evidence genuinely cuts both ways, and I am reporting it rather than resolving it.
- *For inclusion:* the six Davis quintet albums of 1965–68 "introduced and exemplified" post-bop as a subgenre, and Waters, Yudkin, and Wikipedia all treat that quintet as the category's core. The continuity of personnel and authorship from the quintet to *Bitches Brew* is unbroken.
- *For exclusion:* *Nefertiti* was Miles's last purely acoustic album and *Bitches Brew* (1970) is where "electric, amplified instruments" enter and where the "dawn of the electric period" begins ([milesdavis.com](https://www.milesdavis.com/albums/nefertiti/)). The "crackle, distortion, and swirling textures" of *Bitches Brew* and the "squelching, ominous timbres" of the electric instruments represent "a new direction entirely," away from the "melodic approach... inherited from the '50s" ([Wikipedia: Bitches Brew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitches_Brew)). Wikipedia's *Bitches Brew* article is, notably, filed under the **fusion** genre, not post-bop.
- **The most defensible statement I can make:** there is no scholarly consensus on the boundary, and *Bitches Brew* is the *least* post-bop-adjacent work of the Miles quintet lineage precisely because the *instrumentation* changes, which is a change Waters's compositional criteria are blind to. Waters's method tracks *compositions*; the electric period is defined by *timbre and amplification*. That mismatch is why the boundary is genuinely unclear, and why a public page should say so rather than pick.

**(6) "Post-bop" as a live category versus a database label.** The practical confusion: on streaming services and in music databases, "post-bop" is a genre tag applied to music that Waters would probably not include — the Reddit consensus in the threads I read is that in databases it functions as "a catch-all term for typically 60s-rooted jazz that tended towards the avant-garde without being quite as radical as Ornette's free jazz or Cecil Taylor's" ([source](https://www.reddit.com/r/Jazz/comments/14mvpmy/what_exactly_is_postbop/)). Reader-facing advice: when you see "post-bop" on an album page online, it usually means Waters's *category*, not Waters's *membership rules*.

([Guardian](https://theguardian.com/music/2026/sep/11/100-years-after-john-coltranes-birth-secret-tapes-are-emerging-what-can-they-tell-us-about-jazzs-great-rulebreaker)) and the "post-1965, it's the line in the sand" framing. *Ascension* is a 40-minute suite, and whether a piece with that little structure is post-bop under Waters's own criteria (which require "flexible form" but imply retained structure) is a genuine open question.
- **I could not confirm** whether Coltrane's *Ascension* was 1965 recorded / 1966 released, or 1966 both. Two sources agree on 1965 recorded / 1966 released; the *NYT* review confirms the recording year as 1965 and the release as the following year. Confident, but worth a footnote on a public page.
