# bebop

## One-paragraph definition (a curious non-musician can follow it)

Bebop is the jazz of the mid-1940s and early 1950s that made jazz hard, fast and harmonically restless for the first time. Before it, a jazz orchestra played written arrangements you could dance to: a melody, big chords underneath, a rhythm you could feel from across the room. Bebop threw most of that away. It was played by five or six musicians instead of twenty, it ran at breakneck speed, and — this is the part that shocked people — the tune you recognised from the radio was only the *frame*. Most of what you heard was a saxophone or trumpet player inventing melodies over a bass line that walked steadily through the chords. The chords themselves got stranger: instead of simple major and minor chords, players used chords built out of the tensions inside them (the flat ninth, the sharp ninth, the tritone), and chords that substituted one another's function, so the music sounded like it was pulling towards keys it never actually arrived at. To a listener in 1946 it sounded fast, nervous and unmusical on purpose. Kenny Clarke, one of the players who invented it, put it plainly: *"'Bebop' was a label that certain journalists later gave it, but we never labeled the music. It was just modern music, we would call it. We wouldn't call it anything, really, just music."* ([Wikipedia: Bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop), quoting Clarke). The name is a piece of noise, not a description.

## The historical situation (who, where, when, and the social/economic conditions — clubs, labels, racial politics, the postwar music business)

- **Where and when.** New York City, from roughly 1940 to 1955. The centre of gravity was 52nd Street ("Swing Street") and Harlem: Clark Monroe's Uptown House, the Three Deuces, Minton's Playhouse, the Royal Roost. Wikipedia's infobox gives the cultural origins as "Mid-1940s, United States," with swing and Kansas City jazz as the stylistic parents ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)).
- **The strike that made it.** The single most important economic fact about bebop's birth is that it happened during the **1942–44 musicians' strike**, when the American Federation of Musicians barred more than 130,000 members from recording for the major labels. Swing bands broke up because a band that could not record could not tour; the big bands gave way to small combos "more suited to experimentation," and "it meant bebop's evolution went largely unrecorded" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)). Bebop's first real records are therefore *after* the strike, on small labels — a fact that shapes everything about how the canon looks.
- **It was "musicians' music" by circumstance.** Bebop "originated as 'musicians' music,' played by musicians with other money-making gigs who did not care about the commercial potential of the new music. It did not attract the attention of major record labels nor was it intended to" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)). The economic consequence is durable: the repertory is defined by a handful of small-label sessions, not by hit records, and reissues and compilations (*The Charlie Parker Story*, *Birth of the Cool*) rather than original LPs are the canonical objects.
- **Small labels did the documenting.** Apollo (Coleman Hawkins, Feb 1944), Savoy (Tiny Grimes, Sept 1944), De Luxe, Dial, Prestige, Jubilee. The first formal bebop recording is generally given as Hawkins's "Woody'n You" for Apollo, 16 February 1944 ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)).
- **The big-band pipeline.** Parker and Gillespie both came up through the **Earl Hines** band (1943) and then out of it with Billy Eckstine (1944), whose orchestra was recorded on V-discs for the Armed Forces Network. The Eckstine band contained a remarkable concentration of bebop personnel — Gillespie, Dexter Gordon, Gene Ammons, Leo Parker, Tommy Potter, Art Blakey, Sarah Vaughan — and its format of vocalists, banter and bebop soloists is what a later generation of bebop-oriented big bands imitated, a style that "might be termed 'popular bebop'" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)). The military recording network is an underappreciated distribution channel: it made bebop the first jazz to reach mass audiences *without* the record business choosing to sell it.
- **Race and the contract.** Bebop's musicians were largely Black and largely excluded from the mainstream business that profited from Black music — the situation described in the *National WWII Museum*'s account of modern jazz, and visible in the restrictions, the low pay and the club-pay system that drove players to the after-hours jam sessions at Minton's and Monroe's where the music actually advanced. The recorded evidence is thin precisely because the economics were hostile. This is stated as a structural condition in the sources, not a documented quotation, and the corpus does not contain a first-person account of it from a Parker-era player.
- **Minton's as a laboratory, and the politics inside it.** Wikipedia is explicit that the atmosphere at Minton's "was an air of exclusivity: the 'regular' musicians would often reharmonize the standards, add complex rhythmic and phrasing devices into their melodies, or 'heads,' and play them at breakneck tempos in order to exclude those whom they considered outsiders or simply weaker players" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)). The music's difficulty was partly a social technology — a way of sorting musicians. This matters later for the inside/outside thesis: bebop's "outside" was, at the start, partly a *gate*.

## What actually changed musically (concrete: harmony, rhythm, melody, form, instrumentation, tone, compared against what came before)

- **The combo replaces the big band.** "The classic bebop combo consisted of saxophone, trumpet, double bass, drums and piano... This was in stark contrast to the large ensembles favored during the swing era" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)). Fewer players means each one is exposed. The rhythm section stops being an accompaniment and becomes a co-soloist: "rhythm sections that expanded on their role as tempo-keepers" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)).
- **Form: the head is a frame, not the song.** "Typically, a theme (a 'head'... ) would be presented together at the beginning and the end of each piece, with improvisational solos based on the chords of the compositions. Thus, the majority of a piece in bebop style would be improvisation, the only threads holding the work together being the underlying harmonies played by the rhythm section" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)). Compare a Glenn Miller arrangement, where the melody is the content and repeats in a fixed big-band form.
- **The walking bass.** The double bass moves in steady quarter notes through the chord tones — hence "walking." It is the beat-keeper and the harmonic map at the same time; the soloist can hear where the changes are going because the bass is announcing them. (The term itself is not defined in the bebop sources; Columbia's Jazz Glossary treats it under related entries and the general explanation above is the standard plain-language one, offered here as exposition rather than as a quotation.)
- **Harmony: extensions, alterations, and substitution.** "Bebop chord voicings often dispensed with the root and fifth tones, instead basing them on the leading intervals that defined the tonality of the chord. That opened up creative possibilities for harmonic improvisation such as tritone substitutions and use of diminished scale based improvised lines that could resolve to the key center in numerous and surprising ways" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)). In plain terms: instead of naming a chord by its root and fifth, bebop players named it by its *tensions* (the notes that stick out), and then substituted chords a tritone apart — the same distances that define the "flatted fifth" entry in Columbia's glossary of bebop-era terms.
- **The bebop scale.** A scale built by adding a chromatic "passing" note between each of the scale's own notes — major scale, plus the flat 2, flat 5, flat 7 — so that all seven chord tones of any diatonic chord are available inside a single scale. It exists so that a single run of notes works over a moving progression. The bebop scale appears as a named item in Levine's *The Jazz Theory Book*'s table of contents, between "Bebop and Pentatonic Scales" and reharmonisation, which is the corpus's evidence that it is standard pedagogical equipment rather than a Parker invention ([Sher Music product page](https://www.shermusic.com/products/the-jazz-theory-book)).
- **Rhythm: the offbeat, the eight-bar phrase, the "bombs."** Phrases "over an odd number of bars" that "overlap their phrases across bar lines and across major harmonic cadences"; players stating the harmony in their line *before* the rhythm section plays it, "creat[ing] a momentary dissonance [that] creates a strong sense of forward motion" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)). The Columbia glossary defines "bombs" as "irregular bass drum accents (typical of bebop drummers)" ([Columbia Jazz Glossary](http://ccnmtl.columbia.edu/projects/jazzglossary/)) — the drum accents falling where you do not expect them.
- **Melody: Lester Young's phrasing, taken further.** "Bop improvisers built upon the phrasing ideas first brought to attention by Lester Young's soloing style" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)). That is worth sitting with: bebop's melodic ancestor is the *cool* saxophonist, not a bopper. The two streams that later became "hot" and "cool" were, in the 1940s, not yet enemies.
- **Contrafacts.** Chord progressions of pop standards reused under new, more complex melodies — "the blues (at base, I-IV-V, but infused with II-V motion) and 'rhythm changes' (I-VI-II-V, the chords to the 1930s pop standard 'I Got Rhythm')" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)).
- **Tone and the public reaction.** Where swing "appeared to sound racing, nervous, erratic and often fragmented" by contrast with "the bouncy, organized, danceable compositions of Benny Goodman and Glenn Miller," bebop seemed to be doing nothing except being difficult ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)).
- **Where the sound actually came from — the honest dispute.** Charlie Parker's own account of the breakthrough, told as the "Cherokee" story: *"'I'd been getting bored with the stereotyped changes that were being used... and I kept thinking there's bound to be something else. I could hear it sometimes. I couldn't play it.... I was working over 'Cherokee,' and, as I did, I found that by using the higher intervals of a chord as a melody line and backing them with appropriately related changes, I could play the thing I'd been hearing. It came alive."* ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop), quoting Gerhard Kubik's account of Parker's words). Against the reading that bebop was European modernism arriving late, Gerhard Kubik argues the opposite: *"Auditory inclinations were the African legacy in [Parker's] life, reconfirmed by the experience of the blues tonal system, a sound world at odds with the Western diatonic chord categories. Bebop musicians eliminated Western-style functional harmony in their music while retaining the strong central tonality of the blues as a basis for drawing upon various African matrices"* ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)). The corpus does note the counter-evidence without endorsing it: Jimmy Raney described Parker listening to Bartók and Schoenberg, and says "a section from Bartók's Fifth Quartet sounded a lot like some of Parker's jazz improvisation" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)). Samuel Floyd's three-part account of what the blues did to bebop (new harmonic structures; more syncopated linear rhythmic complexity and a melodic angularity that made the blue note of the fifth degree a real device; and the re-establishment of the blues as the organising principle) is quoted at the same page. **The corpus does not settle the African-vs-European-derivative question**, and neither does this file.

## Key figures (3-5, each with why they matter in a sentence)

- **Charlie Parker (alto saxophone, 1920–1955).** The player who, by his own account, found the sound and the harmonic method, and who wrote nearly all the tunes that became the bebop canon; the contested details of his best-known recording session are the standing example of jazz historiography's limits (see *Contested* below).
- **Dizzy Gillespie (trumpet, 1917–1993).** Co-inventor of the harmonic language, the scat singing that carried the "bebop" nonsense syllable into circulation, and the sideman whose presence makes the personnel disputes around the *Charlie Parker Story* unavoidable.
- **Thelonious Monk (piano, 1917–1982).** A regular at Minton's during the formative jam sessions and a presence on the crucial 1944 Hawkins session for Savoy; he claimed his composition "Bip Bop" (later "52nd Street Theme") was the origin of the name, and he rejected the term itself.
- **Kenny Clarke (drums, 1914–1985).** The "father of bebop" by reputation; the drummer who moved the beat off the downbeat and made the rhythm section an equal partner — and the man who said the players never called it bebop.
- **Lester Young (tenor saxophone, 1909–1959).** Not a bebopper, but the melodic source: his phrasing is what the boppers built on, and his light sound is the root of the *cool* line that forked off from bebop in the same decade.
- *(Runner-up, documented but not expanded here: Roy Eldridge, Don Byas, Coleman Hawkins, Dexter Gordon, Bud Powell, Max Roach, Oscar Pettiford, Mary Lou Williams — all present in the sources, all in the first generation.)*

## Inherited / handed on

- **Everything after it.** Wikipedia's own infobox lists the derivative forms as **avant-garde jazz, hard bop, post-bop** — plus a fourth, *wabap*, which is a good indication of how unstable these categories are even to the editors of an encyclopedia ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)).
- **The players, not the genre.** The musicians who then *were* bebop became cool (Mulligan, Konitz, Davis, Tristano), hard bop (Blakey, Gordon, Hawkins) and, a decade later, the avant-garde (Rollins, Coltrane, Dolphy, Coleman). Bebop is a personnel pool more than a style that got replaced.
- **The lingo escaped the music.** The word came with a whole slang, and *Life* magazine on 29 September 1952 listed it: *crazy* "new, wonderful, wildly exciting"; *gone* "the tops—superlative of crazy"; *goof* "to blow a wrong note or make a mistake"; *dig* "to understand, appreciate the subtleties of"; *stoned* "drunk, captivating, ecstatic, sent out of the world" ([Etymonline, "bop"](https://www.etymonline.com/word/bop)). *cool* was on that list too, meaning "tasty, pretty." This is the trail by which two later genre names entered the language through the *other* direction: as adjectives of approval, not as musical descriptions.
- **The bebop scale became a teaching object.** It is now a fixture of the curriculum rather than a style — which is precisely the problem the *inside/outside* thesis has to grapple with (see [`06-inside-outside-thesis/inside-outside-terminology.md`](../thesis/inside-outside-terminology.md)).
- **The outsider pole.** Both Minton's exclusivity and the 1940s idea of playing over the changes "to exclude those whom they considered outsiders" mean bebop carried a gate concept with it from the start — a genealogy the later "outside" musicians inherited and turned against it.

## What newcomers get wrong (3-4 real misconceptions)

1. **"It was fast and loud, so it must be angry."** Bebop's difficulty is structural, not temperamental. The chords move and the tempo is high, but the music is precise rather than frantic; the notorious "racing, nervous" quality was largely *how the public heard it*, not a description of the players' intention. Note also that the sound many people call "cool" — Lester Young's light, behind-the-beat phrasing — is bebop's melodic ancestor, not its opposite.
2. **"Bebop is just fast swing."** It is not: the swing big band wrote its own arrangements and the soloist's job was decoration. Bebop moved the innovation into the solo and made the rhythm section a participant, which is a different social contract inside the band as well as a different sound.
3. **"Bebop came first, then cool/hard bop replaced it."** The sources are against this. Miles Davis Nonet drew from the same personnel as the boppers; *Birth of the Cool* sessions are 1949–50, mid-bebop; Parker and Gillespie came out of the same bands; and the word *cool* was in circulation as bop slang by 1952. The fork is real; the sequence is a later simplification.
4. **"The music was universally hated."** It was not universally hated — it was *unpopular*, which is a different thing, and the dislike was widely racialised: the "bebop musician or bopper became a stock character in jokes of the 1950s, overlapping with the beatnik" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)), a caricature with clear racial freight. The corpus does not contain the reception data that would let us quantify the hostility, and says nothing here about how much of it was open hostility versus simple commercial indifference.
5. **"Bebop and the avant-garde are opposites."** As Mark Gridley puts it in the passage Duhaime quotes at length, early jazz by Louis Armstrong, bop by Charlie Parker and cool jazz by Lennie Tristano "could justifiably be called 'avant-garde'" — and Gridley adds that the era's classifications were "very loose" ([Duhaime, *Eric Dolphy & Outness*](https://x_https_justinduhaime_com_wp_content_uploads_2021_02.txt); the file itself is a 2011 Carleton University seminar paper and its public URL is not in the corpus — see *Sources*).

## Listening notes (how a beginner approaches it, and what will likely put them off first)

- **Start with tracks, not albums.** The 78-era sessions are short (two to four minutes) and there is no filler. That is the *historical* format: bebop was recorded as singles and jam-session documents, so you are not missing a "full experience" by starting with "Ko-Ko" or "Anthropology."
- **Learn the head before the solo.** Because the head frames the improvisation ([Wikipedia: Bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)), you should know the tune. "I Got Rhythm" and "Autumn Leaves" are the two most useful tunes in the whole repertoire for hearing what bebop players are doing to the changes, because the chord grid is so widely known that you can *hear* the substitution. Keith Waters's discussion of "Autumn Leaves" in the 1960s makes exactly this point about the harmonic structure setting up the ear's expectations ([Keith Waters, "Autumn Leaves" in the 1960s](../../raw/waters-autumn-leaves.txt) — file in corpus, public URL not captured).
- **What will put you off first, honestly: the bass line and the tempo.** A beginner's ear will latch onto the walking bass — which is louder, more regular and more "present" than it was in the music they know — and will read it as the tune's melody, then get lost. The second thing that will put you off is speed: the first recording you play at full tempo will feel like noise. Ted Gioia's practical advice from *The History of the Love Song* era of his writing applies here: use **slow listening** — drop the playback speed well below the recorded tempo so the individual notes are audible, and treat it as a practice rather than as a bad first impression ([Ted Gioia, *How to Listen to Jazz*](https://pdfcoffee.com/ted-gioia-how-to-listen-to-jazz-basic-books-2016-pdf-free.html); LCCN 2016000587). His specific observation that slow listening revealed "how much can happen even within the narrow confines of a single note" is exactly the bebop experience.
- **One more expectation to lower.** The famous quote about bop being "racing, nervous, erratic" is a *contemporary* characterisation. If your first exposure is that description, you will be braced for chaos. Play "Boplicity" and the Hawkins Apollo session and listen for how *architectural* this music is.

## CONTESTED AND UNCERTAIN

This genre has the highest dispute density in the corpus. The four that matter:

1. **The birth date.** The corpus does not settle it, and the sources disagree in a way that cannot be harmonised.
   - The *earliest attested use of the word* is 1928: "The term 'bebop' is derived from nonsense syllables (vocables) used in scat singing; the first known example of 'bebop' being used was in McKinney's Cotton Pickers' 'Four or Five Times,' recorded in 1928" ([Wikipedia: Bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)). Etymonline dates the word itself to 1944 as a shortening of "bebop, rebop, bop — nonsense words in jazz lyrics, attested from at least 1928" and links the *style* to Parker and Gillespie ([Etymonline, "bebop"](https://www.etymonline.com/word/bebop)). Note the mismatch: a 1928 attestation for the word, a 1944 date for the coinage, a mid-1940s date for the *style*.
   - The *earliest story of the style* is Parker's "Cherokee" epiphany, which the source places at Clark Monroe's Uptown House "in early 1942" ([Wikipedia: Bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)).
   - The *first formal recording* is 16 February 1944 (Hawkins, Apollo) ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)).
   - The *definitive recordings* are 26 November 1945 (Savoy).
   So: word c. 1928 (or 1944), style early 1942, disc 1944, canon 1945, label spread 1940s. Anyone who tells you a single year is flattening a genuine mess. The corpus also does not resolve whether Parker's account of "Cherokee" is a reliable memory recorded late or a formalist origin story told retrospectively.
2. **Who coined "bebop."** At least five candidate answers, all in the corpus, none preferred by the source:
   - **Jazz musicians scatting nonsense syllables** — the scat/vocable origin above, attested from 1928.
   - **Thelonious Monk** — "claims that the original title 'Bip Bop,' for his composition better known as '52nd Street Theme,' was the origin of the name" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)).
   - **Charlie Christian** — "Some researchers speculate that it was a term used by Charlie Christian because it sounded like something he hummed along with his playing" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)). Note the source itself marks this as speculation by unnamed researchers.
   - **The audience** — Dizzy Gillespie's account: the name was coined by listeners who had no other way to request the pieces, and the press then adopted it: *"People, when they'd wanna ask for those numbers and didn't know the name, would ask for bebop."* ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)).
   - **Latin American bandleaders** — "Another theory is that it derives from the cry of 'Arriba! Arriba!' used by Latin American bandleaders of the period to encourage their bands" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)).
   Against all of these, two things are *not* in dispute: the players themselves rejected the name — "Parker himself never used the term, feeling it demeaned the music" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)) and Clarke's quote above — and by 1945 "bebop/rebop" as nonsense syllables was already ordinary R&B vocabulary (Lionel Hampton's "Hey! Ba-Ba-Re-Bop"). The etymology question and the *dating of the style* are two separate problems that are constantly run together.
3. **The personnel of the Parker Story, track by track.** The November 1945 session is the most heavily disputed recording in jazz. What the source establishes, and it is unusually explicit:
   - **Piano.** Bud Powell was booked but was absent — he had gone to Philadelphia with his mother. Dizzy Gillespie was present and introduced himself to producer Herman Reig with the line: **"Here's your piano player."** Gillespie was then under contract to Musicraft and could not be credited, so the earliest 78s list a pseudonym, "Hen Gates," a name known to be Gillespie's. Argonne Thornton (later Sadik Hakim) was also contacted and later claimed the date; session conductor Herman Lubinsky denied he played, possibly because Hakim was not yet a member of the New York local union and was told not to play. A second 78 of "Thriving on a Riff" (Savoy 945) credits Gillespie on piano; later reissues credit Hakim. Liner-note author John Mehegan believed Powell was present — which is the source of much of the confusion.
   - **Trumpet on "Ko-Ko."** "Many sources state that Gillespie is the trumpet player on this track in place of Davis (including the liner notes of one of Davis' own albums: *The Musings of Miles*)." Reasons given for the substitution: the piece was too difficult for the young Davis; his nerves; or that he was simply not present. Other sources insist it is Davis.
   - **Where the source stands:** it says the absence of Powell "is agreed by other sources," but it does **not** adjudicate the trumpet question, and the underlying issue is that the personnel listings "sometimes contradict each other" across issues of the same recordings.
   This is a good example of the general point: the corpus's *conflicts* are documented and the corpus does not resolve them. Any confident personnel list for these tracks in this project should be marked as contested.
4. **Where bebop's harmonic language came from.** African blues-derived tonal practice (Kubik, quoted above) versus European modernism (Schoenberg/Debussy, and the Raney anecdote about Parker and Bartók). Samuel Floyd's blues-account is given as a third position. The Wikipedia text presents the parallelism without deciding, and I have not added anything the sources cannot support.
5. **A minor unresolved detail:** whether the 1941 Minton's sessions were recorded and released on Esoteric ES 548, and which 1943 Eckstine jam dates are which (Stash ST-260 / ST-CD-535), are stated in the source without corroboration in the corpus. Treat as single-source.
