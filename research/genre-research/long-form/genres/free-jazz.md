# free jazz

## One-paragraph definition (a curious non-musician can follow it)

Free jazz is a loose family of jazz styles that became visible in the late 1950s and early
1960s, in which musicians deliberately let go of the two things that hold most jazz
together: a fixed beat that everybody shares, and a set of chord changes that says where
the music is supposed to be going. What replaces them is not chaos but *listening* — the
music keeps its pulse and its swing and all the old blues and bebop vocabulary, but its
shape is decided in the moment, by the players responding to each other. The centrepiece
is Ornette Coleman's *Free Jazz: A Collective Improvisation*, recorded in one afternoon in
1960 and released in 1961, whose title was so good that it became the name for the whole
movement — a marketing accident that stuck, and that has been misfiling musicians ever
since [1][2]. It is worth holding onto from the start: **"free jazz" is a bucket the
critics built, not a plan the musicians agreed on.** Mark Gridley treats the era's category
names as "very loose," loose in a way that "led some outsiders to assume … that the
different musicians of this period had more in common than they actually did," and notes
that calling the 1960s the *only* avant-garde period is simply false [1].

## The historical situation (who, where, when, and the social/economic conditions)

The music runs back further than its nickname suggests. The standard start date is the
late 1950s [2][3], but there is a genuine prehistory: in the late 1940s Lennie Tristano's
"Intuition" is the piece most often cited, and Ted Gioia argues it belongs with **third
stream** rather than free jazz proper, because it uses contemporary classical techniques
like serialism [2]. That disagreement is not pedantry; it is the whole point of the
neighbouring page, and it is one of the reasons this genre file is careful about its own
boundaries.

The first wave is **Ornette Coleman and Cecil Taylor**, and it begins *while bebop is still
happening*, not after it. Coleman recorded *Tomorrow Is the Question!* and *Something
Else!!!!* for the independent California label Contemporary in 1958 — records that already
abandon typical 32-bar form and "often employ abrupt changes in tempo and mood" [2], and
that were *not* the free jazz of 1960: the melodies are written, the solos lean on bebop
line. Taylor's *Jazz Advance*, released by **Transition** in 1956, kept the big-band form
and "showed ties to traditional jazz, albeit with an expanded harmonic vocabulary" [2].
This is the asymmetry that matters: the avant-garde was not waiting for bebop to end. It
was running alongside it, on small labels, from the mid-1950s.

Coleman's move east and onto **Atlantic** is what made the movement visible. *The Shape of
Jazz to Come* (1959) and *Change of the Century* (1960) "marked a radical step beyond his
more conventional early work"; on them "he strayed from the tonal basis that formed the
lines of his earlier albums and began truly examining the possibilities of atonal
improvisation" [2]. The decisive record was *Free Jazz*, recorded at A&R Studios in New
York in 1960 with a **double quartet split across the left and right channels** — a
separation of the stereo field into two groups improvising against each other. It "marked
an abrupt departure from the highly structured compositions of his past," brought "a more
aggressive, cacophonous texture to Coleman's work," and "the record's title would provide
the name for the nascent free jazz movement" [2].

The social conditions are the ones every account reaches for, and they should be stated
with the same caution. Wikipedia's history notes that critics "have drawn connections
between the term 'free jazz' and the American social setting during the late 1950s and
1960s, especially the emerging social tensions of racial integration and the civil rights
movement," listing *Brown v. Board* (1954), the Freedom Riders (1961), Freedom Summer
(1963) and the Freedom Schools as the context in which the word "free" carried weight
[2]. New Grove makes the political reading more specific for the militant wing: for some
performers the style "was loosely linked to the Black Power movement in the USA, partly
because of the radical political outlook of some of its practitioners and advocates (e.g.,
Archie Shepp and LeRoi Jones – later known as Amiri Baraka) and partly owing to the
explosive, expressionistic nature of the music itself" [4]. That is a real and documented
dimension. It is not the whole explanation — Coleman in 1963 told *Jazz* magazine he felt
indebted to Coleman [sic: to Ornette] [2], and the musical reasons are visible in the
records themselves.

The **economic** condition is the half that is usually left out. Free jazz "was
unpopular and didn't sell well" [4]. Its first audience was not a market, it was
**independent labels and European festivals**: Transition (New York), Contemporary, Prestige,
Blue Note, Impulse!, ESP-Disk (a tiny label created to spread the international language of
Esperanto, which Albert Ayler's *Spiritual Unity* in 1964 turned into "a major source for
avant-garde jazz" [5]), the French **BYG Actuel** (founded March 1967 by Jean
Georgakarakos, Jean-Luc Young and Fernand Boruso, defunct 1973, which "invited American
free jazz musicians to Paris to record in the summer of 1969, a time when they were
receiving little support or attention in the United States") [6], the Italian
**Black Saint/Soul Note** (Black Saint founded 1975 by Giacomo Pellicciotti specifically
"to record avant-garde musicians who might not have an opportunity elsewhere"; Soul Note
followed in 1979 for artists "slightly closer to the mainstream") [7], and **India
Navigation** [8]. Meanwhile many American free jazz musicians "experienced both commercial
success and acceptance in Europe," touring and settling in France, Germany, Japan,
Scandinavia and the Netherlands [4]. **Free jazz was exported because it did not sell at
home.** Any account of it that skips the small-label economy teaches the wrong lesson about
how radical music actually gets made.

It also developed as a *network of scenes*, not a movement with a headquarters: the **AACM**
in Chicago (founded 1965 — Muhal Richard Abrams, Anthony Braxton, Roscoe Mitchell, Hamid
Drake, the Art Ensemble of Chicago), the **Black Artists Group** in St. Louis (1968–72),
**Horace Tapscott's** Pan Afrikan Peoples Arkestra and Union of God's Musicians and Artists
Ascension in Los Angeles, and a European line running through Berlin, Cologne, Amsterdam
and Milan [2][9].

## What actually changed musically (concrete)

Start from what a mainstream post-bebop hard bopper takes for granted, and take it away
one at a time. The changes were specific:

- **Form.** Earlier jazz was "built on a framework of song forms, such as twelve-bar blues
  or the 32-bar AABA popular song form with chord changes. In free jazz, however, the
  dependence on a fixed and pre-established form is often eliminated, and the role of
  improvisation is correspondingly increased" [2]. Coleman went further than most: he
  "rejected pre-written chord changes, believing that freely improvised melodic lines
  should serve as the basis for harmonic progression" [2]. The improvised *melody* became
  the top of the hierarchy; the changes came after.
- **Harmony.** "Previous jazz forms used harmonic structures, usually cycles of diatonic
  chords. When improvisation occurred, it was founded on the notes in the chords. Free
  jazz almost by definition is free of such structures" [2] — while retaining, by
  definition, the *language* of earlier jazz: diatonic, altered-dominant and blues phrases
  remain extremely common [2]. This is the part newcomers get wrong (see below). Not the
  end of harmony; the end of the score.
- **Rhythm and pulse.** Regular meter is gone but not the beat. Free jazz "retains
  pulsation and sometimes swings but without regular meter," with frequent *accelerando*
  and *ritardando* giving "an impression of rhythm that moves like a wave" [2]. On
  Coltrane's *Ascension* (1965), the pulse is doubled by an eleven-piece band and then
  abandoned in waves [2].
- **Collective improvisation.** Marked as "most often heard in early jazz and free jazz"
  by the Columbia glossary [10], and restored deliberately: Mingus's *Pithecanthropus
  Erectus* (1956) is credited with "bringing back collective improvisation in a music scene
  that had become dominated by solo improvisation" [2]. *Free Jazz* puts two bands in
  competition with each other. *Ascension* uses "free-form solo improvisation interspersed
  with sections of collective improvisation reminiscent of Coleman's *Free Jazz*" [2].
- **Timbre and technique.** "Pharoah Sanders and John Coltrane used harsh overblowing or
  other extended techniques to elicit unconventional sounds from their instruments" [2].
  On *Ascension*, Coltrane was "exploring the timbral possibilities of his instrument,
  using over-blowing to achieve multiphonic tones" [2]. Ayler pushed this furthest: his
  language "focused on the possibilities of microtonal improvisation and extended saxophone
  technique, creating squawks and honks with his instrument to achieve multiphonic
  effects," producing a sound described by Gary Giddins as so "room-filling timbre, ripe
  with opulent overtones" that it "sounds as if he is playing two or three notes at a time"
  [5].
- **Composition as energy, not as material.** Britannica's period term is the useful one:
  "**Energy music**, later called 'noise,' became an identifying label for high-energy,
  collective improvisations in which dense sound textures were created from furiously
  generated note sequences" [1]. That is a fair description of the sound, and it explains
  why the genre's own practitioners disliked the name.
- **Volume and presence.** Free jazz is not, as a rule, a quiet music. The Aylers and
  Coltrane's late records are physically loud and long. (Contrast: the near-silent ECM
  lane, which is *also* an outside move — see the glossary.)
- **Instruments.** Opened up, and not only acoustically. Sun Ra's *Heliocentric Worlds*
  and *The Magic City* placed "a musical emphasis on timbre and texture over meter and
  harmony, employing a wide variety of electronic instruments and innovative percussion
  instruments, including the electric celeste, Hammond B-3, bass marimba, harp, and
  timpani" — making Sun Ra "one of the first jazz musicians to explore electronic
  instrumentation" [2]. Ayler brought Ed Blackwell's West African talking drum and Leon
  Thomas's pygmy yodeling into the room [2]. Influences named as formative: John Cage,
  Musica Elettronica Viva, Fluxus, African, Arabic and Indian music, and — via the
  "return to the roots" argument — the non-tonal folk forms of the nineteenth century,
  field hollers, street cries and jubilees [2].

## Key figures

- **Ornette Coleman** (alto, tenor, violin) — the figure the movement is named after and
  the one who, in 1960, put two bands in opposition across a stereo field and gave the
  thing its name; Coltrane said publicly that he felt indebted to him [2].
- **Cecil Taylor** (piano) — the branch that stayed *composed*. Taylor "preferred an arcane
  system of sketches, fragments, codes, and arrows" and "did not provide his musicians
  with these scores. Instead, he played episodes on the piano, which the musicians picked
  up by ear and developed by way of improvisation" [5]. On *Unit Structures* (1966) his
  compositions were "composed almost without notated scores, devoid of conventional jazz
  meter, and harmonic progression" [2]. He was also the most physically forceful theorist
  of the piano's role in this music: the pianist is not an accompanist but the leader, and
  Mark Micchelli's analysis of his solo work treats the *gesture* — how the hand lands, not
  which pitch it takes — as the level at which his improvised music is actually organised
  [11].
- **Albert Ayler** (tenor, soprano) — the branch that went furthest into microtonality and
  multiphonics, and the one who, as Giddins puts it, could be "both cheered and ridiculed"
  for a record whose harmonic language "seems to go beyond the reach of the horn" [5].
- **John Coltrane** (tenor, soprano, flute) — the bridge figure who made the thing
  unavoidable by carrying it into the largest audience jazz had. *A Love Supreme* (Dec
  1964) is modal and composed; *Ascension* (June 1965) is eleven musicians, six horns, and
  free-form versus collective alternation. Six months apart, same leader, the whole
  argument in one pair of records [12].
- **Sun Ra** (piano, bandleader) — the composer-radical, and the sharpest critic of the
  label. Sun Ra "said repeatedly that his music was written and boasted that what he wrote
  sounded more free than what 'the freedom boys' played" [2]. He is the reason "avant-garde
  jazz" and "free jazz" cannot be treated as synonyms — see the avant-garde page.

## Inherited / handed on

**Handed on, in the visible way:**

- The **big-band return of collective improvisation** — Mingus's *Pithecanthropus Erectus*
  (1956) "contained one improvised section in a style unrelated to the piece's melody or
  chord structure" and brought collective improvisation back into a scene dominated by solo
  improvisation [2]. The New Thing and later free-jazz ensembles inherited the tactic.
- **Cecil Taylor's blueprint became the AACM's** — a Chicago school that took "composed
  material, collective improvisation and the highest possible density" as a working method
  [2], and then fed Europe via ECM and Black Saint.
- **The stereo-spanning two-band texture** survives in the Globe Unity Orchestra and in
  large-ensemble writing generally.
- **The multiphonic/overblown soprano-saxophone voice** became a permanent option; Pharoah
  Sanders carried it into spiritual jazz, Alice Coltrane into church music.
- **The extended-technique vocabulary** fed everything from Keith Tippett to Muhal Richard
  Abrams's *Blues Forever* (Black Saint, 1981) to David Murray's *Shakhill's Warrior*
  (DIW, 1992) — the last of which Giddins singles out as the record where "one musician
  came to represent the synthesis between the avant-garde and the jazz tradition" [9].
- **Europe** received the music, paid for it, and answered it: ECM founded 1969 in Munich;
  the Globe Unity Orchestra in Berlin; the Dutch and Scandinavian scenes; Derek Bailey,
  Joachim Kühn, Albert Mangelsdorff, Manfred Schoof, John Surman and Alexander von
  Schlippenbach [4][13].
- **Downstream into other genres.** Sun Ra's electronic and timbral approach fed the
  avant-garde's instrumental palette; Ayler's phrasing shaped Coltrane; the whole
  harmonic vocabulary fed the modern progressive wing (see AllMusic's "Modern Creative"
  reading, which includes Coleman, Taylor, Steve Lacy, Bill Frisell, Henry Kaiser, Tim
  Berne and John Zorn and describes the lineage as "continuing the tradition of the '50s to
  '60s free-jazz mode") [2].
- **A name that outlived its usefulness.** "Free jazz" as a term was still doing work in
  1971, when the St. Louis *Black Artists Group* recorded a track called "No Time" and a
  Chicago bandleader named an album *From Ragtime to No Time* [9] — a reminder that the
  vocabulary of the field is a live, shifting thing, not a settled taxonomy.

**Handed on as a warning, too.** The 1960s critique that abandoning familiar elements
pointed to a lack of technique [2] is the ancestor of every modern dismissal of the branch.
It was wrong, and the way it was wrong is instructive.

## What newcomers get wrong

1. **"It's atonal, so it can't mean anything."** False, and the most common error. Free
   jazz is "free of such structures" harmonically while retaining "much of the language of
   earlier jazz playing" — it is "very common to hear diatonic, altered dominant and
   blues phrases in this music" [2]. Marc Ribot's objection to the "chaos" reading is the
   one to keep: Coleman and Ayler, "although they were freeing up certain strictures of
   bebop, were in fact each developing new structures of composition" [2]. The claim is
   falsifiable and has been tested.
2. **"It's unstructured, so the musicians are just wandering."** Britannica's "energy
   music" is a description, not a diagnosis. Coleman believed improvised melody *should*
   generate the harmonic progression [2]; Taylor played his compositions on the piano and
   the band learned them by ear [5]. The form is real. It is simply not written down where
   you can see it.
3. **"Free jazz and avant-garde jazz are the same thing."** No — and the difference is
   musical, not marketing. Free jazz "retains much of the language of earlier jazz
   playing" and privileges improvisation; avant-garde jazz "emphasizes structure and
   organization by the use of composed melodies, shifting but nevertheless predetermined
   meters and tonalities, and distinctions between soloists and accompaniment" [3]. The AACM
   and Sun Ra are the counter-examples: *structured*, *composed*, and still outside. This is
   the same distinction the inside/outside thesis draws between the harmonic-formal axis and
   the composed/improvised axis [12].
4. **"Outside means marginal / it never sold."** No. *The Shape of Jazz to Come* was famous
   on arrival; plenty of inside records were obscure [12]. And free jazz sold *extremely*
   well — in France, Germany, Japan, Scandinavia and the Netherlands [4]. It sold badly in
   the United States, on small labels, and that is a market story, not a quality story.
5. **"It started after bebop ended."** It started *during* — Transition 1956, Contemporary
   1958, Atlantic 1959 [2][5]. The sequencing is a retrospective critical construction, and
   it is the foundation of the error in the next file.

## Listening notes — HONEST

If your ear is calibrated on mainstream post-bebop — say hard bop, or the Miles quintet —
free jazz is genuinely hard, and it is worth being precise about *why* rather than
pretending it is not.

The hard part is not the notes. The hard part is that **the cues your ear uses to stay
oriented are removed one at a time.** In a hard bop solo you can follow the tune, count the
bars, hear the chord turn under you, and know that the ride pattern will bring the drummer
back at the end of the bridge. That is a scaffold, and it is a real scaffold. Remove the
chord changes and your ear stops being able to predict where the music is. Remove the
regular meter and it stops being able to predict how fast. In *Free Jazz*, two bands are
improvising against each other in opposite stereo channels, so the *ensemble texture itself*
is a source of confusion: at any moment the "song" is genuinely two pieces of music
fighting. Mark Gridley's point is not decorative — Gridley holds that these musicians "had
less in common than the label suggests," and you will hear that.

The second hard part is **register, and it is not a joke**. This music can be very loud,
very dense, and very long, with instruments playing at the top of their range, screams and
shrieks in the mix, and total volume. Ayler's *Spiritual Unity* and Coltrane's *Ascension*
will ask more of you physically than most of the canon. Players in the best-known
contemporary bands are known to wear protection for Coltrane's late work. That is not a
failure of your taste; it is a fact about the music.

The third hard part is **there is no melody to hold.** A Coltrane or Mingus hard bop solo
gives you a theme with a shape you can remember. A free-jazz solo frequently gives you
twenty minutes of material organised by energy, dynamics and density rather than by
phrasing. Mark Micchelli's analysis of Cecil Taylor makes the abstract version concrete:
Taylor's improvised piano is organised around *gesture* — the shape and force of the hand's
movement — "rather than sound-based" rules, and one of his rhythmic concepts is that a
six-note phrase "may be accelerated, slowed, compressed, elongated, or even subjected to
different interpretations by different members of a group simultaneously" [11]. That is
not a description of a bad player. It is a description of a different organising principle,
and it means your ear has to be listening for motion and attack instead of for tunes.

What helps, honestly, and in order:

- **Start with the door, not the destination.** *The Shape of Jazz to Come* and *Change of
  the Century* have written melodies you can hold on to. You are being invited to notice
  that the solos go somewhere the melody did not tell them to go. That noticing is the whole
  experience, and it does not require volume tolerance.
- **Go for a *hugely* composed record first.** *Unit Structures* and *Conquistador!* are
  difficult but they are not formless; and Taylor's slower pieces give your ear something
  to hold while the density rises.
- **Start with the acoustic and small, not the electric and large.** ECM, Black Saint and
  the solo piano records are quieter, better recorded, and far less punishing.
- **Don't start with the loudest record.** If *Ascension* is your first free jazz
  experience, you will form a false opinion that will be hard to undo. Let one come to you.
- **Use the glossary, then stop using it.** Once you can hear a vamp, an ostinato and a
  head-solos-head shape, you have the terms to describe what you hear, and you no longer
  need them to enjoy it.
- **One last thing worth knowing:** you are not being asked to like *everything* in the
  branch. AllMusic's Keith Johnson draws a "Modern Creative" line that includes
  "musicians [who] may incorporate free playing into structured modes—or play just about
  anything," naming Coleman, Taylor, Steve Lacy, Bill Frisell, Henry Kaiser, Eugene
  Chadbourne, Tim Berne, John Zorn and Ray Anderson [2]. That is a spectrum, and you are
  allowed an opinion about where on it you want to sit.

## CONTESTED AND UNSURE

1. **Where it starts.** Late 1940s (Tristano, "Intuition"), mid-1950s (Taylor's
   *Jazz Advance*), late 1950s (the standard date, Britannica and Wikipedia), or 1961
   (Coleman's *Free Jazz*)? Every one of these dates is defensible for a different musician
   [1][2][3]. The banked material does not settle it. My position: the *movement* is
   late-1950s; the *practice* is at least four years older; the *name* is 1961.
2. **Is the word "free" political or metaphorical?** The civil-rights reading is
   well-sourced [2] and the Black-Power reading is explicit in New Grove [4]. But Ted Gioia
   — the source for both Wikipedia's account and much of the trade writing — is himself
   ambivalent, and the banked material does not include his fuller argument. Do not present
   the political reading as *the* explanation.
3. **The Ayler saga.** Gary Giddins writes that *Spiritual Unity* "was both cheered and
   ridiculed" and that Ayler's rejection of gestures linking him to conventional jazz
   "offended some" [5]. The Wikipedia *Spiritual Unity* page tells the origin story: Bob
   Stollman, a fan, approaching Ayler after a solo performance with a $500 advance. The
   banked material supports both the anecdote and the critical hostility; the *size* of the
   hostility is not quantified anywhere here.
4. **Was Cecil Taylor free jazz?** Wikipedia says *Jazz Advance* "showed ties to traditional
   jazz" and that his transition into free jazz came with the arrival of Jimmy Lyons and
   Sunny Murray in 1962, which "encouraged more progressive musical language, such as tone
   clusters and abstracted rhythmic figures" [2]. Giddins's narrative is warmer — the
   European trip of 1957 with Lyons and Murray "represented his most forceful incursion into
   rhythmic freedom to date" [5]. Both are the same events, weighted differently. I lean
   toward Giddins; the ambiguity is genuine.
5. **Sun Ra's genre.** Wikipedia places much of Sun Ra's 1960s output inside free jazz [2]
   while noting he insisted everything was written and that his writing was freer than the
   "freedom boys" [2]. He is the strongest evidence that the label is a poor instrument.
6. **Coltrane's debt.** In a 1963 interview with *Jazz* magazine, "Coltrane said he felt
   indebted to Coleman" [2]. The quoted sentence in the raw source contains a typo
   ("indebted to Coleman") and the *Jazz* article is not in the banked material. The claim
   is plausible and widely repeated; I have not read the primary source.
7. **The Taylor piano-and-gesture analysis.** Micchelli's reading of Taylor as organised by
   "action" rather than pitch is a scholarly interpretation with a stated method, not a
   consensus [11]. It is included here because it is the most concrete account of how a
   pianist can improvise without a score, and it is flagged as interpretation.
8. **Not banked, and therefore not asserted here:** the term "no-time playing" in the
   technical sense (players dropping and reinstating tempo mid-performance), and Dolphy's
   "wides" as a named technique, are widely used in this territory but I could not find
   either in the banked corpus. They are treated in
   [`../06-inside-outside-thesis/outside-glossary.md`](../thesis)
   with an explicit flag that the supporting source was not banked.
9. **The date on *Jazz Advance*.** The banked sources give the recording as 1956 and the
   album as "released in 1956 for Transition" [2] in one place and "*Jazz Advance* — Unit
   Structures — 1957" in Giddins's European-tour discussion [5]. Both appear; the
   discrepancy is unresolved here. I have put 1956/1957 in the table rather than pick.
