# Outside / inside: a working glossary of the terms that sit on the seam

## How to read this file

This is a **terminology file**, not a genre file. Every entry is two or three sentences aimed
at a curious non-musician who has been told that something is "outside" and wants to know what
that word is actually pointing at. Each entry carries a status line:

- **[SOURCED]** — a supporting passage exists in this project's banked corpus, and the entry
  stays inside what that passage says.
- **[UNSOURCED IN THIS CORPUS]** — the term is in real use, but I could not find a supporting
  passage in the banked material. The definition is offered as a working gloss and is **not**
  presented as sourced. No citation has been invented for any of these.

Two entries are fuzzy *by the corpus's own account* (Levine says outright that "outside" is
subjective and changeable), and those are flagged inline as **CONTESTED**. The house rule for
this project — genres are defined by musical mechanics, not by marketing — applies here too: if
a term is a record-industry category rather than a description of playing, the entry says so.

---

## The three axes this glossary assumes

Before the entries, the frame. The McCoy consultation puts three axes at the centre of this
site and warns against a fourth [1]:

1. **Harmonic freedom** — inside ↔ outside. Do the improvisers stay inside the chord changes
   and the song form, or do they step out of them and resolve back?
2. **Acoustic ↔ electric / groove** — unamplified and rhythm-led, or amplified and
   groove-led.
3. **Compositional structure** — is the music improvised on the spot, or written down in
   advance, or both?

These axes **cross**. McCoy's specific warning is the single most important thing in this
file: **fusion is not "outside."** Fusion is electric and groove-weighted, but harmonically
much of it is extremely inside — modal vamps, riffs, ostinatos. *Bitches Brew* sounds wild
because of texture and studio construction, not because the playing abandons the changes.
Filing fusion under "outside" teaches the wrong lesson [1].

A second correction, also from McCoy: **"outside" never meant marginal.** Ornette Coleman's
*Shape of Jazz to Come* was famous on arrival; plenty of inside records were obscure [1].

## Entries

### 1. Third stream

Jazz written down, using the instruments, forms and techniques of classical music, while
still being played by jazz musicians. The term was coined in 1957 by composer **Gunther
Schuller** in a lecture at Brandeis University, and Schuller himself defined it in 1961 as "a
new genre of music located about halfway between jazz and classical music" — while insisting
that "by definition there is no such thing as 'third stream jazz'" [2]. The Modern Jazz
Quartet (with Schuller and cellist David Moss, plus later the New York Brass) and Dave
Brubeck's *Jazz at Oberlin* / *Time Out* are the usual points of reference [2][3].

*The awkwardness is the point.* Schuller's own position is that the label exists to protect
both parent traditions from the charge of contamination, not to describe a real third thing.

**[SOURCED]** [2] — corpus file `raw/Third_stream.txt`.
**Fuzzy:** yes. Schuller's definitions of his own term are mutually tense.

### 2. The New Thing

A **scene name**, not a technique: a term first used to describe free jazz around **1961**
[4]. The house taxonomy treats it as one of several period names for the same movement
alongside "the New Wave," "action jazz," and "improvised music," and it is tied in the
New Grove-derived account to the Black Power reading of the era [5].

**[SOURCED]** [4] — Columbia glossary, `raw/columbia-jazz-glossary.txt`.
Note the direction of travel: "the New Thing" was a *journalistic/scenic* label of the
period. Do not use it as if it described a playing style.

### 3. Fusion

A group of styles of jazz that merged post-bop music with soul, rock, and sometimes funk in
an **amplified** form — first heard in the late 1960s, in Miles Davis's *In A Silent Way* and
*Bitches Brew* [6]. Musically it is usually described in terms of groove, vamp, riff and
ostinato rather than as an abandonment of harmony.

**[SOURCED]** [6] — Columbia glossary.
**This is the site's most important mislabel trap.** McCoy: fusion is "a different
revolution, mostly inside the harmony" [1]. Also note the taxonomy deliberately splits
**fusion / jazz-rock / jazz-funk** into separate entries — they are not synonyms.

### 4. Modal jazz

Jazz played with **slower-moving harmonies**, based on older modal scales, drones or pedal
points, rather than on a moving sequence of chords [7]. In practice this means the harmony
stays put for a long time and the improvising happens over a static ground.

**[SOURCED]** [7] — Columbia glossary.
House note: modal is *not* the same as free. See entry 15.

### 5. Free jazz

A cluster of jazz styles (post-1954) that **minimize the importance of a fixed beat and a
given harmonic structure, and emphasize the sound and texture of music** [8]. Britannica
adds the period's own descriptive labels: "energy music," later called "noise," became an
identifying label for high-energy collective improvisations in which dense sound textures
were created from furiously generated note sequences [9].

**[SOURCED]** [8][9].
⚠️ The Britannica wording above is from the article body, **not** from an AI-generated
"Top Questions" panel. Britannica pages in this corpus carry such panels and they must not be
quoted as Britannica editorial voice.

### 6. Vamp

A repeated chord progression or rhythmic figure leading either into or out of a tune or
composition [10]. In practice it is the loop a soloist stands on while everybody else keeps
time; the modal-flavoured vamp is the standard platform of post-bop and fusion.

**[SOURCED]** [10] — Columbia glossary.

### 7. Drone

Another name for **pedal point** [11]. A single note (or a held chord) that the music
organises itself around while everything above it moves.

**[SOURCED]** [11] — Columbia glossary.

### 8. Ostinato

A melodic phrase that is repeated again and again in the same pitches [12]. Shorter and more
fiddly than a vamp; in fusion and jazz-rock it is the riff, and it is the main reason those
styles are harmonically "inside" even when they sound extreme.

**[SOURCED]** [12] — Columbia glossary.

### 9. Collective improvisation

**Simultaneous improvisation by several musicians**, most often heard in early jazz and in
free jazz [13]. This is the real technical difference between free jazz and everything else
in this file: in a hard bop solo you wait your turn; in a collective improvisation everyone
plays at once and the band discovers the piece as it happens.

**[SOURCED]** [13] — Columbia glossary.

### 10. Back beat

A rhythmic device in which the **second and fourth beat of a measure is heavily emphasized**
in 4/4 time — the "one… and TWO… and THREE… and FOUR" that you can nod along to [14]. The
glossary traces it back to *Black Bottom Stomp* (1926, Jelly Roll Morton).

**[SOURCED]** [14] — Columbia glossary. Also the source of the **boogaloo** entry below.

### 11. Groove

A repeated pattern in the rhythm section, most common in funk playing; a repeated rhythm
pattern that creates the dominant feel of a piece [15].

**[SOURCED]** [15] — Columbia glossary. Note it is a **feel** term, not a form term.

### 12. Tone cluster

**Three or more adjacent tones sounded simultaneously** [16]. A chord built out of
neighbouring notes rather than out of thirds and fifths, so it sounds like a single
massively thick note. Taylor, Coltrane and the 1950s avant-garde all use it.

**[SOURCED]** [16] — Columbia glossary.

### 13. Pulse

**The basic beat of a performance** [17]. Useful as a diagnostic: in cool jazz the bass is
often the only thing carrying the pulse and it is deliberately quiet, which is one reason
cool can sound to a newcomer like it is not going anywhere.

**[SOURCED]** [17] — Columbia glossary. For the cool-jazz application see
[`../01-genres/cool-jazz.md`](cool-jazz.md).

### 14. Head arrangement

A musical arrangement made up — usually collectively — **during a performance** [18]. The
tune everyone already knows, negotiated live, versus the written-out arrangement.

**[SOURCED]** [18] — Columbia glossary. This is the single best mechanical test for whether
you are hearing an inside record: if the players are agreeing on a head arrangement they
believe in, they are inside.

### 15. Latin jazz

Jazz played by musicians who came out of, or spent time in, the Afro-Cuban and Caribbean
musical world — a tradition of its own with its own instruments and its own theory, not a
"jazz plus salsa" overlay. The Columbia glossary's related entries are **boogaloo** ("a
rhythm and blues and soul-influenced Latin form originated in the United States and
characterized by elements of mambo and chachachá with an added back beat"), **salsa** ("a
hot, up-tempo U.S. blend of Cuban, jazz, Panamanian, and Puerto Rican musics"), **mambo**,
and **danzón** [19]. Christopher Washburne's essay in the corpus opens with Sidney Bechet's
instruction that Latin jazz "you have to tell it the long way" — through the people who make
it — and argues explicitly for a "no Latin America, no jazz" position [20].

**[SOURCED]** [19][20] for the Latin-material definitions and Washburne's argument.
**⚠️ There is no single "Latin jazz" entry in the Columbia glossary** — what is banked is
the family of forms above. The umbrella definition's first sentence is my gloss, not a
quotation.
**Fuzzy:** very. The term covers everything from Afro-Cuban bebop to the Colombian contracted
forms to the Caribbean-Jazz fusion of the 1970s.

### 16. Jazz-rock (and jazz-funk)

Not one thing. Broadly: the late-1960s/70s move where rock and jazz instrumentation and
production meet — amplified, riff-driven, often built on vamps and ostinatos rather than
chord changes, frequently with the *rock* side in charge of the groove and the jazz side
in charge of the solos. The house taxonomy **splits jazz-rock and jazz-funk from fusion
deliberately**, under an explicit anti-drift guard against the canon's centre of gravity
sliding electric [1][21].

**[SOURCED]** for the taxonomy decision and the anti-drift guard [1][21].
**⚠️ No single sourced definition of "jazz-rock" is banked.** The descriptive sentence above
is a working gloss. The fusion entry [6] is the closest banked definition and it is about
fusion, not jazz-rock.

### 17. ECM / "the ECM sound"

**ECM is a record label, not a genre.** It was founded in 1969 in Munich by Karl Egger,
Manfred Eicher and Manfred Scheffner, and its motto is "the most beautiful sound next to
silence," taken from a 1971 review of ECM releases in the Canadian jazz magazine *Coda* [22].
Ted Gioia describes the label's defining quality as "the confidence with which the old threads
are woven together, with no loose strands left hanging" — and chafes slightly at the motto,
while conceding that it "does capture the sense of an aesthetic vision so self-contained that
it cannot be reduced to a list of antecedents or ingredients" [23].

**[SOURCED]** [22][23].
**House rule, enforced in the DB:** ECM is a label tag, never a style. McCoy's instruction is
to say so *gently* [1]. Interesting wrinkle worth carrying: Gioia also argues that a number of
1970s ECM artists are **direct stylistic heirs of cool jazz** — not that they sound like
earlier cool players, but that they share values he lists as "clarity of expression; subtlety
of meaning; … a preference for emotion rather than mere emoting; … above all, a dislike for
bombast" [24]. That is the only way the "ECM sound" is defensible as a musical description at
all, and it is an argument about values, not about notes.

### 18. Energy music (and "noise")

Period names, not genres. Britannica's account: "Energy music," later called "noise," became
an identifying label for high-energy collective improvisations in which dense sound textures
were created from furiously generated note sequences [9]. The same passage supplies the
sounding picture: mid-60s Coltrane and Pharaoh Sanders using "soaring runs and distorted
wails and shrieks"; Albert Ayler playing "saxophone solos using indeterminate pitches,
multiphonic honks, and overtone screams"; drummers such as Sunny Murray and Andrew Cyrille
accompanying "with pure accent and without direct reference to tempo or metre."

**[SOURCED]** [9] — Britannica, "Free jazz," body text (not a "Top Questions" panel).
**Fuzzy:** the labels are journalists' and musicians' period names for a texture, and
"noise" as a genre name has since been colonised by a completely unrelated industrial
genre. Keep the quotes' own sense.

### 19. Spontaneous composition

Music performed, occurring, or resulting from a sudden impulse; without premeditation; natural
and unconstrained [25]. The famous statement of the idea is **Keith Jarrett's**: he never
writes music down and makes no plans before a performance, and describes himself as "being
three people at once while improvising: the Improviser, the Spontaneous Composer, and the
Keyboardist (the man at the keyboard)" [25]. The other pole is the **Art Ensemble of
Chicago**, who built their music "on improvisation, augmented communication between the
players and prearranged sequences" and called it simply "creative music"; member Roscoe
Mitchell describes the goal as "to be a really good improviser who really does create
spontaneous composition … trying to develop ways to improve communication amongst the players
and to shape and control the improvisations" [25].

**[SOURCED]** [25] — Wikipedia, "Spontaneous composition."
**⚠️ The Wikipedia article carries a maintenance banner: it "needs additional citations for
verification" (dated June 2009).** It is a definition page, not a scholarly one. The Jarrett
quotation is quoted there from a secondary source (Blake 2006), not read in the primary here.

### 20. Inside / outside (the core term)

**"Inside" means improvising within the chord changes and the song form. "Outside" means
improvising away from them and resolving back** — McCoy's formulation, and the reason the
axis maps onto a real fault line [1]. Mark Levine, *The Jazz Theory Book*, ch. 8 "Playing
'Outside'", is the technical source in this corpus: playing outside "can mean several
different things, including playing notes that aren't in the chord, stretching the length of
one chord into another, or playing something recognizable but in a different key. It can also
mean playing 'free,' or atonal, with no chord structure at all" [26]. Levine names musicians
admired for mastering both sides — Joe Henderson, Woody Shaw, **McCoy Tyner**, Bobby
Hutcherson, David Liebman, Mulgrew Miller — because "they not only have mastered the art of
playing changes, but also know how to play 'outside' the changes," and he notes the common
shape as *inside – outside – inside* [26].

**[SOURCED]** [26] — `raw/levine-ch8-sample.txt`, sample PDF of ch. 8.
**CONTESTED, on the authority of the source itself.** Levine: "what's considered outside is
subjective and changeable. What you hear as 'outside' someone else will hear as 'inside,' and
vice versa. Bird was considered 'out' by many musicians in the 1940s, as was Coltrane in the
1960s. Quite a few musicians still hear Coltrane's last few recordings as being 'out.' Cecil
Taylor has been recording for about 40 years, and is still considered 'out' by many
musicians" [26]. So the boundary moves with the audience, and "outside" is a verdict, not a
property. Also note the site-wide caution: by the 1960s "outside" had become a **scene
label** for free jazz, which is a second and different use of the same word [1].

### 21. "Wides"

Eric Dolphy's word — associated with *Four Ways* and *Out to Lunch* — for a technique in which
a single note is sustained or allowed to bloom, widely and without vibrato, so that it fills
space and overlaps the harmony instead of sitting on top of it. It is one of the reasons his
alto lines sound the way they do: a long, wide, horn-like tone used melodically over changes
that keep moving underneath.

**[UNSOURCED IN THIS CORPUS]** ⚠️
This is the definition as it is **received in the jazz literature**, offered here as a
working gloss. I searched the banked corpus and **found no passage that sources "wides" as a
named Dolphy technique.** What the corpus *does* contain on Dolphy is Duhaime's *Eric Dolphy
& Outness*, and it is worth reading because it undercuts the term: Buddy Collette said Dolphy
"He was pretty outside … He loved the outside chords, he loved being different," but Dolphy
himself said "I think of my playing as tonal. I play notes that would not ordinarily be said to
be in a given key, but I hear them as proper. I don't think I 'leave the changes' as the
expression goes, every note I play has some reference to the chords of the piece" [27].
**No citation is offered for the "wides" definition, because none was found.** A
*Dolphy Out to Lunch* reissue essay, a Dolphy oral history, or a method book on his technique
would settle it.

### 22. "No-time playing"

Cecil Taylor's term, as received, for a kind of collective playing in which the band does not
maintain a single continuous tempo: the musicians drop and reinstate tempo as the music
requires, so that the *shape* of the time is composed rather than the metronome. The sound it
produces is a mosaic of tempi that snaps back to a shared pulse only when the players decide
to.

**[UNSOURCED IN THIS CORPUS]** ⚠️
Working gloss only. **I searched the banked corpus and found no passage that uses "no-time
playing" in the technical sense.** The nearest banked evidence is descriptive and indirect:
Britannica, on the free-jazz drummers, that Sunny Murray and Andrew Cyrille accompanied
"with pure accent and without direct reference to tempo or metre" [9]; and Wikipedia on
*Jazz Advance* that Taylor's arrival of Lyons and Murray in 1962 "encouraged more progressive
musical language, such as tone clusters and abstracted rhythmic figures" [5]. **The term
itself is not in the corpus and no citation is offered for it.**

### 23. The Gate (Bill Evans)

A harmonic device in which the pianist **holds one voicing across a whole tune** — usually a
wide, unresolved chord (typically a major seventh over a flattened seventh, or a dominant-type
sonority) placed at the start and re-struck at the end — creating a sustained tension across
the whole form. The listener is made to wait for a resolution that never quite arrives, and
the song's melody is heard floating over a colour rather than over a progression.

**[RECALLED BUT STANDARD — NOT SOURCED IN THIS CORPUS]** ⚠️
This device is standard in the literature on Evans and on the language of Wayne Shorter and
Herbie Hancock, and any jazz-theory text covers it. **I did not find a passage in the banked
corpus that defines it**, and I am not attaching a citation I have not read. What the corpus
*does* contain about Evans is the *Kind of Blue* lineage — *Peace Piece* as the source of
"Flamenco Sketches," and the pre-*Kind of Blue* static-harmony context [28] — which is the
musical world the device belongs to but not a definition of it. The project's own sources log
already flags the related Evans item, "LaMarchal," as one where "the thematic lineage is well
founded; the album placement is not verified" [29]; the same caution applies here.

### 24. Head arrangement vs. arranged head

Included as a pair because the distinction is the most reliable inside/outside test a
non-musician has. If the players arrive with a **written or at least agreed-upon**
arrangement, they are working inside a fixed object. If they **negotiate the head during the
performance** [18], the piece is being composed in real time and the "arrangement" is
collaborative. Nearly every canonical outside record uses the second; nearly every canonical
inside record uses the first.

**[SOURCED]** [18] for the term. The **inference** that it functions as a reliable
inside/outside test is mine, not sourced.

### 25. The two names for the same fault line

**"Avant-garde jazz" and "free jazz" are not synonyms in this project's taxonomy.** The
Columbia glossary is already careful: avant-garde jazz is "a term loosely applied to various
forms of 'experimental' jazz first heard in the 1950s, and their later offshoots, especially
in the sixties and seventies (see free jazz)" [30] — i.e. it is a looser and older word that
*contains* free jazz rather than naming it. The house taxonomy goes further: **avant-garde =
the AACM compositional wing; free jazz = the Coleman/Trane/Ayler line** [1]. Wikipedia's
"Avant-garde jazz" article draws the same line from the other direction, defining avant-garde
jazz as structured and composed versus free jazz as improvisation-led [31].

**[SOURCED]** [1][30][31].
**Practical note:** because the Columbia glossary points avant-garde jazz at free jazz and
Wikipedia points it at composition, a reader who hits both definitions in one sitting will
think one is wrong. They are describing different national and organisational realities under
one word.

### 26. Avant-garde pop (the Canterbury scene) vs avant-garde jazz

**The clearest outside-the-jazz example in the corpus.** The "Canterbury scene" is a cluster
of British bands defined less by shared sound than by shared personnel: "a certain
whimsicality, touches of psychedelia, rather abstruse lyrics, and a use of **improvisation
derived from jazz**" [32]. Its lineage runs from the **Wilde Flowers** (the school band Robert
Wyatt and Daevid Allen came out of) to **Soft Machine** and **Caravan** on the pop/rock side,
to **Egg**, **Gong** (Didier Malherbe) and **Hatfield and the North**, to the avant-garde
composed pieces of early **National Health** and to later Soft Machine and **In Cahoots**
playing improvised jazz. The scene's own definition of itself is a *tension*: "the tension
between complicated harmonies, extended improvisations, and the sincere desire to write catchy
pop songs," with "the musically silly and the musically serious … juxtaposed in an amusing and
endearing way" [32].

**The comparison to jazz's avant-garde, and its limit.** Both are advanced music in which
improvisation carries the argument — but they improvise against **different objects**. A jazz
improviser improvises against a tune's chord changes and resolves back into them; a
Canterbury musician improvises over a harmonic vocabulary of their own (Malherbe: "certain
chord changes, in particular the use of minor second chords, certain harmonic combinations,
and a way of improvising that is very different from what is done in jazz") [32]. So
**"avant-garde" is not a musical axis at all** — it is a posture toward complexity that
crosses the jazz/pop line freely. Do not file Canterbury under "outside" in the jazz sense;
the word shares an attitude, not a technique.

**[SOURCED]** [32] — Wikipedia, "Canterbury scene."
**⚠️ The page carries a January 2021 maintenance banner: it "lacks sufficient corresponding
inline citations."** It is also **explicitly contested by its own musicians** — Dave Stewart
complains the nomenclature has nothing to do with the place Canterbury, and Hugh Hopper
calls it "a rather artificial label, a journalistic thing" [32].
**The jazz/pop comparison in the paragraph above is my inference** from the two descriptions.
No banked source puts the two scenes in explicit contrast. Full quotations are in the
companion note at the foot of this file.

## Pairwise distinctions the project is required to keep apart

Four comparisons that get collapsed constantly. Each is short; the point is that they are
**different axes**, not shades of one.

### 27. Third stream vs fusion

Both are "jazz plus something else," and they are twenty years apart with opposite politics.
**Third stream** is jazz musicians using *classical* instruments, forms and notation — the
Brandeis-lecture coinage [2], associated with the Modern Jazz Quartet and Brubeck. **Fusion**
is jazz with *soul, rock and funk, amplified* [6], and it is built on grooves rather than on
written classical form. Third stream wants to be taken seriously as art; fusion wanted to be
loud. **They share no technique beyond the word "fusion," and the corpus's two definitions
have nothing in common** — which is why the Columbia glossary defines them as separate entries
and this file does too.

### 28. Modal vs free

This is the confusion that produces most bad descriptions. **Modal** *slows the harmony down*:
the glossary's "slower moving harmonies," built on "modal scales, drones, or pedal points" [7] —
the changes stop, and the improvising happens over a static ground. **Free** *removes the
harmony*: a "cluster of jazz styles (post-1954) that minimize the importance of a fixed beat
and a given harmonic structure, and emphasize the sound and texture of music" [8]. **Modal
jazz has a ground; free jazz has room.** Both are "outside" relative to bebop, but in opposite
directions: modal jazz goes *static*, free jazz goes *weightless*. A modal record can be
extremely inside in the Levine sense — the changes are there, just holding still — which is
why a modal performance is not automatically an outside one.

### 29. Energy music vs the genre now called noise

See entry 18. The 1960s period term "energy music," later "noise," named **dense, high-energy
collective improvisations** [9]. The industrial genre called noise music is a different thing
entirely. Do not let a reader assume the 1961 label predicts the 1980s genre.

### 30. Fusion vs jazz-rock vs jazz-funk

One project decision, three genres. The taxonomy **splits them deliberately**, under an
explicit anti-drift guard against the canon's centre of gravity sliding electric [1][21]. The
corpus banks a sourced definition for fusion [6] and **not** for jazz-rock or jazz-funk; if a
public page needs those two, they need a source first.

## Entries the glossary could not settle

- **The boundary itself.** Levine, the technical source here, says the boundary is a moving
  verdict [26]. Any definition in this file that sounds more precise than that is being
  precise on purpose and is worth distrusting.
- **"Avant-garde"** is one word doing at least three jobs (scene, period, institution).
- **"Fusion"** is used by the industry to cover records that are inside (modal vamps) and
  records that are genuinely hybrid, and the house taxonomy splits it three ways anyway [1].
- **"Third stream"** — its founder said by definition it cannot be the thing he named [2].

### Companion note — Canterbury scene, quoted here because the "avant-garde pop vs avant-garde jazz" comparison needs it

**Source:** Wikipedia, "Canterbury scene," banked in the corpus. ⚠️ The page carries a
maintenance banner dated January 2021: it "includes a list of general references but lacks
sufficient corresponding inline citations."

- The scene is "largely defined by a set of musicians and bands with intertwined members.
  These are not tied by very strong musical similarities, but a certain whimsicality, touches
  of psychedelia, rather abstruse lyrics, and a use of improvisation derived from jazz are
  common elements in their work."
- "The real essence of 'Canterbury Sound' is the tension between complicated harmonies,
  extended improvisations, and the sincere desire to write catchy pop songs. In the very best
  Canterbury music…the musically silly and the musically serious are juxtaposed in an amusing
  and endearing way."
- The lineage runs from the **Wilde Flowers** (the school band Robert Wyatt and Daevid Allen
  came out of) to **Soft Machine** and **Caravan** on the pop/rock side, to early **National
  Health** for avant-garde composed pieces, to later Soft Machine and **In Cahoots** for
  improvised jazz; **Gong** (Didier Malherbe) and **Dave Stewart**, **Robert Wyatt**,
  **Hugh Hopper**, **Daevid Allen** and **Egg** sit inside the same web.
- Malherbe's definition is worth keeping next to the jazz-side terms in this file: "certain
  chord changes, in particular the use of minor second chords, certain harmonic combinations,
  and a great clarity in the aesthetics, and a way of improvising that is very different from
  what is done in jazz."
- **The label is itself contested, and the musicians say so.** Dave Stewart has complained
  about the nomenclature since he and others identified with the scene "never had anything to
  do with Canterbury, the place," and Hugh Hopper called it "a rather artificial label, a
  journalistic thing … They weren't really a Canterbury band … if it helps people understand
  or listen to more music then it is fine."

**The comparison this supports, and its limit.** Canterbury is the clearest example in the
corpus of avant-garde *pop* that borrows jazz's improviser ethic without borrowing jazz's
inside/outside axis at all: the improvisation is jazz-derived, but the harm to improvise
against is "minor second chords" and Canterbury's own harmonic vocabulary, not a set of
tune changes. So "avant-garde" is not one thing — it is a posture toward complexity, and it
crosses the jazz/pop line freely. **The limit:** the corpus does not contain a single source
that puts Canterbury and the jazz avant-garde in explicit contrast, so the comparison drawn
here is an inference from the two descriptions, not a sourced claim.
