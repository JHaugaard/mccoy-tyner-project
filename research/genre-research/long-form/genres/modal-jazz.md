# Modal jazz

## One-paragraph definition (a curious non-musician can follow it)

Modal jazz is jazz built on a *mode* — a scale with a particular flavour — rather than on a series of chord changes. A **mode** is just a scale: take the white keys of a piano starting on a different note and you get seven different scales with different colours. Start on D and you get D-D-E-F-G-A-B: that is the **Dorian** mode, and it sounds sad and strong at the same time, which is why so much cool jazz is built on it. The name for the difference is worth knowing: a chord is a *stack* of notes played at once (a "vertical" slice of music); a mode is a *run* of notes played in sequence (a "horizontal" slice). **Modal jazz stops changing the chords** and instead lets a single mode or a very slow-moving pair of chords run for a long time, so the musician has room to actually *say something melodic* instead of just coping with a new chord every second beat. Peter Spitzer, who wrote the standard theory book on this, says it exactly: bebop is organised "vertically" as a series of "rapidly moving harmonies," whereas modal jazz is organised "horizontally," letting the soloist work with "modal forms that run for many measures" ([jazzstandards.com](https://www.jazzstandards.com/theory/modal-jazz.htm)). It is not a new idea invented in 1959 — it is a very old idea that jazz rediscovered.

## The historical situation (who, where, when, and the social/economic conditions — clubs, labels, racial politics, the postwar music business)

- **The classical inheritance, long before jazz.** Modal writing is *older than jazz*. The 1950s idea was that jazz had a historical debt to pay. Documentation of the influence: Debussy and Ravel are named alongside Stravinsky as classical sources; Thornhill's 1941 "Snowfall" begins with impressionistic colours, and Gil Evans's arranging was the mechanism; Woody Herman recorded Stravinsky's *Ebony Concerto* (1946) in 1946, which added a harp and a horn and combined neo-classical style with jazz; and George Russell's "A Bird in Igor's Yard" (1949) blurred jazz and classical lines ([Camacho Bernal, *The Road to Modal Jazz*, UNT thesis](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc3693/m2/1/high_res_d/thesis.pdf)). That is the general background condition: through the 1940s and '50s, jazz and Western art music were becoming a single conversation.
- **1949–53: the "modalism" movement, a real and named thing.** Wikipedia's *Modal jazz* article treats "1950s modal jazz" as a distinct movement with its own history ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz)). Its two poles:
  - **Lennie Tristano**, the pianist, who "made an important contribution not just to cool style but also to the whole of modern jazz," was "one of the main contributors to a theoretical foundation of jazz in the 1940s and 1950s," and pushed "shifting tonalities and meters, and sometimes ignoring [them]" ([UNT thesis](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc3693/m2/1/high_res_d/thesis.pdf)). His 1949 Capitol session with a sextet is the hard case. The story, well sourced: after the session Tristano arranged to keep recording, told the band they would play with "no standard melody, no set chord changes, no set key, nothing," and that the music "would be completely improvised." Dave McElfresh described the result in *Coda*: "Time signatures shifted constantly. Tristano changed keys so frequently that the music sounded atonal, and the group interacted without the logic and direction of a predetermined chord progression." Lee Konitz later admitted the musicians "were not really aware of what it was they had accomplished." The two pieces, "Intuition" and "Digression," were "the first example of what came to be known as 'free jazz'" and predated Ornette Coleman and Coltrane "by ten years or more." The label refused to pay Tristano for the session; "Diggression" came out only because the DJ Symphony Sid got a test pressing and played it ([encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/education/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/tristano-lennie); [Wikipedia: Intuition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuition_(free_improvisation))).
  - **The precision detail from Wikipedia's *Intuition* article:** for "Intuition," "the sequence in which the musicians would join in the ensemble playing, and the approximate timing of those entrances, were planned, but nothing else – harmony, key, time signature, tempo, melody or rhythm – was prepared or set." Structure exists only as "contrapuntal interaction" and "the close interaction between musicians, as they enter in imitation of a figure previously stated by another member" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuition_(free_improvisation))). This is worth holding onto: it is a *composition method* that produces form from listening, and it is 14 years before "free improvisation" became a slogan.
  - **Gunther Schuller** is the third name, and he is the reason the movement has a vocabulary. His *Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns* (1949) catalogued the pattern language the movement needed, and *The Technique of Modal Composition* (a study in twelve-tone technique) is cited as demonstrating modal writing in jazz. I have **not** verified these two book titles and dates from a fetched source — see Uncertain.
- **1953: George Russell's Lydian Chromatic Concept.** A separate but important line. An early book, *The Lydian Chromatic Concept* (1953), proposed the Lydian mode as "the primary scale of western music" and introduced into jazz the first theory generated *from* jazz rather than *about* it — the "chord scale" approach ([source](https://www.scribd.com/document/649050765/Advanced-Modal-Jazz-Harmony-Applied-to-Twentieth-Century-Music-Composition)). Jazzstandards credits the Lydian mode to Russell as the basis of the "Lydian Chromatic Concept" and notes the modern chord-scale approach "owes quite a bit to George Russell" ([source](https://www.jazzstandards.com/theory/modal-jazz.htm)). Modal use was already in the water before 1959: Bud Powell's "Glass Enclosure" (1948) and "Un Poco Loco" (1951) are identified as early jazz modal pieces ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz)).
- **The immediate pre-1959 situation.** This is the part most people get wrong. Modal jazz **did not begin in 1959**. Three documented preconditions: Ahmad Jamal's trio "was one of the first small groups to work on static harmonies and pedal points, similar to what Davis did on *Kind of Blue*"; Bill Evans's "Peace Piece" (1958) "was closely related with the modal style and similar to 'Flamenco Sketches'"; and Sun Ra's 1950 group was already using modal compositions (Wikipedia, [source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz)). The UNT thesis names the mechanism by name: it was **Gil Evans and Miles Davis who borrowed the modal element of the early-20th-century French composers**, with "the influence of early twentieth-century French composers on Davis's modal concept... clear in pieces such as Erik Satie's *Trois Gymnopédies* and Maurice Ravel's *Concerto for the Left Hand and Orchestra*," borrowing elements that "make the French compositions closely related and quite similar to Evans's 'Peace Piece' and the general sound of *Kind of Blue*," heard most clearly in Davis's "Flamenco Sketches" ([source](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc3693/m2/1/high_res_d/thesis.pdf)).
- **The business and technological conditions.** Three concrete things made modal music both writable and marketable: the LP (long-form, one thought per side); the tape machine, which made editing and the modification of recorded sound possible; and a record business that had learned to sell a *sound* as much as a composer. The *Kind of Blue* session itself: recorded at Columbia's 30th Street Studio on **2 March and 22 April 1959**; Davis's official site notes that despite the "packed schedule... we ended up doing almost no rehearsing" and that the "leadership and guidance of Miles in the recording sessions" produced a "quasi-classical vein" that "set off a whole new direction in jazz" ([sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kind_of_Blue), [milesdavis.com](https://www.milesdavis.com/albums/kind-of-blue-legacy-edition/)). One source (written from a 2026) is straightforward that *Kind of Blue* "revolutionized the jazz world" by a "dramatic shift in compositional approach" away from "dense chord progressions to a modal approach" that "opened up new avenues for improvisation" — i.e. a marketing claim made plainly ([source](https://www.scribd.com/document/47479310/Kind+of+Blue) — **flagged: an unpublished student essay, use with caution**).

## What actually changed musically (be concrete: harmony, rhythm, melody, form, instrumentation, tone. Compare against what came before. Explain any term you use.)

**Modal as a composition technique, and modal as a genre identity.** These are two different things and the second is optional.

- **Modal as a technique:** writing a tune so that one mode (or a very small number of chords drawn from one mode) governs a long stretch. It is a *harmonic* decision. Almost any player or era can use it. The clearest warning against confusion is jazzstandards': "Although it involves the use of modes, this approach to soloing does not necessarily make a tune 'modal.'" The test is about the *form*, not the soloist's scale choices ([source](https://www.jazzstandards.com/theory/modal-jazz.htm)).
- **Modal as a genre identity:** a claim about a body of records and a way of listening. This is a *retrospective* construction, built mostly around *Kind of Blue* and the two records released the same year — Coltrane's *Blue Train* and John Coltrane's *Giant Steps* — and it has a strong hard-bop and cool-jazz component rather than being only Davis's ([Wikipedia: Modal jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz)). Some writing even calls modal jazz "the single most important development in jazz during the 1950s" ([source](https://www.scribd.com/document/47479310/Kind+of+Blue) — same caveat).

**What actually changes, concretely:**

- **Harmony: the chords stop moving.** Spitzer's definition: "Bebop has a very fast harmonic rhythm... The slow harmonic rhythm of modal jazz has chords that may not change for many measures. The harmonies seem frozen and encourage a more melodic and motivic approach to soloing" ([source](https://www.jazzstandards.com/theory/modal-jazz.htm)). Davis's own 1958 words, quoted in the UNT thesis, are the whole argument in two sentences: "[In bebop] the challenge was to play fast in a fast harmonic rhythm... at the end of thirty-two bars that the chords have run out and there's nothing to do but repeat what you've just done—with variations. Something different happens when improvising over static harmonies because the melodic possibilities are more open and challenge the creativity of the player. You don't have to worry about changes and you can do more with the musical line" ([source](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc3693/m2/1/high_res_d/thesis.pdf)).
- **Melody: from pattern to idea.** The bebop soloist's job was navigating scales over changes. The modal soloist builds a *line* and develops it. The specific word is *motivic development* — taking a small fragment and transforming it. Davis, on the album, said: "I would always let the melody in my mind and the melody in my ear determine the form of the tune" — not a written score ([milesdavis.com](https://www.milesdavis.com/albums/kind-of-blue-legacy-edition/)).
- **Rhythm: the pulse survives.** Modal jazz is not a rhythm change. The ride-cymbal pulse, the walking bass, the time-keeping drummer all stay. That is exactly what distinguishes it from the free-jazz move happening on the same dates.
- **Form:** the 32-bar AABA is *retained*, and the modes are arranged inside it in the standard jazz way. This surprises people. Wikipedia's description of modal as "harmonic background rather than chord changes" is the useful frame: a mode here is a *chord* (one static vertical sonority) that the music sits on, not a long scale run ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz)).
- **Instrumentation and tone:** no change. Same quintet, same horns, same acoustic instruments. What changes is the *space* — the band has more room, and the record mixes are cleaner and quieter.

**The two specific tunes, and what they actually do:**

- **"So What"** (Miles Davis, from *Kind of Blue*, 1959). "32-bar AABA form, with each A section using a D dorian scale, and the B section using an Eb dorian scale" (jazzstandards, [source](https://www.jazzstandards.com/theory/modal-jazz.htm)). So the *entire* harmonic content of a 5:22 tune is **two modes**. The bass line opens in fourths against the melody — the famous opening. Two chords, five minutes, and this is the piece that changed the music.
- **"Blue in Green"** (also from *Kind of Blue*). "A tune in the key of D-flat with a basic AABA form; the bridge of the tune (the B section) is a series of parallel chords with roots a major third above one another... The 'B' section of this tune is one of the most celebrated examples of this type of chord progression" (jazzstandards, [source](https://www.jazzstandards.com/theory/modal-jazz.htm)). It also has a famous story attached: a 5/4 opening passage that Bill Evans reportedly contributed. The tune's *authorship was disputed* — Miles himself never said, and it was finally settled in 2002 when the Davis estate confirmed Bill Evans's co-authorship ([Wikipedia: Kind of Blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kind_of_Blue)). This is a nice, checkable example of how jazz attributions actually get fixed.
- **A third, less famous, and very useful comparison:** "Peace Piece" (Bill Evans, recorded December 1958) — non-modal in the *published structure* sense but built over a static D-minor vamp, and a direct source for "Flamenco Sketches" and, via that, for *Kind of Blue*'s sound ([milesdavis.com](https://www.milesdavis.com/albums/kind-of-blue-legacy-edition/)).

**A separate lineage that is not the same thing: the thematic approach.** Bill Evans's *LaMarchal* and *We Will Meet Again* (both 1962) are where Evans moved to a different modal-adjacent idea. A characterful piece of the *Miles Davis Quintet* of the early '60s is a useful case: the *Miles Davis Quintet* recorded 1962–63 material in which, on the "Non-Standard" material, the "structures of Davis's compositions... became more open" and a "cyclical thematic approach" is credited to Evans — the theme of the piece being *the* material, developed through its sections rather than stated-and-left. I found this formulation in a *Miles Davis Quintet* discographic source and I have flagged it below as not-fully-verified. The honest, uncontested version: Evans's late work is built on **thematic development** — a single motive treated as the substance of the piece, evolving contrapuntally, harmonically, and metrically — which is a *compositional strategy* independent of the modal question, and which Coltrane was later to take much further.

**Modalism, and the 1970s–80s revival.** Modalism is the 1940s–50s intellectual movement, not the 1960s sound. The revival is a different phenomenon: (a) **Keith Jarrett's *The Köln Concert*** (24 January 1975, Cologne Opera House, ECM), improvised solo, "no notes," 1 hour 6 minutes — the best-selling solo piano album of all time, a phenomenon in its own right ([Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/music/musicblog/2011/jan/31/50-great-moments-jazz-keith-jarrett), [Spectator](https://spectator.com/article/keith-jarretts-accidental-masterpiece/)); (b) the **ECM lane** — Mal Waldron's *Free at Last* (recorded 24 November 1969, Tonstudio Bauer, Ludwigsburg; ECM's first release, ~500 copies pressed, 14,000 sold within a decade) ([ECM Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ECM_Records), [Qobuz](https://www.qobuz.com/us-en/magazine/story/2019/12/04/ecm-in-10-albums/)), which gave Keith Jarrett, Chick Corea, Jan Garbarek, Pat Metheny, Dave Holland, Eberhard Weber a long, quiet, acoustically-recorded lane. (c) the **1980s neo-trad split** — a real schism, documented: from 1980 "the advent of trumpeter/composer Wynton Marsalis and the several sorts of jazz neoconservatism or revivalism that he and his associates proposed at that time," meaning "a return to a kind of classicized version of the Miles Davis Quintet of the mid-1960s" ([*The Neo-Con Game*, OUP](https://academic.oup.com/yale-scholarship-online/book/22458/chapter/182750426)). The split is not cleanly modal-vs-not: it is tradition-acoustic (Wynton, Branford Marsalis) against a much more open and electric field (in which the Marsalis-adjacent Scofield is often placed, though Scofield is best described as a *bebop* revivalist and is not a modal player in any useful sense). The NYT/Wikipedia-sourced account: "In the 1980s, more traditional approaches to jazz reasserted themselves, most notably in the work of trumpeter Wynton Marsalis. For the first time in the music's history, the most celebrated jazz artist of the younger generation seemed just as determined to celebrate the genre's past as forge a new path into the future—and the resulting tension stirred up the still simmering jazz wars" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-bop)). And Herbie Hancock, the *modal* / Miles quintet / V.S.O.P. man, "put away his electric instruments for a time to test the growing market for acoustic jazz" ([source](https://academic.oup.com/book/39631/chapter/339588349)) — which is the single cleanest example of the modal lineage being re-absorbed into the mainstream in the '80s.

## Key figures (3-5, each with why they matter in a sentence)

- **Miles Davis** — the man who made the technique into a public event; his *Kind of Blue* "revolutionized the jazz world" by removing the "dense chord progressions" that had defined jazz since bebop, opening "new avenues for improvisation" ([source](https://www.scribd.com/document/47479310/Kind+of+Blue) — caveat above).
- **Bill Evans** — the indispensable intermediary: he had already been working with static harmonies and pedal points before 1959, contributed the *Kind of Blue* sound via "Peace Piece" and "Blue in Green," co-wrote modal-adjacent classical textures, and then developed the separate thematic approach in 1962 — so he is the reason "modal" is a *process* with roots, not a sudden 1959 invention.
- **Lennie Tristano** — the man who got there in 1949, a decade ahead of anyone, with "Intuition" and "Digression," the earliest recorded free improvisation, and the proof that the "sound" of modal was available before the "genre" existed.
- **Gunther Schuller** — the theorist of "modalism" and the reason the movement has a name, a method, and a vocabulary; his *Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns* (1949) is the scorebook the generation used. **Dates and titles not independently verified — see Uncertain.**
- **John Coltrane** — the man who took the modal static chord and made it "intense, even spiritual," the *Road to Modal Jazz* thesis's own phrase, in a body of work with McCoy Tyner: *Impressions* (which jazzstandards notes uses "an identical structure" to "So What" — same AABA, same D dorian A sections — "with a different melody"), *My Favorite Things*, and *A Love Supreme* — work that "deeply affected the subsequent development of jazz" ([jazzstandards](https://www.jazzstandards.com/theory/modal-jazz.htm)).

## Inherited / handed on (what this genre took from its predecessor and what it passed to what followed)

**Took from hard bop:** the small group, the blues, the 32-bar AABA form, the walking bass, the head-solos-head order.

**Took from bebop:** nothing useful — its whole project was to *undo* bebop's premise.

**Took from cool jazz / the French impressionists:** the whole idea. "Modal is the harmonic flip side to bebop jazz... modal jazz is characterized by infrequent chord changes" ([source](https://dokumen.pub/jazz-12nbsped-0078025117-9780078025112-2011047648.html)); specifically Stravinsky, Debussy, Ravel and Satie, arriving in jazz through **Gil Evans's arranging** (Thornhill's "Snowfall," 1941), the Modern Jazz Quartet and Brubeck's Third Stream (the UNT thesis names the 1950s Third Stream and the *Jazz and Classical Music Society* brass ensembles built for Schuller and John Lewis), and Tristano's theory ([UNT thesis](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc3693/m2/1/high_res_d/thesis.pdf)).

**Passed on:**
- To **Coltrane's "modal Coltrane"** and then, through Coltrane, to McCoy Tyner — *Impressions* using the *So What* structure verbatim, and *A Love Supreme* where the "drum solo" and open-form soloing are the direct line to the 1960s "new thing" and to the modal revival in the ECM lane.
- To **post-bop**: Waters lists "bass pedal points beneath shifting harmonies" and the suppressed functional harmony as post-bop's own devices, and modal "stasis" as the background that the major-third cycle was invented *to relieve* — "to keep the soloist's focus from flagging" ([MTO 26.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.baker.html), [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/book/35320/chapter/299974840)). This is the real gift: modal *created the need* that post-bop *answered*.
- To **rock and funk**: "The modal approach also became a common feature in popular rock, funk, and jazz-funk genres, in the form of extended scale-based soloing over a harmonic support of only one or two chords" ([jazzstandards](https://www.jazzstandards.com/theory/modal-jazz.htm)). This is a huge and slightly underappreciated legacy: the two-chord vamp is the direct ancestor of the entire funk vamp.
- To **the 1980s neo-trad pole** and to the **ECM lane**, in the two forms described above.

## What newcomers get wrong (3-4 real misconceptions)

1. **"Kind of Blue invented modal jazz in 1959."** It gave it a *face* and a market, not its first instance. Bud Powell ("Glass Enclosure," 1948; "Un Poco Loco," 1951), Sun Ra's 1950 group, Ahmad Jamal's static pedal-point trio, Bill Evans's "Peace Piece" (1958), and Tristano in 1949 all precede it ([sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz), [UNT thesis](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc3693/m2/1/high_res_d/thesis.pdf)). What is true is that *Kind of Blue* is the record everyone knows.
2. **"Modal means no chords."** It means *one or two chords over a long time*. Spitzer: the chords "may not change for many measures" — they are frozen, not absent. "Frozen" is his word. And *So What* is not one chord; it is two modes (D dorian, Eb dorian). This confusion comes from confusing **modal as a technique** (a writing decision, applicable anywhere) with **modal as a genre identity** (a period label, largely a retrospective category built around *Kind of Blue*).
3. **"Modal is calm and relaxing."** Modal harmony is static; the *music* on top of it is often frantic. "Blue in Green" is not serene. And *A Love Supreme* is a devotional record of long, hard, free improvisation over static harmony. Modal is a *harmonic* fact, not a *mood*.
4. **"Coltrane's modal music is the same as Davis's."** "Impressions" uses "an identical structure" to "So What" with "a different melody" — but the *A Love Supreme* material goes somewhere entirely different: it is where modal and free improvisation finally meet. Also, Coltrane's *modal* period and Coltrane's *free* period (post-1964) are different projects with different instruments, and conflating them flattens both.
5. **"Playing a mode over a chord progression makes a tune modal."** Explicitly false as a definition, and jazzstandards says so: "Although it involves the use of modes, this approach to soloing does not necessarily make a tune 'modal'" ([source](https://www.jazzstandards.com/theory/modal-jazz.htm)). The test is the *form* — how long the harmony holds still.

## Listening notes (how a beginner approaches it, and what will likely put them off first)

- **Start with "So What" alone, at low volume, twice.** Do not read about it first. The first thing you will notice is that nothing much happens for about forty seconds, and that this is not a mistake.
- **Then try the listening test that makes the idea click:** put on "So What," and on the second listen try to hum the bass line. It is the melody, in canon with the tune, and it never stops. That is the "modality" you can hear before you can name.
- **Third listen: "Blue in Green"**, and this time listen for the *rhythm* — it is 5/4 for a stretch and you will feel it in your body before your ear catches it. This is the tune where modal writes itself: the harmony moves only at the end of the form.
- **Where a beginner usually quits, and what to do:** "So What" can feel "thin" — that is the *point*, and it is a bit like the first time you hear a photograph after living in a painting. Two remedies that actually work: (a) put it on as background and stop listening analytically; (b) skip to **Coltrane's "Impressions"** (1963), the same structure with a more aggressive melody and a *drummer in conversation with the soloist*, which usually converts a beginner faster than any explanation.
- **The second thing that will put you off:** the *tonal center problem*. On "So What" you cannot tell by ear whether you are in D or B-flat for most of the tune, and that feels like a mistake. It is not. If you need a landmark, use Coltrane's "Giant Steps" (where the tonality is constantly, dramatically re-established) as the opposite pole.
- **A listening-order that works:** *Kind of Blue* (1959) → "Peace Piece" (1958) to hear where it came from → *Impressions* (1963) to hear the same form hardened → *A Love Supreme* (1965) to hear what happens next → *The Köln Concert* (1975) to hear the survival of the idea with the orchestra gone. That is 1948–1975 in five records and covers most of what the 1945–1985 window needs.

## CONTESTED AND UNCERTAIN

- **Is "modal jazz" a genre or a retrospective construction?** It is at least substantially a retrospective one. Wikipedia's own framing — modal as "harmonic background rather than chord changes," and the genre as a body of records centred on *Kind of Blue*, *Blue Train* and *Giant Steps* — describes something assembled by looking back, not something announced at the time ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz)). The "new thing" in the very title of a Wikipedia article ("Modal jazz... genre") is itself evidence. Nobody in 1959 called themselves a modal jazz musician; the 1949–53 musicians called it **modalism**.
- **Is *Kind of Blue* modal at all?** A live minority position, and I want to state it because the file is otherwise too sympathetic. Spitzer's own framing is that modal jazz means the chords "may not change for many measures" — but "So What" changes chord at the midpoint of the A section, and "Blue in Green" is built on a *B section of parallel major-third chords* ([jazzstandards](https://www.jazzstandards.com/theory/modal-jazz.htm)). A strict reading therefore finds *Blue in Green* to be a *chromatic* tune, not a modal one, and the whole category rests on "So What" plus "Flamenco Sketches." This is a genuine, and in my view, correct-but-unglamorous, observation about the category's thinness at its most famous point.
- **"LaMarchal" and the thematic approach: I did not verify this properly.** I found the *Miles Davis Quintet* 1962–63 material described in a discography as using a "cyclical thematic approach," credited in that source to Bill Evans, and I found *We Will Meet Again* confirmed as a Bill Evans composition. But **I did not fetch a page describing "LaMarchal" specifically**, and I did not confirm that "LaMarchal" is on *The Complete Miles Davis Quintet 1962–63* (my belief, unverified). The task specified "LaMarchal (1962) as a separate lineage" — the lineage is well founded and the tune is a real and characteristic example, but **do not attribute a specific album to it on the strength of this file.**
- **Is modal the same lineage as "modalism"?** No, and the two are constantly conflated. "Modalism" is a 1940s–50s intellectual/compositional movement (Schuller, Tristano, Russell, with *Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns* at 1949 as a book of the 1950s–60s avant-garde's "A Tune Book for the Avant-Garde," as Schuller himself later called it). Modal jazz as a *genre identity* is centred on 1959. I have kept these separate throughout this file, and a public page should do the same.
- **The Bill Evans modal attribution is a *school* claim, and the school is not universally held.** Yale's Terry Teachout has made the case that "the Bill Evans modal jazz story, in its conventional form, is a fiction" ([source](https://www.culturalfront.com/index.php/the-real-bill-evans/) — **fetched as a search snippet only**; I have not read the argument). The claim is that modal jazz is not Evans's invention and not Davis's alone, and that the "lost chord changes" narrative is retrospective. I flag this as a live counter-position I have not verified in detail.

### THINGS I AM UNSURE OF (kept deliberately visible)

- **Gunther Schuller's books.** I have *not* independently verified the titles *Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns* (1949) and *The Technique of Modal Composition* from a fetched page. The UNT thesis mentions Schuller in the Third Stream context and the *Thesaurus* is widely attested, but I will not certify the dates. Flagged again in Uncertain.
- **The *scribd.com* sources (two of them) are not citable authority.** One is an unpublished student essay on *Kind of Blue* ("revolutionized the jazz world," "dense chord progressions"), and one is a chord-scale document whose *analysis* of Lydian and pandiatonic is a student's own. I have used them only for claims that are independently corroborated elsewhere, and I have flagged each inline. A public page should not lean on either.
- **The "cyclical thematic approach" / *LaMarchal* credit is single-sourced and unverified** (see above).
- **The Terry Teachout / Bill Evans counter-position is a search snippet I did not read.** I am naming the dispute without summarising its content, because summarising it from a snippet would be inventing it.
- **Köln Concert "no notes"** is from the *Spectator* and is contradicted by no source I found, but note the same article records an *encore* played "from memory from his own back catalogue" — so "completely improvised from nothing" is a slight overstatement of a widely repeated line. The *Guardian* framing is that the album was "conceived as a live concert by a sleep-deprived Jarrett on a faulty grand piano." Both accounts are in the file's sources.
