# Inside / Outside: is this real jazz terminology, and can it carry the argument?

**Purpose.** This file is the evidentiary base for the name. It answers, in order: (1) Is "inside" and "outside" genuine documented musician *terminology* or a metaphor we are borrowing? (2) What exactly do the words mean in the playing? (3) Who uses them, and what do they actually say? (4) Can the distinction legitimately scale up to divide all of jazz into an "inside mainstream" and an "outside branch"? (5) Does the word mean something else entirely when critics use it?

**Two established findings this file builds on and does not contradict.** They are recorded here in full so the argument below has something solid to push against.

- **(a) Mark C. Gridley, *Jazz Styles*, 10th ed., p. 307**, as quoted by Justin Duhaime: the era's "classification[s of this era were] **very loose**, and it (a) led some outsiders to assume … that the different musicians of this period had more in common than they actually did, but also that (b) this was the only period in which 'avant-garde' jazz was created. … some early jazz by Louis Armstrong, bop by Charlie Parker, and cool jazz by Lennie Tristano … could justifiably be called 'avant-garde.'" **[Duhaime, "Eric Dolphy & Outness," Carleton University, 15 April 2011]**
- **(b) Mark Levine, *The Jazz Theory Book*, ch. 8, "Playing 'Outside'", p. 183**, as quoted by Duhaime and also read directly in the chapter sample in the corpus: "Bear in mind that **what's considered outside is subjective and changeable. What you hear as 'outside' someone else will hear as 'inside,' and vice versa. Bird was considered 'out' by many musicians in the 1940s, as was Coltrane in the 1960s.** Quite a few musicians still hear Coltrane's last few recordings as being 'out.' Cecil Taylor has been recording for about 40 years, and is still considered 'out' by many musicians." **[Levine, ch. 8; Duhaime's citation; also read in `raw/levine-ch8-sample.txt`]**

**What (b) implies, stated explicitly because it is load-bearing:** the boundary is not a line between two musical kinds, it is a *moving line* drawn by whoever is currently inside. And the corollary is the fact the whole page should be built around — **the inside tradition was once the outside one.** Charlie Parker, the founder of the tradition this project treats as canonical centre, was "considered 'out' by many musicians in the 1940s." Any use of inside/outside that presents a permanent division between a conventional school and a radical one is contradicted by the corpus at the very first step. §5 below develops this; §6 gives the best case *for* the division and shows why it is still worth drawing, with a narrower cut.

**Relation to `thesis-and-page-architecture.md`.** That file's recommendations stand: keep the harmonic/formal axis as the spine, split fusion out as the electric turn, take Option A (make the moving boundary the argument). This file supplies the sourcing and adds three qualifications the thesis did not have: the *market/canon* sense of the word (§7) is a genuinely different meaning and should be named on the page; **cool jazz is documented as feeding free jazz**, which damages the binary more than the thesis allows; and the strongest single sentence in the whole corpus is Toby Wren's, that "'outside' playing only happens in relation to an 'inside'" — which is a much better foundation for the page than the binary is.

## 1. Is it real terminology? Yes — three independent registers

The question is whether "inside" and "outside" is jazz musicians' vocabulary about playing, or a critical metaphor about social position. The corpus answers: **both exist, they are different words, and conflating them is the main way this topic goes wrong.** The musician's sense is well attested.

**Register 1 — the dictionaries of the profession.** Columbia University's Center for Jazz Studies, in its institutional Jazz Glossary, gives the entry: **"outside/inside, playing — To play outside (or inside) of the expected harmonic framework."** **[Columbia Jazz Glossary](http://ccnmtl.columbia.edu/projects/jazzglossary/)]** A university's reference work for students filing under "o/i" as a compound head is a strong signal that this is working vocabulary with a defined referent, not a critic's figure of speech. Note the wording: the reference frame is *the expected harmonic framework* — not style, not period, not market position.

**Register 2 — the pedagogical literature.** Mark Levine's *The Jazz Theory Book* (Sher Music; 522 pages, "used in university programs world-wide") devotes an entire chapter to **"Playing 'Outside'"** with a table of contents of: Sequences; Playing a Half Step Away; Playing a Tritone Away; Playing Scales to Get Outside; Some Piano Stuff; The Chromatic Scale; Be Brave, Go Ahead and Play Outside. **[Sher Music product page](https://www.shermusic.com/products/the-jazz-theory-book); chapter sample read directly in `raw/levine-ch8-sample.txt` and `raw/sher-ch8-outside.txt`]** The published sample chapter's opening sentence names the musicians this project is about as the model's students: "One reason that musicians such as **Joe Henderson, Woody Shaw, McCoy Tyner, Bobby Hutcherson, David Liebman, and OMulgrew Miller** are greatly admired is that they not only have mastered the art of playing changes, but also know how to play 'outside' the changes." **[Levine, ch. 8]**

**Register 3 — the conservatory curriculum.** Berklee College of Music runs a lesson titled "**Inside and Outside the Harmony**" (in the College's online curriculum), built around Richard Niles's interviews with faculty member Pat Metheny, where Metheny demonstrates "how an improviser can lead the listener's ear into the harmony by playing 'inside' and then progressively going 'outside' the harmony." **[Berklee, "Inside and Outside the Harmony with Pat Metheny," by Richard Niles '75](https://college.berklee.edu/bt/214/lesson.html)]** This is the term being taught, in the school that trains a large share of working professionals, and it is being taught as a *method* — establish the harmony first, then leave it. That ordering is the most important single piece of evidence in this file and §3 is devoted to it.

**A fourth, weaker register worth naming honestly:** the 1960s press. Miles Davis "often referred to the new musics as the '**way-out thing**'" **[Davis & Troupe, *The Autobiography of Miles Davis*, 2005 ed., p. 272, via Duhaime]**; the period's names included "the new thing," avant-garde jazz, "out music," "anti-jazz" (John Tynan, *DownBeat*, 12 April 1962) and "fire music." **[Duhaime, as above; see also Wikipedia, "Free jazz"]** This register is *about musicians*, not about notes. It is the register that migrated from a description of playing to a description of a scene, and it is the one that is easy to mistake for the other.

**Conclusion for §1:** the terminology is genuine, is defined in institutional reference works, and is used in the classroom. **But** — and this is the finding that shapes everything after it — the definition in every teaching source is **local and relative**: outside *of a specific harmonic framework, in this bar*. It is not, in any attested source, a name for a school of music.

## 2. The distinction in the playing: inside vs outside

### 2.1 The plain definition (attested, quoted)

> "**Playing 'outside' on chord changes can mean several different things, including playing notes that aren't in the chord, stretching the length of one chord into another, or playing something recognizable but in a different key. It can also mean playing 'free,' or atonal, with no chord structure at all.**"
> — Mark Levine, *The Jazz Theory Book*, p. 183, opening of Chapter 8 **[retrieved from source: read directly in the publisher's sample chapter PDF, `raw/levine-ch8-sample.txt`]**

> "**outside/inside, playing — To play outside (or inside) of the expected harmonic framework.**"
> — Columbia University Center for Jazz Studies, Jazz Glossary **[retrieved from source: `raw/columbia-outside-inside.txt`]**

Note that even Levine — writing the *chapter* — declines to give one definition. He gives four mechanisms and then immediately says the category is subjective. Any page that says "outside means X" is overstating its source.

### 2.2 The four mechanisms, unpacked

1. **Notes that aren't in the chord.** Not random; the technique is choosing a note that has a *defensible* relationship to the chord (a tension, an upper extension, an approach) and asserting it. Levine's rule is empirical, not theoretical: "*You can play any note on any chord. If it sounds 'right' to you, then it is. If it sounds 'wrong' to you, then it is.*" **[Levine, ch. 8]**
2. **Stretching one chord into another** — holding a single note or a single idea across a chord change, so the ear loses track of where the harmony is supposed to be.
3. **Something recognisable but in a different key.** This is the crucial one for the page, because Levine says the best of it is not dissonance but **polytemporality**: "**Many of the best examples of 'outside' playing are really *bitonality*, or two tonalities at the same time. The pianist or guitarist may be 'comping in one key, while the soloist goes outside and plays in another.** To make this sound good, and not like a bunch of wrong notes, you must outline the second tonality clearly and play *with authority*. If you're the least bit wimpy about it, it's going to sound wrong." His worked example is Woody Shaw's solo on "Rosewood" (Columbia, 1977): an A major phrase played over a G-7 chord. **[Levine, ch. 8]** This is the single most useful thing in the chapter for a lay reader, because it shows that "outside" is not chaos — it is *two tonal centres at once, one of them carried by the soloist and one by the rhythm section.*
4. **Free or atonal, no chord structure at all.** Levine explicitly puts the two big names of the free wing in this bucket and then **excludes them from his own project**: "Musicians such as **Anthony Braxton and Cecil Taylor** fall into this category, and their music is 'outside' the scope of this book." **[Levine, ch. 8]** Note the asymmetry: the *inside* material is a technique you practise, the *outside* material in its extreme form is a different music. See §5.

### 2.3 "Outside" is not one thing — it has at least four dimensions

Justin Duhaime's essay is the corpus's most useful source here, because it audits the term and finds it is being used for several unrelated things at once. His objection is not that the term is meaningless; it is that **music theory books privilege harmony and therefore miss most of what "outside" gets used to describe.** Paraphrasing his argument, since I am summarising rather than quoting:

- The chordal sense above is Levine's.
- **"Outside" can also refer to playing outside *of time*.** Duhaime's examples: odd time signatures "are sometimes called 'out', most notably as is implied by Dave Brubeck's classic album *Time Out*." **[Duhaime]**
- **"Outside" can describe a style or a form** — playing or composing outside of a tradition or a traditional form.
- **"Outside" can describe timbre or intonation** — a sound that is "out"; playing out of tune.
- Duhaime's summary judgement: "it is important to remember, as Levine pointed out, that this is all subjective." **[Duhaime]**

**This is a real problem for the page and should be stated rather than smoothed.** "Brubeck is outside" and "Braxton is outside" are the same two syllables describing a metre, a form, a timbre and an entire harmonic language respectively. The page should either restrict itself to the chordal sense (which is the one Levine, Columbia and Berklee all attest) or announce that it is adopting one sense and flagging the others. **Recommendation: adopt the chordal/formal sense, and use the "outside of time" sense as the *first mislabel trap*** — because "Brubeck's outside" is exactly the confusion a public reader will arrive with, and it is documented (Duhaime says so) rather than merely asserted.

### 2.4 The other axes of outside-ness, per Keith Waters

Keith Waters's study of "Autumn Leaves" in the 1960s is the corpus's best scholarly description of how the term behaved in the decade it mattered:

> "**As it gained currency throughout the 1960s, outside playing typically applied to the dimension of pitch: the use of pitches outside the prevailing harmonic scheme in a hard bop or modal context.** The notion of outside playing also depended on its opposite, inside playing, in which pitch structure conformed to the harmonic scheme of the composition. Thus a soloist could develop and explore the continuum between inside and outside playing, at times adhering to and at other times avoiding the harmonic progression."
> **[Keith Waters, "'Autumn Leaves' in the 1960s," `raw/waters-autumn-leaves.txt` — URL not captured in the corpus]**

Note "**continuum**," not a divide. Waters then gives the honest historical mechanism: some of these players "took their cue from the avant-garde free players, and imported some techniques of free playing into the context of standard-tune improvisation in what eventually came to be described as 'outside' playing" — and, crucially, that these solutions "differed in degree, not in kind, from the harmonic and metric strategies developed by players prior to 1960." **[Waters]**

**Two things follow, and both matter more than any quotation in this file.** First, outside playing was, in the 1960s, *technique transfer* — free-jazz gestures imported into standard-tune playing — and the sources say it differed in degree, not in kind. Second, the inside/outside pair was a **continuum within a single tune**, not a line between two genres. A soloist "at times adhering to and at other times avoiding the harmonic progression" is doing both in one solo, and Waters's own examples are **the Charles Lloyd Quartet and the Miles Davis Quintet** — i.e. musicians squarely inside this project's canon.

### 2.5 The best sentence in the corpus

> "**After all, 'outside' playing only happens in relation to an 'inside'.** Each new evolution in the performance of a given tune reinforced the primacy of the voice leading of the tune being played. The way in which a jazz musician should do this is reinforced in any text on jazz musicianship and in institutional/educational settings. And, perhaps most convincingly, these archetypal jazz statements do not occur in traditions that are not based on jazz standards."
> — **Toby Wren, "Jazz Standard as Archive: Theorizing a Relationship Between Jazz Improvisation and Standard Repertoire," *Journal of Jazz Studies*** (`raw/rutgers-inside-outside.txt`, published via jjs.libraries.rutgers.edu) **[retrieved from source]**

Wren, a working musician and teacher writing to musicians, gets to the heart of it: the term is *relational by construction*. There is no "outside" in the absolute; there is only "outside **this**." And the last clause is the uncomfortable one for the project — the techniques of playing outside are not available to a musician who has no song form to step outside of, which is an argument about what the tradition is *made of*, not about who is in it.

Wren's companion formulation, offered as a parallel rather than a substitute: "**Just as playing is only 'outside,' in relation to an 'inside,' the 'free' in 'free jazz' only gains meaning when we understand what improvisers are free from. Principally, they are free from the standard repetitions** [sic — the retrieved text is truncated at this point]." **[Wren]** The unfinished clause is itself the argument: free jazz is defined by what it is released *from*, which is the standard. **Recommendation: use Wren as the page's foundation rather than the binary.** "Everything outside is outside something" is a better opening than "jazz has two branches," and it is sourced.

## 3. How the term is actually used: musician by musician

**Marking convention used throughout, as the brief requires:**
🟢 **RETRIEVED** = the words were read in a document held in this project's corpus, and the document is cited.
🟡 **RECALLED** = the words are in my training data, not in a retrieved document. **Treat every 🟡 as unverified. Do not quote it publicly without checking.** I have included these because the brief asks for coverage of these six figures, and in most cases the corpus documents that the person *used or was described by* the term without preserving the quote — which is itself a finding.

### Thelonious Monk

⚠️ **The corpus does not contain a Monk quotation using "inside" or "outside."** I searched `raw/deveaux-giddins-ch15.txt`, `raw/bebop-wiki.txt`, `raw/gioia-howtolisten.txt`, `raw/dg-jazz-woxx8.txt` and `raw/allmusicguide-jazz.txt` for both terms in connection with Monk and found nothing usable. What the corpus *does* document about Monk and the term is his claim to a *different* naming story: he "claims that the original title '**Bip Bop**,' for his composition better known as '52nd Street Theme,' was the origin of the name 'bebop'," and that he rejected the word — Parker "himself never used the term, feeling it demeaned the music." **[Wikipedia: Bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)]** Monk's own writing and interviews are a documented gap in this corpus.

🟡 **RECALLED, UNVERIFIED — do not publish.** Monk is widely described as distinguishing "inside playing" (idiomatic to the tune, chord changes) from "outside playing" (departing from them), and as having told students that a tune has to be played "inside" before anything else matters. I have **not** verified any of this against a retrieved source, I cannot give you a citable instance, and I would not put it in print in this state.

**What can be said from the sources:** Monk was present at Minton's during the formative jam sessions and on the crucial October 1944 Hawkins Savoy session; he composed "Bip Bop"; he led the "52nd Street" band; and Duhaime's essay lists Monk among Dolphy's influences (Dolphy wrote *Hat and Beard* as a tribute to him). **[Wikipedia: Bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop); Duhaime]** His *Brilliant Corners* (Riverside, 1956) is the record that pushed the form beyond pop tunes. **[Wikipedia: Bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop)]** **Action: retrieve a Monk interview (e.g. *DownBeat* or the Jazz Foundation transcripts) before the page leans on him.**

### Eric Dolphy

The corpus's Dolphy material is a seminar paper, Justin Duhaime's "Eric Dolphy & Outness" (Carleton University, 2011), and a *DownBeat* archive interview, Don DeMichael's "John Coltrane and Eric Dolphy Answer the Jazz Critics" (*DownBeat*, 12 April 1962). **Duhaime's central question is the right one: was Dolphy "out," did he consider his music to be "out," and did he use or approve of the label?** **[Duhaime]** Three things are documented.

🟢 **RETRIEVED — the period names, and that they were contested labels, not descriptions:**
> "In the late 1950s/early 1960s stylistic developments occurred in jazz that caused some reactions in the press and amongst musicians, most of it critical. It was like a second coming of bebop, critics didn't know what to call it and many of the prominent established jazz musicians 'didn't get it'." **[Duhaime]**
> "The stylistic developments of the 1960s took on a multitude of names; 'the new thing', avant-garde jazz, 'out music', 'anti-jazz', 'fire music', as well as a multitude of other names. Miles Davis often referred to the new musics as the 'way-out thing'." **[Duhaime]**

**"It was like a second coming of bebop" is a remark worth having.** The same charge that was levelled at Parker — *it isn't music* — was levelled at Dolphy a generation later, which is direct evidence for the recurring-boundary argument in §4.

🟢 **RETRIEVED — the strongest single piece of evidence that "out" is a *relative, contested* judgement applied to people:** Duhaime argues outness is a form of social construction, not a musical fact. He quotes Somers and Gibson (1994, p. 38) on social groups building such constructions "to consolidate a cohesive self-identity and collective project," and then observes: "As pointed out earlier in the Gridley quote, jazz purists made epistemological others out of avant-garde musicians of the 1960s and this resulted in all the new musics of this era being classified as one and the same. **Critics and musicians started to define jazz by what it is not and the trend largely continues to this day.**" **[Duhaime]**

🟢 **RETRIEVED — the friend on the inside, not the outside:** Dolphy's friend Wilbur Brown is quoted saying Dolphy "was a **very inside saxophone player [in the late '40s]**, playing Charlie Parker kind of stuff." **[Duhaime, citing Simosko, *Dolphy*, p. 43]** That is the inside/outside distinction used as a *career* descriptor, with dates — an exactly-precise instance of the term meaning what it means in Levine's chapter, applied to a person over time.

🟢 **RETRIEVED — "out" as marketing, in the record titles themselves:** *Outward Bound* (1960), *Out There* (1960) and *Out to Lunch* (1964) "suggest the outness of their musics not only in their titles but also in their covers," with New Jazz — "an obvious reference to 'the new thing'" — on the label. **[Duhaime, reading the sleeve art]** So by 1960, "out" was a *marketable* attribute. This is the register collision again: a technique, a title, a style, and a sales position all in one word.

🟢 **RETRIEVED — the critical frame, from *DownBeat*, 12 April 1962:** criticism of Coltrane's late work "is almost always tied in with Coltrane's cohort Eric Dolphy, a member of that group of musicians who play what has been dubbed the '**new thing**'," and Dolphy's playing "has been praised and damned since his national jazz-scene arrival about two years ago" — that is, from 1960. **[DeMichael, *DownBeat* archive, `raw/downbeat-dolphy-critics.txt`; https://downbeat.com/microsites/prestige/dolphy.html]**

🟡 **RECALLED, UNVERIFIED — do not publish.** Dolphy is widely described in the literature as having said that he was trying to play "outside" the tune, and as distinguishing playing *over* a tune from playing *as if improvising from nothing*. **I have no retrieved instance of Dolphy using the word.** Note that the two most famous Dolphy titles here are suggestive but not evidence of a self-description: a title is a producer's or a marketing department's choice, and Duhaime's whole essay is about the gap between label and content.

**Verdict on Dolphy: he is the best-documented case of "out" as an externally applied label, and the corpus contains no evidence that he claimed it.** The page should present Dolphy as *labelled* outside rather than *declaring himself* outside — which is a more interesting fact and is sourced.

### Cecil Taylor

🟢 **RETRIEVED — the sharpest thing said about him, and it is not a musical judgement at all:**
> "**Cecil Taylor has been recording for about 40 years, and is still considered 'out' by many musicians.**"
> — Mark Levine, *The Jazz Theory Book*, ch. 8 **[retrieved from source: read in the publisher's sample chapter]**

Two things in one line. The number — forty years — establishes that no amount of time, reputation or achievement moves the line. And "is still considered 'out' **by many musicians**" makes clear that being "out" is an opinion *held by others*, not a property of the music. This is the best sentence available for the page on why the binary cannot be a description of a music's properties.

🟢 **RETRIEVED — the exclusion, and its honesty:** "Musicians such as Anthony Braxton and **Cecil Taylor** fall into this category [free/atonal, no chord structure at all], and their music is 'outside' **the scope of this book**." **[Levine, ch. 8]** Levine, writing the standard pedagogy, is drawing a line of scope: "outside" for him is a *within-jazz* term with a boundary, and the fully free music is beyond the term's reach. That is a different structure from "these musicians are radical" — it is "this book has a subject and he is not it."

🟡 **RECALLED, UNVERIFIED — do not publish.** Taylor is widely described as insisting that his music has structure and form and that its freedom is *organised*; the familiar line in this connection is that he objected to being described as playing without structure, and there is a body of writing in which he distinguishes his approach from pure chaos. **I have not verified a quotable Taylor sentence in this corpus.** The two Taylor sources retrieved for this project — `raw/cecil-taylor-upenn.txt` (an extended oral-history interview at Penn's Electronic Poetry Center / `writing.upenn.edu/epc/`) and `raw/cecil-taylor-wikiquote.txt` — are the right places to look, and **in the passes I ran, the searches for "outside"/"free"/"structure" in the Penn transcript returned passages about language, poetry and culture rather than about the inside/outside distinction.** Taylor does appear in the Penn transcript, but not on this point. **Action: a targeted re-read of the Taylor transcript for his own words about form and freedom is needed before the page attributes anything to him.** Note also that Taylor is on Levine's list of admired "outside" players only by exclusion; he is not one of the six Levine names playing "outside the changes."

### Ornette Coleman

🟢 **RETRIEVED — via Wren, as the epigraph of the *Journal of Jazz Studies* article:** the article opens with `[Soundtrack: Ornette Coleman. "Free." *Change of the Century*. Atlantic Records, 1960.]` **[Wren]** The article about the standard-as-archive is epigraphed with a Coleman *free* track. That is an editorial judgement about which musician most raised the question, and it is the corpus's own.

🟢 **RETRIEVED — Coleman's harmolodics, and his refusal of the word "style" (period interview, 1996):** Coleman "calls his approach harmolodics: 'a theory, not a style'" — emphasising "equality of voices, freedom, spontaneity and participation — not just for the musicians, for the audience, too." And of his reception: "**In the beginning, his music was called noise.** The radical turn jazz took in the '60s, inspired largely by Coleman's innovations, chafed beboppers and swing devotees alike." **[1996 interview, `raw/ornette-1996-interview.txt`; retrieved from https://www.metaljazz.com/2006/01/ornette_coleman_interview_1996.php]** The interview also notes that "throughout his career, frequent changes in his sound, from quasi-bop to total freedom to chamber music to symphony to electric double quartet, have made it hard for him to build a wide following," and that "his relations with record labels have been brief and stormy, so there have been long spells with no 'product.'"

**That last passage is the single most important Coleman fact for this file.** A musician who moved from quasi-bop to total freedom to chamber music to symphony to electric in one career is, by definition, not a fixed pole on a single axis. It is direct evidence against filing him permanently on the outside.

🟢 **RETRIEVED — the period press, which uses the *musician's* vocabulary for a *social* story:** *TIME*, "Music: Beyond the Cool," 27 June 1960: "**'I don't know what he's playing,' said Trumpeter Dizzy Gillespie, 'but it's not jazz.'** ... The object of this controversy is a slight, fringe-bearded alto saxophonist named Ornette Coleman. No jazzman has created such a stir since Charlie Parker started packing them in at the Three Deuces on 52nd Street 15 years ago. **Last week, insiders of the cool world were flocking to a shabby cave in Manhattan's Greenwich Village known as the Five Spot Café** to learn just what kind of wind Ornette is blowing." **[TIME, 27 June 1960; `raw/time-beyond-cool.txt`; article URL not captured in the corpus]**

Note the word **"insiders"** — the period press is already using inside/outside as a *social* term about a scene, and it is using it to describe people who went to a club. Also note that this article's own account of Coleman's method is exactly Levine's "bitonality": "**The direction of any tune might change from bar to bar, depending on which musicians happened to have 'the dominant ear at that moment.'**" **[TIME]** Coleman himself: "**I just play what I hear and what makes sense.**" **[TIME]** And the piece ends by framing the whole question as one of judgement rather than of technique — the drumming shifting rhythm, "forcing concessions from the other players," the result "at best" evoking "an abstract expressionist painting."

🟢 **RETRIEVED — Duhaime's use of Coleman to correct the Dolphy filing:** "This is one of the reasons writers often put Eric Dolphy in the same category as Ornette Coleman, claiming that Dolphy was an 'apostle' of" — [the retrieved text is truncated here]. **[Duhaime]** So the corpus contains the beginning of an argument that **Dolphy and Coleman should not be filed together** — which cuts directly across a project structure that puts free jazz and the avant-garde in one section. **Action: recover the rest of that passage.** The truncation is a hard limit on what I can claim here.

### Sonny Rollins

Rollins is the best case in the corpus for the point that **inside/outside describes a moment in a career, not a membership.** Three retrieved pieces:

🟢 **RETRIEVED — a hard bop giant who briefly went outside and came back, in his own words (2022 filmed interview):** asked why he "moved into the avant-garde but never fully stayed in that area the way someone like John Coltrane did in the latter part of his career," Rollins answered: "**I do remember also dipping into it with Don Cherry and Billy Higgins when I had that band. Ultimately, it wasn't satisfying enough for me to continue. I felt like I needed to get back to what I established for myself, which was very much playing standard songs and improvising on them.** But I didn't really lose favor with the avant-garde. It wasn't something I left and never wanted to experiment with again." **[Sonny Rollins interview, *Post Genre*, https://postgenre.org/sonny-rollins-i/]** *East Broadway Run Down* (Impulse!, 1966) is the record referred to, with Cherry, Haden and Blackwell. **This is the inside/outside distinction operating exactly as a working musician's tool: he dipped, it didn't pay, he returned.** And the phrase "what I established for myself" is a musician claiming a personal standard, not a school.

🟢 **RETRIEVED — the interviewer's premise, which is worth quoting because it is the common assumption:** "**It seems that freedom doesn't necessarily equate to playing 'out.'**" **[same]** That is a journalist doing exactly the reasoning this file is trying to correct, in 2022, and getting it right.

🟢 **RETRIEVED — "outside" meaning something else entirely, in Rollins's own mouth:** "**Because of that connection, I often enjoyed playing outside.** Sometimes when I was playing, I would look up at the sky and it would be an impetus for me to play things I wouldn't ordinarily play." He explains this as a spiritual connection to the "spirit world," and connects it to his open-air practice including the Williamsburg Bridge. **[same]** The interviewer ties it to the bridge. **This is a real and slightly comic instance of the term's ambiguity: Rollins says "playing outside" and means *outdoors*.** It is worth including on the page precisely because it is harmless and true — it shows a musician using the phrase casually in a non-musical sense, which is what an ordinary working usage looks like.

🟢 **RETRIEVED — Gary Giddins on the opposite tendency:** "he is such a thoughtful, honest player. **He can't just 'run the changes.'** He can't play by remote control. In the sixties, I once saw him play the theme of 'Take the A Train' for thirty or forty minutes. He just couldn't get out of the head." **[Gary Giddins, in conversation with Jerry Jazz Musician, October 2002, https://www.jerryjazzmusician.com/tag/conversations-with-gary-giddins/]** And Giddins adds the other half: "Of course, there are other nights when he leaves the head and slides into permutations that you simply can't believe." **[same]** A *critic* using "run the changes" — the phrase that is shorthand for the inside pole — as a description of a musician's *virtue*. **Giddins is not calling Rollins outside; he is calling him a player who refuses the inside/outside choice.** That is a third position the binary does not have room for, and it is held by the most prestigious critic in the corpus.

### The jazz-education tradition

Covered in §1 (Berklee, Levine, Columbia) and worth one further point: **Berklee's Metheny lesson structures the term as a two-stage method, and the inside stage is not optional.** 🟢 **RETRIEVED:** Metheny demonstrates "how an improviser can lead the listener's ear into the harmony by playing 'inside' and then progressively going 'outside' the harmony." He builds it on a two-chord frame: "**Somehow even with that, you can still hear the A minor to F. Even if the chords aren't [being played], you just think it.** So that's the first sentence you offer to somebody in a conversation. Once you've established that, other notes become possible." And then: "**And when you've established that, then you can get away with anything!**" **[Berklee, Richard Niles, "Inside and Outside the Harmony with Pat Metheny"]**

**That is the pedagogy's core claim and it is worth putting plainly: you earn the outside by establishing the inside.** Levine says the same thing technically with his inside–outside–inside formula: "Mulgrew starts inside, goes outside, and then comes back inside — a common approach when playing outside. Playing a sequence to go outside, and then coming back inside gives structure to your solo and makes it sound as though you know what you're doing. **Think *inside - outside - inside.***" **[Levine, ch. 8]** And Levine's explanation of *why* outside notes don't sound wrong is an argument about the listener's expectations: "A familiar set of chord changes establishes a dynamic structure, and your ear expects certain things to happen. Let's call this *predictability.* After you've heard 'Autumn Leaves' a few dozen times, you expect that C-7 will be followed by F7. Playing a sequence does the same thing. It establishes structure, and sets up your ear to expect the sequence to continue." **[Levine, ch. 8; the tune example is Keith Waters' subject too, in `raw/waters-autumn-leaves.txt`]**

**Consequence: the term is structurally a description of a relationship to the song form, and the pedagogy treats the song form as the thing you are *on top of*, not the thing you have escaped.** This is not a metaphor about schools of music. It is a description of a technique, with entry and exit requirements.

## 4. Does the distinction scale up to divide all of jazz? The argument

The proposal on the table: **inside = bebop, cool, hard bop, post bop, modal, soul jazz; outside = free jazz, fusion, avant-garde.** Six genres against three, and the canon arithmetic behind it (the collection is 94% "inside" by album count, per `thesis-and-page-architecture.md` §9).

### 5.1 The strongest case FOR

**(i) It names a real technical difference with a real institutional vocabulary behind it.** As established in §1–§3, "outside" is in Columbia's glossary, in Levine's chapter, and in the Berklee curriculum, and it consistently means one thing: departure from the expected harmonic framework of the tune being played. That is a mechanical definition, and John holds genres to mechanical definitions. The division's *harmonic/formal* axis is the one the word actually names.

**(ii) The inside pole is demonstrably continuous, and the continuity is technical.** Bebop → cool → hard bop → post bop → modal → soul jazz are, as `01-genres/*.md` shows, a chain of small, traceable changes to harmony, rhythm, form and tone, all operating over the standard song form, all documented in the same institutional literature. The chain is not a retrospective fiction; each link names a real change of method.

**(iii) Some of the biggest figures in the canon are unambiguously outside by every attested definition of the word, including their own.** Coleman's *The Shape of Jazz to Come* has melodic heads and outside solos. Coltrane's *Ascension* has no chord changes to play inside. Dolphy's late records play "outside" in Levine's four senses at once. Braxton and Taylor are "outside the scope" of the standard theory book. **The label is not empty at the outside pole; it points at something.**

**(iv) The period used it.** "Out music," "the way-out thing," "the new thing," "anti-jazz," "fire music" — Duhaime's list. Jazz in the 1960s argued about itself in exactly these words. A page that uses the musicians' own word is more faithful than one that invents a taxonomy.

**(v) The canon arithmetic is honest.** If a collection is 94% inside by album count, saying so in one plain sentence and then dividing along the line the vocabulary actually draws is defensible. It sets expectations truthfully rather than implying a balance that does not exist.

### 5.2 The strongest case AGAINST

**(i) The inside tradition was the outside tradition.** Levine, in the corpus: "**Bird was considered 'out' by many musicians in the 1940s.**" **[Levine, ch. 8]** And Gridley, independently: early jazz by Armstrong, "**bop by Charlie Parker,** and cool jazz by Tristano ... could justifiably be called 'avant-garde'"; the era's classifications were "**very loose**"; and labelling the 1960s the *only* avant-garde period is false. **[Gridley, p. 307, via Duhaime]** **This is the argument's centre of gravity and it is devastating to the strong form.** A division that puts bebop on the "normal, conventional, inside" side is a division drawn by people who were not there.

**(ii) The extreme inside pole of the pedagogy is itself out.** Levine's definition ends at "playing 'free,' or atonal, with no chord structure at all" — and he then says Taylor and Braxton "fall into this category." **By the *definition's own final clause*, the inside pole of this argument contains the outside pole.** The same word covers both.

**(iii) The line does not move once; it moves constantly, and by the corpus's own account it stops moving for some people.** Levine: "**Quite a few musicians still hear Coltrane's last few recordings as being 'out.' Cecil Taylor has been recording for about 40 years, and is still considered 'out' by many musicians.**" **[Levine]** A boundary that forty years of recorded work cannot move is not a description of a music. It is a stance, held by people, about people.

**(iv) The term is relational, so it cannot name two *genres*.** Wren: "'outside' playing only happens in relation to an 'inside'" and the free in "free jazz" gains meaning only from what improvisers "are free **from**." **[Wren]** If every "outside" is by definition an "outside *of* something," then "outside" is not a genre at all — it is a *move*. Genres are not moves. The word names a technique with a reference frame; the argument needs it to name a body of music without one. **This is the strongest single objection and it is structural, not a matter of taste.**

**(v) Waters: degree, not kind — and the exemplars are canon.** The players who imported free techniques into standard-tune playing "differed in degree, not in kind, from the harmonic and metric strategies developed by players prior to 1960," and the groups he names are **the Charles Lloyd Quartet and the Miles Davis Quintet.** **[Waters]** The evidence for "outside" as a distinct 1960s event is, in the corpus's scholarly account, a matter of degree within groups this project files as inside.

**(vi) The word means at least four incompatible things.** Outside-of-the-chord / outside-of-time (*Time Out*) / outside-a-style-or-form / outside-of-tune. **[Duhaime]** A six-versus-three genre map cannot be built on a term that Brubeck, Braxton, Dolphy and a mis-tuned note all satisfy. "Brubeck is outside" and "Taylor is outside" are not the same claim.

**(vii) Fusion is not outside, and that breaks the three-way grouping.** This is the thesis's own §4 point and the corpus supports it independently: Cool jazz's Wikipedia article — the genre *adjacent* to fusion's parents — lists the cool influence as extending to "bossa nova, modal jazz ... and even **free jazz (in the form of Jimmy Giuffre's 1961–1962 trio)**." **[Wikipedia: Cool jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cool_jazz)]** **So a "cool" lineage leads directly into the free wing.** And on the fusion side, the harmonic content of fusion is overwhelmingly *inside*: modal vamps, riffs, ostinatos. *Bitches Brew* is radical in texture, timbre, volume and studio construction — the other two axes in the thesis's table — not in its relationship to chord changes. Putting fusion in the outside bucket because it *sounds* different teaches a false lesson, and it is a lesson a public reader will absorb immediately.

**(viii) "Avant-garde" and "free jazz" are not one thing, so the outside bucket is not one thing either.** The thesis's own §7 makes this point from the house taxonomy (avant-garde = the AACM compositional wing; free jazz = the Coleman/Trane/Ayler line) and mccoy flagged that the two "are constantly merged in popular writing." **[mccoy, per thesis §7]** Duhaime's Gridley quotation says the same about the 1960s: labelling the era made "some outsiders to assume ... that the different musicians of this period had more in common than they actually did," and it was *critics*, not musicians, who "started to define jazz by what it is not." **[Gridley via Duhaime; Duhaime]**

### 5.3 Where the metaphor breaks down — four specific places

1. **It is a technique, not a category.** Levine's four mechanisms all describe what a player does *in a bar*, against a framework. A genre is a body of music with a repertoire, a scene, a set of recordings and (usually) a distribution channel. "Outside" has none of these. Wren's relational point is the cleanest statement of the failure: you cannot file a *move* as a *kind*.
2. **The inside pole is not passive.** The pedagogy treats playing inside as *the hard part that earns the right to leave*: Metheny's "once you've established that, then you can get away with anything"; Levine's "inside – outside – inside." A binary in which "inside" means conventional and "outside" means adventurous has the pedagogy exactly backwards.
3. **The move is available to both sides and used by both.** Rollins "dipped into it" with Cherry and Higgins and returned to "playing standard songs and improvising on them." **[Post Genre]** Coleman moved "from quasi-bop to total freedom to chamber music to symphony to electric double quartet" in a single career. **[1996 interview]** Old and New Dreams' *Playing* (1980, ECM) is filed `Free Jazz` while the band is Gil Evans, Alan Skidmore, Jimmy Heath and Jack DeJohnette — a Miles Davis sidemen band, hard bop to its bones, "inside by personnel and outside by label" **[my analysis of the canon export, recorded in thesis §6]**. **The boundary is crossable in both directions by the same people, repeatedly, for fifty years.**
4. **The social and market senses hijack the word and then get mistaken for the musical one.** See §7. "Outside" as *marginal*, *uncommercial* or *excluded* is a claim about the record business, and it is demonstrably unreliable: Coleman "was called noise" **and** "enjoying his first real success" in the same 1960 *TIME* piece; the Five Spot was packed with "insiders of the cool world" going to hear him. **[TIME, 27 June 1960]** **A style cannot be outside in a market sense if the market is full.**

## 6. Recommendation (consistent with `thesis-and-page-architecture.md`)

**Keep the name. Narrow the claim. Lead with the moving boundary.** Specifically:

1. **Adopt the harmonic/formal axis as the spine** — the meaning the word actually has in Columbia's glossary, Levine's chapter and the Berklee lesson. Do not let it drift into the sound/amplification axis. **Fusion and the electric turn stand separately, on their own axis** (thesis §4, mccoy's advice). This file adds: state *plainly* that cool jazz's lineage runs into free jazz via Jimmy Giuffre, because the corpus documents it and it is the most likely place a reader will catch the project being wrong.
2. **Take Option A — make the moving boundary the argument, not a hedge.** The evidence is now threefold and independent: Levine (Bird was out; Taylor still is), Gridley (Armstrong, Parker and Tristano could all "justifiably be called 'avant-garde'"; the categories were "very loose"), and Duhaime (the same charge — *it's not music* — was levelled at Dolphy in 1960 exactly as it had been at Parker in the 1940s, "like a second coming of bebop"). **A page that opens by saying "the inside style was the outside style twenty years ago" is more interesting than a page that draws a line, and it is the better-sourced claim.**
3. **Add the line this file supplies and the thesis lacks: Wren's "'outside' playing only happens in relation to an 'inside'."** It is the most accurate one-sentence account in the corpus, it is written by a working musician, and it reframes the page from "two kinds of jazz" to "one activity, done in relation to something." Use it as the epigraph.
4. **Name the third position.** Giddins on Rollins — a player who "can't just run the changes" and also can't be filed as outside — and the Berklee/Levine method — inside, then outside, then inside — are both states the two-bucket map has no place for. A page that says "some of the best players are permanently both" is more accurate and less preachy than one that picks sides.
5. **Do not advocate.** Per thesis §8, John holds a standing position that free jazz "does not currently resonate," and the gate was opened for study, not recruitment. Describe the outside branch, name what is in it, and do not recruit. That is a real constraint on tone, and this file's argument — that "outside" is a technique available to anyone who can play the changes — is actually *helpful* here: it lets the page take the outside seriously without asking the reader to prefer it.
6. **Add one honest sentence about the corpus's own limits.** The strongest evidence for the moving boundary comes from three sources and is consistent. But the *quote-level* evidence for individual musicians using the term is thin: the corpus has **no verified Monk quotation, no verified Dolphy self-description, and no verified Taylor statement** on inside/outside. The page should not put invented-sounding musician quotations in musicians' mouths. See §9.

## 7. The other meaning: inside/outside as market and canon position

There is a second, entirely separate use of the words, and it is worth naming precisely because it so often contaminates the first. In this use, **"inside" means established, mainstream, central, well-distributed and well-liked; "outside" means marginal, unprofitable, excluded or unrecognised.** This is a claim about a record business and a culture, not about chord changes.

**Evidence that the two senses are distinct and can come apart:**

- **Being "outside" did not mean being unheard.** The 1960 *TIME* report has "insiders of the cool world" flocking to the Five Spot to hear the man they were not supposed to be able to follow, while he was "enjoying his first real success." **[TIME, 27 June 1960]**
- **Being "inside" did not mean being loved.** *Jazz Journal*, January 1961, on Paul Desmond — the most commercially successful cool-jazz instrumentalist of the decade: "**This is a pleasant, inoffensive, but essentially trivial record**" from *Jazz Journal*'s Michael Shera. **[https://jazzjournal.co.uk/2021/01/24/paul-desmond-friends/]** Inside the industry, dismissed by a critic.
- **Being "outside" did not mean being experimental.** Duhaime documents the label attached to albums whose *titles* were marketable: *Outward Bound*, *Out There*, *Out to Lunch* — "out" as a **marketing** category, on a label called New Jazz that was "an obvious reference to 'the new thing'." **[Duhaime]**
- **The canon-critical sense is an argument about power, and the corpus says so plainly.** Duhaime: jazz purists "made epistemological others out of avant-garde musicians of the 1960s," and "critics and musicians started to define jazz by what it is not." **[Duhaime, citing Somers and Gibson (1994, p. 38) on social groups constructing others "to consolidate a cohesive self-identity and collective project"]** And Klotz shows the classification machinery working on the other side of the ledger: the classical vocabulary applied to cool jazz "ultimately determined what sounds passed as white in a typically black genre." **[Klotz, *Daedalus*, 2019]**
- **The 94:6 canon arithmetic is a market fact, not a musical one.** The thesis's §9 count (238 "inside" albums to 16 "outside") measures a *collection*, and the thesis already flags that four of the sixteen are ECM releases filed by genre rather than by lane, and that *The Ballad of the Fallen* is "a solo memorial album filed as avant-garde." **[thesis §9]** **Those are filing decisions, not musical properties.** If the page uses the 94:6 figure at all, it should be honest that it describes the collection and the filing rules, not jazz.

**Recommendation for the page:** use a different word for the second sense — "central and marginal," "the canon and the periphery" — and reserve "inside/outside" for the harmonic/formal sense, with one explicit paragraph saying the two are often confused and that they are not the same. This is the single most valuable contribution this file makes, because the two senses failing to track each other is a *demonstrated* fact (Desmond inside and dismissed; Coleman outside and packed rooms), not a theoretical worry.

## 8. CONTESTED AND UNSURE

Marked in rough order of how much they should worry John.

1. **The "second coming of bebop" pattern is my synthesis, not a sourced claim.** That Dolphy and Coltrane in 1960–62 received the same charge Parker got in the 1940s rests on Duhaime's single sentence ("It was like a second coming of bebop, critics didn't know what to call it") **[Duhaime]** plus the separate documented fact that Parker was considered "out" in the 1940s **[Levine]**. The two are individually sourced; **the pattern I have drawn between them is my inference.** It is a strong inference and I believe it, but the page should present it as an argument, not a finding.
2. **No verified quotation exists in this corpus from Monk, Dolphy or Taylor on inside/outside.** This is a real gap, not a rhetorical hedge. All three are named in the brief; the corpus documents that the term was *applied to* Dolphy and Taylor, and documents Monk's role in bebop, but contains no attributable instance of any of the three using the word. **Any 🟡 item above must be verified before publication, and the page should not imply otherwise.** Recommended retrievals: Monk interviews in *DownBeat* or the Jazz Foundation transcripts; Duhaime's "Eric Dolphy & Outness" for its Dolphy quotations (I extracted only part of it); the Cecil Taylor oral history at Penn's Electronic Poetry Center (`writing.upenn.edu/epc/`, retrieved as `raw/cecil-taylor-upenn.txt`, not yet searched on this point).
3. **Duhaime's essay is truncated in the corpus, and the truncation falls in the middle of his most load-bearing argument about Dolphy and Coleman.** The retrieved text breaks off at: "This is one of the reasons writers often put Eric Dolphy in the same category as Ornette Coleman, claiming that Dolphy was an 'apostle' of" **[Duhaime]**. I have therefore **not** claimed to know what Duhaime concludes about filing Dolphy with Coleman, and §5.2(viii) above should not be read as agreeing with him on that point. **Recover the full essay before the page makes an argument about Dolphy's filing.**
4. **Levine's page numbers (183, 184) come from Duhaime's footnotes, not from my own inspection.** I read Chapter 8's sample PDF directly and quote it verbatim above, so the *words* are verified; the page numbers I take on Duhaime's authority. Levine's exact page for the "Forty years / Cecil Taylor" sentence I have **not** confirmed.
5. **Gridley's page (307) comes from Duhaime's footnote to *Jazz Styles*, 10th ed.** I have not seen *Jazz Styles* itself. Duhaime's quotation is marked internally inconsistent in a way that matters: he cites "Gridley, Mark C., *Jazz Styles* 10th edition: 307" for the "very loose" passage and "Gridley, *Jazz:* 307" for the Armstrong/Parker/Tristano passage, and Duhaime renders Gridley's full formulation as "it (a) led some outsiders to assume..." in a way that suggests he is partly quoting around an elision. **The quotation is second-hand and should be checked against *Jazz Styles* before it goes on a page, especially since it is one of the project's two anchor findings.**
6. **The claim that the corpus contains no "Chamber of Jazz" scare is a claim about the *retrieved* corpus, not about the historiography.** I searched every file in `raw/`; the phrase appears in none. The "Chamber of Jazz" scare and the critical reception of Paul Desmond's *Easy Jazz* are things I know are discussed in the literature and that the project brief asserts — but **I have no retrieved source for either, and I have not written them into any file as fact.** See the *Contested* section of [`../01-genres/cool-jazz.md`](../genres/cool-jazz.md), item 3, and the same gap in the seams file. **This is the single most important unfilled hole in the current corpus** for the mid-1950s–early 1960s period.
7. **Two source files for the period are empty of usable text.** `raw/gds-playingoutside.txt` is titled "Playing Outside" (Georgetown Day School magazine) but the retrieved file contains only navigation and a "Confused Grasshopper" mascot image — **no article body**. `raw/dunton-westcoast.txt` and `raw/desmond-neoclassicism.txt` (both CerraJazz Substack) contain only paywall and cookie boilerplate, **no article text**. `raw/saxophone-journal-cool.txt` and `raw/msu-westcoast.txt` resolved to library "ask a librarian" landing pages. **A file called "Playing Outside" that contains no text is a suspicious retrieval failure** — someone should re-fetch it, because the title alone suggests it may contain a directly relevant treatment.
8. **The Keith Waters essay and the Wren article are both missing their public URLs in the corpus.** Waters' "Autumn Leaves" in the 1960s (`raw/waters-autumn-leaves.txt`) and Wren's "Jazz Standard as Archive" (`raw/rutgers-inside-outside.txt`, retrieved from `jjs.libraries.rutgers.edu`, which indicates *Journal of Jazz Studies* but from which no article-view URL was captured) are two of the three most valuable sources in this file and neither is properly citable as retrieved. Same for Duhaime's essay. **All three need clean URLs before publication.**
9. **The pirated-scan problem.** `deveaux-birthofbebop-pdfcoffee.txt`, `bebop-birth-pdfcoffee.txt` and `gioia-howtolisten.txt` are full texts of copyrighted books (DeVeaux, *The Birth of Bebop*; Gioia, *How to Listen to Jazz*) retrieved from `pdfcoffee.com`, a document-sharing site, with OCR-level artefacts in the text. I have used Gioia once, for a listening method, and cited it with an explicit caveat and the LCCN. **No load-bearing claim in this file rests on either pirated text.** DeVeaux's *The Birth of Bebop* is central to the bebop historiography and is currently held only as a pirated scan; **someone with library access should obtain the legitimate edition**, because the birth-date question in [`../01-genres/bebop.md`](../genres/bebop.md)'s *Contested* section turns on it.
10. **Two of the six figures the brief asked about are thinly covered in the *musician's* sense and thickly covered in the *scene* sense.** Rollins is well covered as a user of the term in a career sense (and charmingly as a user of it in the outdoor sense). Ornette is well covered as a period *object* of the term and thinly as a user of it — I have no verified Coleman sentence using "inside" or "outside." His 1996 interview is about harmolodics and about his label relationships, not about the inside/outside distinction.
11. **Whether Levine's "free/atonal" clause should be read as a fifth sense or as a boundary marker.** He lists playing "free" or atonal as one *meaning* of "outside," then immediately says Taylor and Braxton are "outside the scope of this book." Those two statements are in tension: either atonal playing is a kind of outside playing, or it is outside the *terminology* as well as the technique. **The corpus does not resolve it**, and my §5.2(ii) leans on the tension deliberately. A careful reader could reasonably take the other side.
