# Inside/Outside: does the name work, and what should the page do with it?

**Status:** advisory. Written by space-bunny, 2026-09-28, for John.
**Sources of authority in this file:** (a) my own primary-source research, cited inline;
(b) a direct consultation with **mccoy** on 2026-09-28, whose advisory is preserved
verbatim at `../raw/consultation-mccoy-2026-09-28.txt` and credited below as **[mccoy]**.
Where the two agree, that convergence is itself evidence. Where they differ, I say so.

---

## 1. Short answer

**The name works — but it will mislead on fusion, and it will collapse on itself if you
push it hard enough.**

"Inside/outside" is not a metaphor you have invented. It is a genuine term of art in the
music, with a documented lineage. That is a gift: the page's title is a piece of musician's
vocabulary, and a curious reader who later meets the phrase in a liner note will already
understand it.

The trouble is that the word is **relatively defined** — what counts as "outside" is
decided by whoever is inside at the time. And **fusion, the third member of your catch-all,
is not actually outside at all.** Both problems are fixable, and how you fix them is most
of the value of this file.

---

## 2. The name is real musician's vocabulary — sourced

This is the foundation, and it is solid.

- Mark C. Gridley's *Jazz Styles* uses **"out music"** as a period term for the 1960s
  developments, and treats the era's category names as **"very loose"** — loose in a way
  that "led some outsiders to assume … that the different musicians of this period had more
  in common than they actually did." Gridley further notes that calling the 1960s the *only*
  avant-garde period is false, since early jazz by Armstrong, bebop by Parker, and cool
  jazz by Tristano "could justifiably be called 'avant-garde.'" **[Justin Duhaime,
  "Eric Dolphy & Outness," citing Gridley, *Jazz Styles*, 10th ed., p. 307]**
- Mark Levine's *The Jazz Theory Book* has a chapter titled **"Playing 'Outside'"**, and
  defines it as meaning "several different things": playing notes that are not in the chord,
  stretching one chord into another, playing something recognisable in a different key, or
  playing "free"/atonal with no chord structure at all. **[Levine, *The Jazz Theory Book*,
  p. 183, via Duhaime]**
- Miles Davis himself called the new music **"the way-out thing."**
  **[Davis & Troupe, *The Autobiography of Miles Davis*, 2005 ed., p. 272, via Duhaime]**
- The period had a whole **vocabulary of names** for the same shifting territory: "the new
  thing," avant-garde jazz, "out music," "anti-jazz" (John Tynan, *DownBeat*, 4/12/1962),
  "fire music," and after Ornette Coleman's *Free Jazz: A Collective Improvisation* (1960),
  the word "free" became a catch-all for all of it. **[Duhaime, as above; see also
  Wikipedia, "Free jazz"]**

That last point is the most useful thing in this section. **"Free jazz" was itself a
branding accident** — one album title that stuck and swallowed everything around it. You
are planning to do something similar with your catch-all bucket, and this is the historical
precedent for why that is a bad idea. Say so on the page; it is a gift of an opening.

**Independent confirmation [mccoy]:** "playing outside" is a real term of art — improvising
away from the chord changes and resolving back — and by the 1960s "outside" attached to free
jazz as a scene label. mccoy's read is that the name maps onto a genuine fault line: the
canon's centre of gravity improvises *inside* song form and harmony, while free jazz and
the avant-garde deliberately release those constraints.

## 3. Problem one: the word is relative, and the line moves

This is the finding I think matters most, and it is sourced.

Levine, quoted by Duhaime, is explicit: **"what's considered outside is subjective and
changeable. What you hear as 'outside' someone else will hear as 'inside,' and vice versa.
Bird was considered 'out' by many musicians in the 1940s, as was Coltrane in the 1960s."**
**[Levine, via Duhaime]**

Read that again with the page in mind. **Charlie Parker was "out" in the 1940s.** The
"inside" tradition you are about to describe — the one that begins the story and carries the
canon's 94% — was itself the radical, unacceptable, too-fast music that the establishment
complained about, twenty years before free jazz existed.

Two consequences, and you can take whichever you like:

**Option A — use it as the page's argument.** The line between inside and outside has
moved at least twice, and every generation has been embarrassed by its own parents. Nothing
is permanently outside. The page becomes a story about a boundary that keeps moving, which
is true, sourced, and quietly subversive — and it inoculates the reader against the idea
that any of this is a settled taxonomy.

**Option B — use it as a hedge.** State once, early, that "inside" and "outside" are
relative terms, and then use them anyway for convenience. Lower risk, less reward.

I lean to A. It is the more interesting page and it is defensible from the sources.

**A caution worth taking:** "outside" never meant *marginal* or *obscure*. Ornette
Coleman's *The Shape of Jazz to Come* (1959) was famous on arrival; plenty of inside
records were obscure. Do not let "outside" slide into "fringe" or "failed" on the page —
a reader who takes that impression will come away with a false picture of the music
business. **[mccoy]**

### And a qualification against my own argument

The research came back with a fact that cuts against the inside/outside binary more than I
have allowed for: **cool jazz is itself an influence on free jazz**, via Jimmy Giuffre's
later acoustic work with the Jimmy Giuffre 3 (1956) and the Seal Records period. The
supposedly "inside" West Coast school fed material directly into the 1960s experimental
wing. This is documented in `01-genres/cool-jazz.md`.

I am not revising the recommendation — the three-axis structure survives it, and the
harmonic/formal reading of the title is still the right one. But the page should not imply
a clean one-way flow from inside to outside, because the lineage runs both ways and a
knowledgeable reader will know it. The honest version of the claim is narrower and still
good: **the categories are porous and the influence ran in both directions**, which is
another reason to frame the page as overlapping conversations rather than a march of styles.

A second, sharper version of the same point: the musicians themselves often refused the
label. Eric Dolphy — the figure most often filed as the prime example of "outside" playing —
said: *"I think of my playing as tonal… I don't think I 'leave the changes' as the expression
goes, every note I play has some reference to the chords of the piece."* Buddy Collette
disagreed: "He was pretty outside." Justin Duhaime's conclusion: *"Outness is ultimately a
construction and it's not one that Dolphy used to describe himself."*
**[Duhaime, "Eric Dolphy & Outness"]**

That is worth a line on the page. The most famous "outside" player declined the term, and
the disagreement about him is still unresolved.

## 4. Problem two: fusion is not "outside" — and this is the big one

**This is the correction you most need, and both mccoy and I arrived at it independently.**

The instinct to group free jazz, fusion and avant-garde into one "outside" bucket is
tempting because all three are the things that sound *different*. But they are different
along **different axes**, and fusing them teaches a false lesson.

**Fusion is electric and groove-driven, but it is overwhelmingly harmonic *insider*.**
Modal vamps, riffs, ostinatos, backbeats. *Bitches Brew* sounds radical because of its
**texture, timbres, studio construction and volume** — not because the players abandon the
chord changes. That is mccoy's argument, and it is musically correct.

So the honest axes are **three, and they cross**:

| Axis | Inside pole | Outside pole | Who sits where |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Harmonic / formal** — do you improvise over a song's changes? | bebop, cool, hard bop, post bop, modal, soul jazz | free jazz, much avant-garde | *This is the axis the title names.* |
| **Acoustic / electric** — amplified or not? | all of the above, unplugged | fusion, jazz-rock, jazz-funk, the 70s electric sound | *This is fusion's actual axis.* |
| **Composed / improvised** — is the form written down? | the head-and-changes tradition | AACM and European composed avant-garde, Coltrane's late work | *Cuts across both.* |

**Recommendation:** keep Inside/Outside as the spine of the page, but let it mean the
**harmonic and formal** fault line — which is the meaning the word actually has in the
music. Then let **fusion stand as its own section on its own axis**, "the electric turn" or
similar. mccoy's exact counsel: "If John wants a seventh section, my counsel is 'The
Outside' (free/avant, one section) and let fusion stand separately as 'The Electric Turn' —
different axis, different story."

The payoff is that this is *more* interesting, not less. A reader who is told that the
electric turn and the avant-garde are **two different revolutions that happened at the same
time** learns something true. A reader told there is one outside thing learns a category
that never existed.

**Corroboration from the canon itself [my analysis of `app/public/data/albums.json`]:** the
site's own taxonomy already splits what you were going to lump — `Free Jazz` (7), `Fusion`
(3), `Jazz-Rock` (2), `Jazz-Funk` (1), `Avant-Garde Jazz` (2), `European Jazz` (1), and
`ecm` as a *tag* rather than a style. The database has already made the distinction your
catch-all would erase.

## 5. Proposed page architecture

A shape, not a layout — you said design comes later, and I have not touched it.

**Opening — the two questions the page answers**
1. What are these words, and who invented them?
2. Why does anyone care which one a record is?

**Part 1 — Inside: the line that holds** (harmonic centre; the canon's 94%)
Bebop → Cool Jazz → Hard Bop → Post-Bop → Modal → Soul Jazz
In this order, because it is the order they were made.

**Part 2 — The Outside** (free jazz and the avant-garde — one axis, two arguments)
Free Jazz · Avant-Garde Jazz
With the Gridley caveat up front: these musicians had less in common than the label
suggests, and "avant-garde" as a name is loose.

**Part 3 — The Electric Turn** (fusion's own axis)
Fusion · Jazz-Rock · Jazz-Funk
Opening with: *this is a different argument, not a louder version of the same one.*

**Part 4 — The seams** (where the boxes fail)
The place to put everything in section 3 above, the mislabel traps, and the hinge records.

**Part 5 — Start here** (gateway records per genre)

Total: eight genres instead of seven, split on an axis that survives scrutiny. Everything
in your original brief is still present; nothing has been dropped.

## 6. Hinge albums — the records that make the boundary visible

A hinge record is one a reader can hold in each hand. These are the most useful thing on
the whole page, because they turn an abstraction into a listening experience.

Already in the canon, per mccoy, best first **[mccoy]**:

- **John Coltrane, *A Love Supreme* (Dec 1964) vs *Ascension* (June 1965)** — same leader,
  six months apart, from the most beloved inside-modal record in the canon to a full outside
  statement. mccoy's verdict: "the single best 'the line is a door, not a wall' exhibit."
  I would build the page's central hinge out of this pair.
- **Eric Dolphy, *Out to Lunch!* (1964)** — sounds free, is tightly composed. The hinge in
  one man, and the record that most reliably proves "free-sounding ≠ free."
- **Jackie McLean, *Destination Out* (1963)** — the title is the concept; a hard bopper
  stepping out.
- **Andrew Hill, *Point of Departure* (1964)** — Dolphy inside Hill's structures; again the
  title tells the story.
- **Sonny Rollins, *East Broadway Run Down* (1966)** — the most inside tenor giant of his
  generation playing with Cherry, Haden and Blackwell.
- **Ornette Coleman, *The Shape of Jazz to Come* (1959)** — inside melodic heads, outside
  solos. The door opening.
- **Charles Mingus, *Pithecanthropus Erectus* (1956)** — inside tradition, avant-garde
  gestures, predating the labels.
- **Miles Davis, *Bitches Brew* (1970)** — outside texture over inside vamps; the fusion
  hinge precisely because it fools people.

One more of my own, which cuts across all of the above — from the live canon export:
**Old and New Dreams, *Playing* (1980, ECM)** is filed under `Free Jazz`, but the group is
Gil Evans, Alan Skidmore, Jimmy Heath and Jack DeJohnette — a Miles Davis sidemen band,
hard-bop to its bones. A record that is inside by personnel and outside by label. Use it to
make the point that our filing categories are a convenience, not a fact.

## 7. Mislabel traps — the things a public explainer will otherwise get wrong

Straight from mccoy **[mccoy]**, and each one is a live risk for a page aimed at the public:

- **Kind of Blue is not bebop and not cool jazz** — it is the modal break from both.
- **A Love Supreme is not free jazz** — a composed, harmonically grounded suite. *Ascension*
  is the free one.
- **Bitches Brew is not free jazz** — studio-built fusion over grooves. (Nor, per section 4,
  is it properly "outside" at all.)
- **Birth of the Cool is the reaction *away* from bebop**, not bebop.
- **Out to Lunch is not free jazz** — composed post-bop with outside solos.
- **Getz/Gilberto is bossa nova**, adjacent to cool but not cool jazz proper.
- **"Avant-garde" is not a synonym for free jazz.** In the house taxonomy, avant-garde is
  the AACM compositional wing; free jazz is the Coleman/Trane/Ayler line. Worth stating
  plainly, because the two are constantly merged in popular writing.
- **ECM is a label, not a genre** — the "ECM sound" is a marketing category. mccoy's note:
  "say so gently." I agree on the gentleness; John holds this as a house rule (see §8), so
  the page should be accurate about it without picking a fight with anyone who doesn't.
- **Hard bop vs post bop**: mid-60s Blue Note gets lumped. *Speak No Evil* is post-bop, not
  hard bop.

mccoy's intelligence on John's standing positions **[mccoy]**. All of it is consistent
with what I read directly in `config/style-research/free-jazz.md` and

  anyway, on 2026-07-28, because he chose to *study* the idiom rather than dismiss it.
  **The page should therefore not advocate for the outside branch.** Describe it; do not
  recruit for it.
- Fusion is under an explicit **anti-drift guard** — the gate is open but he watches for the
  centre of gravity sliding electric. The taxonomy splitting fusion / jazz-rock / jazz-funk
  is deliberate.
- The canon is personal and Blue Note-centred: hard bop is the spine; the post-bebop-to-
  pre-fusion window is the heart. The window is now 1945–1985, with bebop admitted as
  foundation on 2026-09-24.
- He insists genres be defined by **musical mechanics, not marketing**, and treats ECM as a
  label tag, never a style — a house rule enforced in the DB.
- He keeps boundaries porous but wants the distinctions *named* — "hard bop vs soul jazz" is
  exactly the kind of soft edge he wants flagged as contested. This page is well suited to
  him: it can be porous *and* precise.
- **inf:** He values co-existence over succession. Eras overlap; the site's own Working page
  exists to show coming, going and overlap. **A page that reads as a tidy march of styles
  would grate on him.** Frame the genres as simultaneous conversations, not a queue.

That last one is the most useful single line in this file, and it reshapes the architecture
in §5. Sequence Part 1 chronologically, yes — but say explicitly, and early, that these
styles were all happening at once and that the boundaries were drawn afterwards by critics.

## 9. The canon-derived argument for the title (mine, from the live export)

I analysed the live published canon — `jazz-canon/app/public/data/albums.json`, 254 albums,
1945–1983:

| | Albums | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Inside** (bebop, cool, hard bop, post bop, modal, soul jazz) | 238 | 94% |
| **Outside** (free jazz, fusion, jazz-rock, jazz-funk, avant-garde, European) | 16 | 6% |

Full working, per-style album lists, and the hinge records in
[`../04-players/canon-inventory.md`](../canon/canon-inventory.md).

**The title is an accurate description of the collection's actual proportions, roughly
94:6.** That is a strong, checkable defence of the name, and it is worth putting on the page
in one plain sentence. It also sets expectations honestly: this is a canon that mostly holds
one family of music and is reaching, deliberately and self-consciously, toward the others.

One caveat found in the data, flagged for mccoy rather than for the page: four of the
sixteen outside albums are ECM releases filed by *genre* rather than by *lane* — *Conference
of the Birds*, *Offramp*, *The Ballad of the Fallen* and Old and New Dreams' *Playing*. The
free-jazz style module states the rule that ECM releases belong to the `ecm` style and must
be handed off. Either the rule is not enforced downstream or the export predates it. It does
not change the page, but the page should not lean on *The Ballad of the Fallen* — a solo
memorial album filed as avant-garde — as an example of anything.
