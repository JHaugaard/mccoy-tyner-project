# Free jazz, avant-garde jazz and fusion: three branches, not one "outside"

## Why this matters

A planned public page on jazzcanon.com proposes to gather free jazz, avant-garde jazz
and jazz fusion into a single "outside" catch-all, presented as one thing that happened
to jazz after bebop. This file exists to show that the bucket is not a simplification
but an error, and that the error is load-bearing.

**What the bucket actually asks a listener to accept.** To file the three together is to
tell a listener that they share an origin, an aim and a musical method. They do not:

- they do not **begin** at the same time — the span between them is roughly fifteen
  years, which is most of a generation;
- they do not **want** the same thing — one wanted to stop improvising over chord
  changes, one wanted to be taken seriously as composition, one wanted a bigger
  audience and a louder instrument;
- they do not even sit on the **same side** of the harmonic line the page's own
  "inside/outside" axis describes. Fusion is overwhelmingly *inside* the changes.
  Filing it as "outside" teaches the reader the opposite of the truth about the
  record most people know as fusion's landmark.

**What is gained by the bucket.** One thing: navigability. A reader who wants a
tolerable listening experience can be pointed at three sections rather than one, and
the page can offer a single "if this is hard, start here" paragraph. That is a real
pedagogical benefit and it is not nothing.

**What is lost.** Far more:

- The **chronology collapses.** The reader loses the fact that experimental practice
  was running *alongside* bebop from roughly 1955 — a contemporaneous conversation,
  not an aftermath. The era becomes a succession, and succession is the one story
  `thesis-and-page-architecture.md` refuses to tell ("co-existence over succession").
- The **harmonic lesson inverts.** *Bitches Brew* and *Head Hunters* get filed as
  outside. They are built on vamps, riffs and single-chord grounds. A reader who
  absorbs the page's filing will mis-hear them.
- **Genuinely different problems get presented as one aesthetic position.** The
  outside branches contain a live, unresolved argument — was going electric a
  betrayal? — and the bucket dissolves it into a mood.
- **A naming error becomes a teaching error.** "Avant-garde" is a house-category word
  in this project, defined as the AACM compositional wing; "free jazz" is the
  Coleman/Coltrane/Ayler line. Merging them retires a distinction the taxonomy exists
  to keep.
- And the **ECM-style trap** gets worse: once "outside" is a bucket, a record can be
  filed by which bucket it is nearer rather than by how it is built.

**The verdict in one line, argued in full below:** the free/avant material belongs
together (they are, per Wikipedia, "originally synonymous," and they share a scene);
fusion does not, and should be split off as **"The Electric Turn"** — McCoy's own
recommendation, recorded in `raw/consultation-mccoy-2026-09-28.txt`: *"If John wants a
seventh section, my counsel is 'The Outside' (free/avant, one section) and let fusion
stand separately as 'The Electric Turn' — different axis, different story."*

## Where each branch begins — the asymmetry is the argument

The single most important fact about these three branches is that **they are not
contemporaries.** The bucket implies they are; the record says otherwise.

### The avant-garde / experimental wing: alongside bebop, from about 1955

Wikipedia's *Avant-garde jazz* infobox dates the cultural origins to **mid-1950s
United States**, and the article body traces the practice back further still — to
Lennie Tristano's collective free improvisation on "Intuition" and "Diggression" of
1949 — while placing the genre's actual birth in the mid-to-late 1950s. The same
article makes the crucial structural point: avant-garde jazz was founded by musicians
who "rejected the conventions of bebop and post bop in an effort to blur the division
between the written and the spontaneous aspects of these genres."

Read that carefully. It means the experimental wing was a *parallel stream*, not an
escalation. Bebop did not finish and then produce the avant-garde; both were running,
and they were arguing with each other. Wikipedia's *Jazz* style-origins infobox for
avant-garde jazz lists **jazz, bebop, musical modernism, avant-garde music and
20th-century classical music** — it declares itself a child of bebop.

The infrastructure followed immediately, and it is the same independent-label
ecosystem that will later carry free jazz: Cecil Taylor's *Jazz Advance* on Transition
(1956), Contemporary from 1958, Atlantic from 1959–60, George Russell's *Jazz in the
Space Age* (1960). The independent labels are part of the story rather than an
afterthought: Jubilee, Prestige/New Jazz, Savoy, Delmark (Bob Koester, 1953), and from
1963 ESP-Disk, founded by Bernard Stollman — whose **second release** was Albert
Ayler's *Spiritual Unity*, recorded 10 July 1964. That single fact is a good
compression of the whole branch: the avant-garde built its own distribution because
the major labels would not carry it.

### Free jazz: the *name* is 1960–61, the practice is older

Free jazz proper is named from **Ornette Coleman's *Free Jazz: A Collective
Improvisation*** — recorded 21 December 1960, released September 1961 on Atlantic.
Coleman had been leading small groups since 1958. So the branch has a clean naming
date that is *later than the practice it names* by years.

Wikipedia is also explicit that the terms were originally near-synonymous: "One of the
earliest developments within avant-garde jazz was that of free jazz, and the two terms
were originally synonymous." That sentence is why this file treats free jazz and
avant-garde as a *pair* and fusion as the odd one out, rather than as three equals.

### Fusion: it does not exist until roughly 1968–70, and it is a child of the rock market

This is the load-bearing chronology, and it is the part the bucket most badly
distorts. AllMusic, quoted in Wikipedia's *Jazz fusion*, states that **"until around
1967, the worlds of jazz and rock were nearly completely separate."** The same
article's origin story:

- When **John Coltrane died in 1967**, rock was the most popular music in America, and
  *DownBeat* went so far as a headline: **"Jazz as We Know It Is Dead."**
- Guitarist **Larry Coryell** — "sometimes called the godfather of fusion" — described
  his generation: **"We loved Miles but we also loved the Rolling Stones."** His Free
  Spirits recorded *Out of Sight and Sound* in 1966, released 1967.
- **Charles Lloyd** played rock and jazz at Monterey in 1966 with **Keith Jarrett** and
  **Jack DeJohnette** in the quartet, then adopted the trappings of the California
  psychedelic scene — the Fillmore West, the clothes, albums called *Dream Weaver* and
  *Forest Flower* — which were bestselling **jazz** albums in 1967.
- Per music journalist Zaid Mudhaffer, the term **"jazz fusion" was coined in a review
  of David Axelrod's *Song of Innocence* on its 1968 release.**
- Miles Davis wrote in his autobiography that in 1968–69 he was listening to **Jimi
  Hendrix, James Brown and Sly and the Family Stone.**

The landmark dates then land in quick succession: *In a Silent Way* (recorded
18 Feb 1969, released 30 Jul 1969), *Emergency!* (Sept 1969), *Bitches Brew* (recorded
Aug 1969, released 30 Mar 1970), *Mwandishi* (1971), *The Inner Mounting Flame* (1971),
*On the Corner* (Oct 1972), *Head Hunters* (Oct 1973), *Agharta* (Aug 1975).

### The chronology, stated as the point

| Branch | Earliest practice | Name/label date | Origin story |
|---|---|---|---|
| Avant-garde / experimental | 1949 (Tristano); genre mid-1950s | "new thing," mid-1950s onward | Rejects bebop and post-bop conventions from inside the tradition; borrows from musical modernism |
| Free jazz | 1958 (Coleman's small groups); Taylor's practice earlier | *Free Jazz*, rec. 21 Dec 1960, rel. Sept 1961 | Coleman/Taylor/coltrane/Ayler; a name coined for an ongoing practice |
| Fusion | 1966 (Coryell's Free Spirits, Charles Lloyd at Monterey) | Term "jazz fusion" coined 1968 (Mudhaffer) | Rock market. "Until around 1967 the worlds of jazz and rock were nearly completely separate" |

**By roughly 1970 all three are running simultaneously — but for entirely different
reasons, in different rooms, on different labels, aimed at different audiences.** That
is the fact the single bucket erases. As `01-genres/free-jazz.md` puts it in its
listening notes, citing Mark Gridley: these musicians "had less in common than the label
suggests." The page's own Working view — coming, going, overlap — cannot be drawn
correctly if the three are welded together.

## What each one wanted

### Free jazz: stop improvising over chord changes

The primary, load-bearing innovation is **harmonic**. Release the improviser from the
chord progression and the entire scaffolding of the bop soloist comes down. What the
listener loses, per `01-genres/free-jazz.md`, is the set of cues that let an ear stay
oriented: the tune, the bar count, the chord turn, the ride pattern's return. That is
a real cost and the branch's practitioners knew it.

The personnel: Coleman, Cecil Taylor, late Coltrane, Ayler, Pharoah Sanders, Sunny
Murray. Wikipedia places much of Sun Ra's 1960s output inside free jazz while noting
he insisted everything was written and that his writing was freer than the "freedom
boys'" — which the free-jazz file treats as "the strongest evidence that the label is a
poor instrument."

### Avant-garde jazz: structure, and standing as composition

Wikipedia draws the distinction explicitly, and the house taxonomy adopts it: avant-garde
jazz "lacks free jazz's thoroughly improvised nature and is either fully or partially
composed," and it "emphasizes structure and organization by the use of composed
melodies, shifting but nevertheless predetermined meters and tonalities, and
distinctions between soloists and accompaniment."

So the avant-garde wing wanted something adjacent to but **not the same as** free jazz:
not the *removal* of structure but the **ownership** of structure by composers who
happen to be jazz musicians. Its institutional wing is the **AACM** (Association for
the Advancement of Creative Musicians) in Chicago — Muhal Richard Abrams, Anthony
Braxton, Roscoe Mitchell, Hamid Drake, the Art Ensemble of Chicago — whose members
"tended towards eclecticism." Its European lineages run through **Globe Unity
Orchestra** in Berlin and the labels **BYG Actuel** (Paris, 1967–73), **Black Saint**
(1975) and **Soul Note** (1979) in Italy, and **India Navigation** (founded 1972, Bob
Cummins) in New York. In Los Angeles, **Horace Tapscott's PAPA's Arkestra and UGMAA**;
later, **Kamasi Washington**. The Black Arts Movement overlaps it directly: Amiri
Baraka recorded spoken word with the New York Art Quartet ("Black Dada Nihilismus,"
1964, ESP) and with Sunny Murray ("Black Art," 1965, Jihad).

### Fusion: groove, electricity, and a market

Wikipedia's own framing of fusion's virtues is telling: the pioneers "emphasized
exploration, energy, electricity, intensity, virtuosity, and volume." The driving
instrument change is stated plainly — a fusion band "is less likely to use acoustic
piano and double bass, and more likely to use electric guitars, electric pianos,
organs, synthesizers, and bass guitar."

Fusion wanted an **audience**. That is not a pejorative; it is the reason fusion
exists, and it is the thing the avant-garde camp found unforgivable. The band names
tell the ambition: Weather Report, the Mahavishnu Orchestra, Tony Williams Lifetime,
Return to Forever.

### Did they want the same thing? No — and the argument is real

The genuine dispute **inside** the avant-garde camp about fusion is the most valuable
single thing in this file, because it is an argument rather than a category:

- **The capitulation argument.** Fusion used the electric instrument, the amplifier and
  the rock market to reach people; in doing so it accepted groove as a primary value and
  accepted a "jazz-funk" harmonic simplification — a single riff, a ground. Read
  against the free-jazz premise that the *harmonic release* was the point, this looks
  like the branch undoing itself from the inside. Wikipedia's *Avant-garde jazz*
  infobox does not even list fusion as a derivative form: it lists **free jazz and
  post-rock** as derivatives, and puts **jazz fusion** under "Other topics" — a related
  conversation, not a descendant. That is a small textual fact with a large
  implication: even the reference works do not file fusion as avant-garde's child.
- **The reach argument.** The same move put new listeners in front of Wayne Shorter,
  Chick Corea, Herbie Hancock, Keith Jarrett, Jaco Pastorius, Billy Cobham and Larry
  Coryell, at a scale the small-label avant-garde could not approach. On this view the
  electric turn is a distribution problem solved, and the accusation of capitulation
  mistakes marketing for a musical retreat.

**This file does not settle that argument, and neither should the page.** Report it.
The point for the page's architecture is narrower: the argument *only exists* if fusion
is treated as a separate thing that the avant-garde had a position about. Fold fusion
into the bucket and the argument disappears, because a thing cannot be in tension with
itself.

## The straddlers — why the categories are porous in fact

The most valuable evidence against the bucket is not the chronology or the definitions.
It is the personnel. The musicians who moved in *both* directions are not anomalies at
the edge; they are the load-bearing structure of the whole period, and the genres
survive as categories only because we have agreed to stop looking at them closely.

**The stragglers are not the boundary. They are the connective tissue.** A boundary is
where a category ends. These are the people who made both categories mean anything.

- **Weather Report** (founded 1970) — Joe Zawinul, Wayne Shorter, Miroslav Vitous,
  Alphonse Mouzon, with Jerome Harris and Fernando Saunders, and Jaco Pastorius.
  Columbia, ARP synthesisers, Fender Rhodes. Shorter came out of the Miles Davis
  quintet and the post-bop lineage; Zawinul out of the jazz-organ and free-jazz
  worlds. The band is a *personnel* argument against the bucket, made permanently
  audible.
- **The Mahavishnu Orchestra** — *The Inner Mounting Flame* (1971). Guitar, sitar and
  Indian raga, Larry Young's Minimoog, pitch-bend, psychedelic-rock dynamics. This is
  an *Indian-classical and rock* branch of the tree, not a free-jazz branch and not
  the same thing as Weather Report's synth-and-sax. Filing it beside Ayler and
  Taylor is a category error with completely different sources.
- **Tony Williams Lifetime** and **Return to Forever** — same period, same electric
  turn, different personnel and different market position again.
- **Miles Davis, *Mwandishi* (1971, Warner)** — the band that fused the electric turn
  to the exploratory line, and the reason the fusion/avant-garde split is so blurry
  in the middle. *On the Corner* (1972) and *Emergency!* (1969) go further and are
  the *extreme* cases in which there are no tunes and no chords — a useful reminder
  that the harmonic rule has exceptions at its own frontier.
- **Don Cherry** — the cleanest case of a musician who belongs to all three
  conversations. With **Codona**, he recorded with Gábor Szabó-adjacent Hungarian
  guitarist **László Gáspár**'s colleagues in the trio that ECM recorded in 1979, 1981
  and 1983: **Collin Walcott** (a New Thing/AACM-era figure) and **Naná Vasconcelos**
  (Brazilian percussion, a modal and folkloric path entirely). Cherry also led
  **Orgone** and **Mosaic**. This one band is a worked refutation of the bucket.
- **Keith Jarrett** — his ECM trajectory is the clearest single arc that runs from
  inside (the Charles Lloyd quartet at Monterey, 1966) through the electric turn to
  the acoustic free-jazz wing (*Solo Concerts*, and the ECM records), and he does it
  without ever being a member of a different genre each time.
- **Jack DeJohnette** — in the Charles Lloyd quartet at Monterey in 1966, *inside*
  fusion's origin story, and simultaneously a free-jazz player on ECM.
- **The AACM** — not a straddler so much as a *bridge institution*: Abrams, Braxton,
  Mitchell, Drake and the Art Ensemble of Chicago take techniques from the European
  free-jazz scene and the Black Arts Movement back into an American jazz context and
  hand them on to *Kamasi Washington* and the present.
- **Old and New Dreams, *Playing* (1980, ECM)** — **the single cleanest exhibit of
  "inside personnel, outside filing."** The band is Gil Evans, Alan Skidmore, Jimmy
  Heath and Jack DeJohnette: a Miles Davis sidemen's group. The canon files the album
  under Free Jazz. The personnel say the opposite, and the personnel are the evidence
  you can check. If the page's bucketing is going to be trustworthy, this album is
  the test case, and it fails the bucket.

**The general point.** If the categories were walls, the personnel graph would have
three components. It has one large component with a great deal of overlap, and the
labels are drawn across it by critics, marketing and record-company A&R, not by the
players. The categories remain *useful* — but only as a description of dominant aims
and typical sound, held with an explicit awareness that individuals traverse them.
`thesis-and-page-architecture.md` says exactly this: "outside" never meant marginal;
*Shape of Jazz to Come* was famous on arrival and plenty of inside records were obscure.

**A second crack in the binary.** Per `01-genres/cool-jazz.md` in this corpus, cool
jazz influenced free jazz, via **Jimmy Giuffre**. The canonical "inside" style is one
of the *inputs* to the branch the bucket calls outside. The inside/outside line is
already porous from its far side, not just its near side.

## The harmonic point — why fusion is inside, and deserves its own axis

This is the section the page most needs, because it is the one that is *falsifiable*:
it is a claim about the notes.

**The claim.** Free jazz released the improviser from the chord changes. Fusion, in
overwhelming majority, did the opposite. It kept a harmonic ground and simplified it.

**The evidence, from the reference account itself.** Wikipedia's *Jazz fusion*,
arrangements section: fusion "arrangements vary in complexity. **Some employ
groove-based vamps fixed to a single key or a single chord with a simple, repeated
melody.** Others use elaborate chord progressions, unconventional time signatures, or
melodies with counter-melodies, in a similar fashion to progressive rock. These
arrangements, whether simple or complex, typically include improvised sections that
can vary in length, **much like in other forms of jazz.**"

And jazz-funk, the groove-first pole of the style: "a simple structure based around
one or two riffs, and a harmonic structure that allows musicians to improvise."

Both descriptions are *inside* descriptions. A vamp fixed to a single key, a riff
repeated over a static ground, a single chord — these are the mechanisms of *inside*
playing, and the inside/outside axis is precisely the axis on which the free-jazz
branch was defined. What has changed is **texture, amplification, timbre, studio
construction and market**. *Bitches Brew* sounds extreme because of what it is made
of, not because the playing abandons the changes.

**The honest exceptions, which strengthen rather than weaken the point.** The extreme
cases exist and they are famous: *Emergency!* and *On the Corner*, where there are no
tunes and no chord changes. They are best read as the frontier of the electric turn —
the point at which the vamp dissolves into pure groove-and-timbre — and even there the
line is one of *continuity with* the static-ground practice, not a break from it. Fusion
is not uniformly inside. It is *predominantly* inside, with an avant-garde wing, which
is a completely different shape from a branch that is defined by being outside.

**Therefore.** Fusion's operative axis is not inside↔outside. It is
**acoustic↔electric**, with a second axis of **texture↔harmony** (where the music puts
its structural energy). Filing fusion under "outside" gets the axis wrong, and the
consequence is that a reader who takes the page's word for it will hear *Head Hunters*
as a rejection of harmony when it is a very sophisticated piece of work *on* harmony.
McCoy's line in the consultation is the crisp version: "Bitches Brew sounds wild because
of texture and studio construction, not because the playing abandons changes. If the
page lets readers file fusion under 'outside,' it teaches the wrong lesson."

**The house taxonomy already agrees.** The house rule, enforced in the DB, is that
genres are defined by musical mechanics and that ECM is a label tag, never a style. A
bucket that files fusion by *mood* breaks the project's own definitional rule. ECM is
the sharpest case: founded in **1969** by Karl Egger, Manfred Eicher and Manfred
Scheffner, with the motto drawn from a 1971 *Coda* review — **"the most beautiful sound
next to silence"** — ECM is a *label* that happened to release Kenny Wheeler, Keith
Jarrett, Codona, Old and New Dreams and much of the free-jazz wing. It is not a genre,
and a bucket that lets a label stand in for a genre will eventually file by logo.

## What the outside branch gave rock

The bucket has one real claim in its favour: these musicians did change something
outside jazz, and the changes are concrete.

**From the avant-garde / experimental wing:**

- **Improvisation as a structural principle.** Not decoration on a groove — the method
  by which the material is generated. This is the single most consequential export, and
  it went everywhere.
- **Instrumental music as serious composition.** The AACM and its European counterparts
  (Globe Unity Orchestra in Berlin; BYG Actuel in Paris 1967–73; Black Saint and Soul
  Note in Italy) established that a jazz player could hold a composer’s job. Post-rock
  and contemporary-classical improvised music are direct descendants — Wikipedia's
  *Avant-garde jazz* infobox lists **post-rock** as a derivative form, and does not
  list fusion.
- **Expanded timbre and extended technique** as ordinary practice: prepared and
  detuned instruments, overblown reed work, percussive treatment of everything.
- **Tape and electronics** entering the improvising musician’s toolkit.

**From free jazz specifically:** the *Sound* of large-group acoustic intensity, and
the demonstration that a piece could be twenty minutes of energy-shape with no tune —
`01-genres/free-jazz.md`'s note that a free-jazz solo is "organised by energy, dynamics
and density rather than by phrasing" describes a method that mapped cleanly onto
British and European improvisation scenes.

**Reactions from inside the tradition, attributed carefully.** The bucket is supposed
to be where jazz went *away*; these are the contemporaneous responses that show the
inside was not a monolith either.

- **Miles Davis on hearing Eric Dolphy.** James Moore's account records Miles's
  reaction to Dolphy's playing in terms like "ridiculous," "sad," and "bad." Whatever
  the occasion, it is evidence that musicians at the centre of the canon heard the
  Dolphy–Coltrane wing as an assault rather than an increment.
- **Thelonious Monk on Ornette Coleman** — the assessment that there was "nothing
  beautiful" in it, that it was "playing loud and slurring." The two complaints are
  diagnostic: *loud* is a texture complaint, *slurring* is a tonal/phrasing complaint.
  Monk was not objecting to the market or the politics; he was objecting to the sound.
  That is a musical objection, and it belongs on a page that claims to argue about
  musical mechanics.
- **Mark Gridley's warning** that the era's loose category names "led some outsiders to
  assume ... that the different musicians of this period had more in common than they
  actually did." This is the most useful single citation in the file, because it is an
  authority inside the tradition making the bucketing argument rather than a critic
  from outside it.

And the flip side, which belongs here too because it is sourced and it cuts the other
way: **Charles Lloyd's *Dream Weaver* and *Forest Flower* (1967) were bestselling jazz
albums.** *DownBeat* began covering rock in 1967. Whether you call that capture or
reach, it is the same fact — and it is the fact the "outside was marginal" reading
cannot survive. `thesis-and-page-architecture.md` makes the same point about
*Shape of Jazz to Come*: outside never meant marginal.

## The honest verdict

**The single "outside" bucket should be split into two things, and only two:**

1. **The Outside** — free jazz and avant-garde jazz, treated as a pair (the reference
   works treat them as originally synonymous, and they shared a scene, a set of
   labels, a period and a set of arguments), with the internal distinction between the
   improvisation-led line and the composition-led line preserved *inside* the section
   rather than dissolved. Axis: **harmonic freedom, inside↔outside.**
2. **The Electric Turn** — fusion, with its own axis: **acoustic↔electric**, plus
   **texture↔harmony** as a second axis, and the explicit note that its arrangements are
   predominantly inside the changes (vamps fixed to a single key or a single chord,
   riffs, improvised sections much like other forms of jazz). *Bitches Brew* for the
   landmark, *Head Hunters* for the groove-first listener.

**The argument against the bucket, in the order a reader will find it persuasive:**

1. **Chronology.** The experimental wing is contemporaneous with bebop from c.1955;
   free jazz is named in 1960–61; fusion does not exist until c.1968–70. "Until around
   1967, the worlds of jazz and rock were nearly completely separate." A bucket for
   three things that begin fifteen years apart is not a category, it is a decade.
2. **Harmony.** Fusion is mostly *inside*. This is the falsifiable part, and it is the
   part that would teach a reader something they can use at the keyboard.
3. **Aims.** Release the harmony / own the composition / reach the audience. Three
   different projects, and the disagreement between the second and the third is an
   argument worth keeping visible rather than dissolving.
4. **Personnel.** Weather Report, the Mahavishnu Orchestra, Don Cherry's Codona, Keith
   Jarrett, Jack DeJohnette, and Old and New Dreams' *Playing* are all load-bearing
   links *between* the categories. The categories are porous in fact, not just in
   theory, which means the bucket does not become more useful by suppressing the
   overlap — it becomes less honest.
5. **Taxonomy hygiene.** "Avant-garde" is a house word for the AACM compositional wing;
   ECM is a label, not a style. The bucket is the mechanism by which both errors enter
   a public page.

**What the page gains from the split:** a reader who wants a single door into the
outside material still gets one door. What it loses: nothing except a false economy.

**One last framing note, consistent with the project's stated preference for
co-existence over succession.** None of this says the three branches are unrelated, and
this file does not argue that they should be walled off from one another. It says they
are *three different conversations running at the same time*, and that a reader can
only follow the conversations if the page names them separately. The Working page's
coming/going/overlap view is the right picture; the single bucket is a picture of one
line running through a period that had several.

   sourcing in this file.** Both were supplied to this file as banked, and they are
   widely repeated in the literature. I could **not** relocate the underlying source
   documents during this pass — no "James Moore" attribution, no "ridiculous/sad/bad"
   string, and no "nothing beautiful / loud and slurring" string appears anywhere under
   `research/genre-research/`, including `raw/pod-book-cooks-lee.txt`, which is the
   natural home for a Monk-on-Coleman quotation and does not contain one. **Before
   publication, either bank the primary source or cut both reactions.** Do not publish
   them on the strength of this file's characterisation. The *Gridley* caution and the
   Charles Lloyd sales figures are solidly sourced and carry the section without them.
2. **The exact date of the naming of *Free Jazz*.** Recorded 21 December 1960, released
   September 1961. Both are given in the banked material and they are not in conflict —
   but a public page should say which it means by "1961," and "recorded 1960, released
   1961" is the honest phrasing.
3. **Whether the *Spiritual Unity* date is 1964.** Given here as recorded 10 July 1964
   (ESP-Disk's second release, after its 1963 founding). `01-genres/free-jazz.md`
   carries the Giddins account of the $500 advance. The corpus does not settle whether
   the widely cited 1964 release date is the recording or the release.
4. **How "far avant-garde" is from free jazz.** Wikipedia is clear on paper
   (composed vs. thoroughly improvised). In practice the boundary is a spectrum —
   Dolphy is the standing counter-example, sounding free and playing composed material.
   The house taxonomy splits them deliberately; the banked material supports the split
   as a *definitional* choice, not as a clean historical separation.
5. **"Third stream" is not used in this file and that is a decision, not an oversight.**
   `raw/Third_stream.txt` is banked. The banked material does not clearly establish
   whether third stream (the Gunther Schuller / Yusef Lateef / Ran Blake line, and its
   later academic wing) should be treated as a fourth conversation or as a cross-cutting institutional category. This file's
 three-way split does not have room for it, and the omission is a hole. Flagged
 rather than resolved.
6. **Old and New Dreams' filing.** *Playing* (1980, ECM) being filed Free Jazz in the
   canon while consisting of a Miles Davis sidemen's group is asserted here from the
   canon's own filing plus the personnel. The banked material supports both halves; it
   does not contain a source explicitly *commenting* on the mismatch, so this is the
   file's own inference from two banked facts rather than a quotation.
7. **The "predominantly inside" claim about fusion is strong but not exhaustive.** The
   reference account itself says arrangements "vary in complexity" and that *some* use
   "elaborate chord progressions"; the extreme cases (*Emergency!*, *On the Corner*)
   have no chords at all. The word doing the work is "overwhelmingly," and that is a
   judgment about the corpus as a whole, not a statistic the banked material contains.
