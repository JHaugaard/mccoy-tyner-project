01The verdict, in short

The name works — but it will mislead on fusion, and it collapses if pushed too hard

“Inside/outside” is not a metaphor John invented. It is a genuine term of art in the music with a documented lineage, which makes the page title a piece of working musician’s vocabulary rather than a slogan. That is a real gift: a reader who later meets the phrase in a liner note will already understand it.

Two problems, both fixable, and the fixing is most of the value here. First, the word is relatively defined — what counts as outside is decided by whoever is inside at the time, and Charlie Parker was once on the wrong side of it. Second, and more seriously, fusion is not outside at all. It is electric and groove-driven but harmonically it stays inside, and filing it in an “outside” bucket teaches the wrong lesson.

My recommendation: keep Inside/Outside as the page’s spine, define it strictly as the harmonic and formal fault line, and split fusion out as its own section on its own axis — the electric turn.

02Why the name is not a metaphor

Four documented anchors, each with a source I actually retrieved this session.

AnchorEvidenceSource
“Out music” is a period term Mark Gridley’s Jazz Styles uses it for the 1960s developments. Duhaime, Eric Dolphy & Outness (2011), citing Gridley p. 307
It had many rival names “The new thing,” “out music,” “anti-jazz” (John Tynan, DownBeat, 4/12/1962), “fire music,” and after Ornette Coleman’s Free Jazz (1960) the word “free” became a catch-all for all of it. Duhaime, as above
Miles said it himself Miles Davis called the new music “the way-out thing.” Davis & Troupe, Autobiography, 2005 ed., p. 272, via Duhaime
There is a textbook chapter Mark Levine’s The Jazz Theory Book has a chapter titled Playing “Outside”, defining it as playing notes not in the chord, stretching a chord into another, playing in a different key — or playing free with no chord structure at all. Levine p. 183, via Duhaime
The last item in that table is the gift and the trap in one. “Free jazz” became a genre name because of an album title. It is a branding accident. The page is about to do something structurally similar with its own catch-all bucket — which is an argument for saying so plainly, in public, rather than repeating the mistake.

And here is the problem with the word

“What’s considered outside is subjective and changeable. What you hear as ‘outside’ someone else will hear as ‘inside,’ and vice versa. Bird was considered ‘out’ by many musicians in the 1940s, as was Coltrane in the 1960s.”
Mark Levine, The Jazz Theory Book, p. 183, quoted in Duhaime

Read that with the page in mind. Charlie Parker was “out” in the 1940s. The inside tradition the page is about to describe — the one carrying the canon’s 94% — was itself the radical, too-fast, unacceptable music that the establishment complained about, two decades before free jazz existed.

That gives John a real choice, and both options are defensible:

  • Option A, my recommendation. Make the moving boundary the page’s argument. The line has moved at least twice and every generation is embarrassed by its parents. Nothing is permanently outside. This is true, sourced, quietly subversive, and it inoculates the reader against thinking any of this is a settled taxonomy.
  • Option B. State once, early, that the terms are relative, then use them anyway for convenience. Lower risk, less reward.
One thing the page must not imply: that “outside” meant marginal or obscure. It did not. Ornette Coleman’s The Shape of Jazz to Come (1959) was famous on arrival, while plenty of inside records were forgotten. A reader who takes “outside” to mean “failed” leaves with a false picture of the music business. — mccoy

03Three axes, not one — and this is the important correction

The instinct to group free jazz, fusion and avant-garde into one outside bucket is tempting, because all three are the things that sound different. But they are different along different axes, and fusing them teaches a false lesson.

Fusion is overwhelmingly harmonic insider. Modal vamps, riffs, ostinatos, backbeats. Bitches Brew sounds radical because of its texture, timbres, studio construction and volume — not because the players abandon the chord changes. That is mccoy’s argument, and it is musically correct.

Axis 1 — harmonic & formal

Do you improvise over a song’s chord changes?

InsideBebop, cool, hard bop, post bop, modal, soul jazz
→
OutsideFree jazz, much of the avant-garde
This is the axis the title names — and the only one where the name is true. mccoy and space-bunny arrived here independently.

Axis 2 — acoustic & electric

Amplified, or not?

AcousticThe whole inside tradition, unplugged
→
ElectricFusion, jazz-rock, jazz-funk, the ’70s sound
This is fusion’s actual axis — a completely different argument, running alongside the first.

Axis 3 — composed & improvised

Is the form written down before the players arrive?

Heads & changesThe improvising mainstream, both inside and outside
→
ComposedThe AACM and European wings; Coltrane’s late work
Cuts clean across both — which is why “free jazz” and “avant-garde jazz” are not synonyms.

Keep Inside/Outside as the spine, but let it mean Axis 1 only — which is the meaning the word actually has in the music. Let fusion stand as its own section on Axis 2. mccoy’s exact counsel: “If John wants a seventh section, my counsel is ‘The Outside’ (free/avant, one section) and let fusion stand separately as ‘The Electric Turn’ — different axis, different story.”

The payoff is that this is more interesting, not less. A reader told that the electric turn and the avant-garde were two different revolutions happening at the same time learns something true. A reader told there is one outside thing learns a category that never existed.

The canon’s own database has already made this distinction. It does not have a single outside style — it splits the territory into Free Jazz, Fusion, Jazz-Rock, Jazz-Funk, Avant-Garde Jazz and European Jazz, and treats ECM as a tag rather than a style at all. The taxonomy would erase a distinction the data has already drawn.

04One lens, not a frame

An earlier draft of this document argued that the Inside/Outside title was justified by the canon's own proportions. That argument has been withdrawn — and the reason is worth stating, because it is a better argument than the one it replaced.

238 albums — 94%
6%
Inside — bebop, cool, hard bop, post bop, modal, soul jazz Outside — 16 albums total
Do not build the page on this ratio. It is a snapshot of 254 albums on 2026-09-28, and it is about to stop being true. The canon is young and moving fast — the project was only opened to bebop in the week before this was written. In a year the 94:6 split could be 80:20, and any argument resting on it expires with it.

More importantly, it is the wrong kind of evidence. A page explaining jazz genres to people who have never heard of them cannot be justified by what one collector happens to own. That question — what should I tell these people to listen to? — is answered by listening, not by inventory. Filtering the recommendations through the canon would quietly narrow a universal question down to a private one.

So the title's warrant is the one that survives: inside/outside is documented musician's vocabulary, with a textbook chapter, a period label and Miles Davis's own phrase behind it. That does not expire. The canon ratio remains useful in one narrow way — as evidence that the site's own filing categories are porous, which the records in §06 demonstrate directly — and that is how it is used now.

What the export still tells us, used carefully

  • The site has no styles outside the seven-genre frame. Twelve primary styles, all mapping onto John’s seven, three of them being splits of the catch-all. The frame is comprehensive, not narrow.
  • Arrival order is not musical order. Cool jazz entered in 1950, before hard bop (1952) and modal (1958); avant-garde jazz arrived in 1972, after free jazz (1960) and after jazz-rock (1969). A fact about the collection, not the history — useful only as a caution against reading the collection as a narrative.
  • The modal/hard-bop boundary is porous by the site’s own tagging. Most canon records carry two or three styles, and ecm is used as a tag but never a primary style — the export already treats a label as a lane rather than a genre, which is the correct call and supports §03.

The four ECM albums filed by genre rather than lane, and the mis-tagged solo memorial, are catalogued in the canon inventory. That is a data-hygiene item for mccoy, not a question for the page.

05The genre map

Eight sections instead of seven. Everything in the original brief is still present; nothing has been dropped — one has been moved to the axis it actually belongs to.

Bebop

1945–48 · 5 albums

Fast, harmonically dense, and at the time a refusal to be background music. The thinnest edge of the collection and its only genuine gateway rather than a survey.

Enter: Charlie Parker, The Charlie Parker Story, 1945

Cool Jazz

1949–56 · 34 albums

Long unhurried lines and room to breathe. The gentlest door in the canon — and the one the collection itself opened with, in 1950.

Enter: Miles Davis, Birth of the Cool, 1950

Hard Bop

1955–65 · 98 albums

The spine of the canon. Blues-based, blues-inflected, and it swings hardest. The most direct, most physical music on the page.

Enter: Art Blakey, Moanin’, 1958 — “if he converts no one, nothing will” — mccoy

Post-Bop

1964–70 · 61 albums

Where the music stops being a vehicle for solos and starts being composition. The term itself is genuinely contested — see the disputes below.

Enter: Wayne Shorter, Speak No Evil, 1964

Modal

1959– · 30 albums

Harmony stops moving so the melody can. The most approachable important record in jazz, and the most misfiled — Kind of Blue is neither bebop nor cool.

Enter: Miles Davis, Kind of Blue, 1959

Soul Jazz

1958–70 · 10 albums

Groove leads; organ and electric piano; gospel and R&B underneath. Not lesser hard bop — a different job. mccoy notes John defends it against exactly that sneer.

Enter: Lee Morgan, The Sidewinder, 1963

The Outside — free jazz & the avant-garde

1960– · 9 albums

One axis, two arguments. Free jazz abandons the chord changes. The avant-garde wing — the AACM and European composed lineages — is broader and often quasi-composed, and the two are constantly merged in popular writing. Gridley’s warning is the right one: the loose categories “led some outsiders to assume … that the different musicians of this period had more in common than they actually did.” Say plainly that they are not the same thing.

Enter: Ornette Coleman, The Shape of Jazz to Come, 1959 — inside melodic heads, outside solos. The melodic door. Spiritual door: Alice Coltrane, Journey in Satchidananda, 1970.

The Electric Turn — fusion

1968–85 · 6 albums, filed three ways

A different axis, and the section should open by saying so. Electric, groove-led, aimed at a rock-sized audience — but harmonically still inside, on vamps and riffs. There was a real argument within the avant-garde camp that this was capitulation; the counter-argument is that it reached an audience the clubs never could. Let the page state the argument rather than settle it.

Enter: Bitches Brew, 1970 as the landmark; Head Hunters, 1973 for the groove-first listener.

One oddity the data supplies for free: the canon files The Shape of Jazz to Come as post-bop, not free jazz, while filing Ascension as free jazz. Both defensible; together they are a neat demonstration that the labels do not settle the question. The page’s own site is the best available proof of its thesis.

06The hinge records

The highest-value thing in this corpus. A hinge record is one a reader can hold in each hand — it turns an abstraction into a listening experience.

All eight were nominated by mccoy. They were selected from the canon, and that selection method is now suspect — see §04. They belong here because of what each record does, not because it is owned. The explainer path carries an open task to re-derive the entry points from listening rather than inventory.

A ninth, from my own analysis of the export rather than mccoy’s list: Old and New Dreams, Playing (1980, ECM) is filed Free Jazz, but the group is Gil Evans, Alan Skidmore, Jimmy Heath and Jack DeJohnette — a Miles Davis sidemen band, hard bop to its bones. Inside by personnel, outside by label. It is the cleanest available demonstration that the site’s own filing categories are a convenience rather than a fact.

07Mislabel traps

Things a public explainer will otherwise get wrong, gathered from mccoy’s advisory. Each is a live risk for a page aimed at readers with no vocabulary.

The trapThe truth
Kind of Blue is bebop / cool It is the modal break from both.
A Love Supreme is free jazz A composed, harmonically grounded suite. Ascension is the free one.
Bitches Brew is free jazz Studio-built fusion over grooves — and, per section 03, not properly “outside” on any axis but the electric one.
Birth of the Cool is bebop It is the reaction away from bebop.
Out to Lunch is free jazz Composed post-bop with outside solos.
Getz / Gilberto is cool jazz Bossa nova — adjacent to cool, but not cool jazz proper.
“Avant-garde” = free jazz Not a synonym. In the house taxonomy, avant-garde is the AACM compositional wing; free jazz is the Coleman/Trane/Ayler line. A real historical error in popular writing.
There is an “ECM sound” genre ECM is a label, and the “sound” is largely a marketing category. mccoy: “say so gently.” Accurate, without picking a fight.
Mid-’60s Blue Note is all hard bop Speak No Evil is post-bop, not hard bop.

08Five dates that carry the most weight

If the page needs only a handful of dates, these are the ones doing real argumentative work. The full 1945–1985 timeline, with confidence marks on every anchor and a canon-density histogram, is in the corpus.

1945

The canon begins, in bebop. Five albums, 1945–48 — its thinnest and earliest edge.

1950

Birth of the Cool and Subconscious-Lee are the collection’s first two albums. Its centre of gravity is already a reaction to something — the canon opens in the answer to bebop, not in bebop itself.

1959

Kind of Blue and The Shape of Jazz to Come in the same year. Modal composition and the door to free jazz open together and diverge immediately. Nothing else in the timeline shows the split this sharply. This is the hinge year.

1964 → 1965

A Love Supreme to Ascension. Six months, same musician. The single clearest demonstration that the line is a door and not a wall.

1970

Bitches Brew. Electric without abandoning the changes. The event most often filed under the wrong axis — and the reason the page needs two axes rather than one.

09A shape for the page

Structure only — design is John’s and comes later. Ordered so that it respects the one fact most likely to grate: these styles were all happening at once.

PartContainsJob it does
OpeningTwo questions: what are these words, and who invented them? Why does it matter which one a record is? Teaches the vocabulary before using it.
1 · InsideBebop → Cool → Hard Bop → Post-Bop → Modal → Soul Jazz The harmonic centre, in the order it was made. Say explicitly that the boundaries were drawn afterwards by critics.
2 · The OutsideFree jazz & the avant-garde One axis, two arguments, with the Gridley caveat up front.
3 · The Electric TurnFusion, jazz-rock, jazz-funk Opens by saying: this is a different argument, not a louder version of the same one.
4 · The SeamsHinge records, mislabel traps, the moving boundary Where the boxes visibly fail. The most interesting part of the page.
5 · Start HereOne gateway per genre Turns an orientation into an evening’s listening.
A framing constraint that matters more than it sounds. mccoy’s read is that John values co-existence over succession — eras overlap, and the site’s own Working page exists to show coming, going and overlap. A page that reads as a tidy march of styles would grate. Sequence the inside line chronologically, yes, but state early and plainly that these are simultaneous conversations and that the boxes were named afterwards.

10Three questions for John

I have taken a position on each, so these are confirmations rather than blockers — but all three change the page’s shape, so they are worth a decision.

Question 1

Make the “outside is a relative term” argument the spine of the page, or state it once and move on?

Recommendation: make it the spine. It is the most interesting thing available, it is sourced (Parker was “out” in the 1940s), and it inoculates the reader against thinking any of this is settled.

Question 2

Split fusion out as “the electric turn,” on its own axis?

Recommendation: yes. Every other input agrees, and it is the single change that most improves the page — it stops the page teaching that electric music and free music are the same argument.

Question 3

Does the outside section get one entry (free + avant-garde) or two?

Recommendation: one section, two clearly separated subsections, with an explicit note that they are not the same thing. mccoy argues for one; Gridley’s loose- categories warning supports keeping them visibly distinct even inside one section.
