**Soul jazz** or **funky jazz** is a subgenre of [jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz "Jazz") that incorporates strong influences from [hard bop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_bop "Hard bop"), [blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blues "Blues"), [soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_music "Soul music"), [gospel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_music "Gospel music") and [rhythm and blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhythm_and_blues "Rhythm and blues"). Soul jazz is often characterized by [organ trios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organ_trio "Organ trio") featuring the [Hammond organs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammond_organ "Hammond organ") and small combos including [saxophones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxophone "Saxophone"), [brass instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brass_instrument "Brass instrument"), [electric guitars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_guitar "Electric guitar"), [double basses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_bass "Double bass"), [bass guitars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bass_guitar "Bass guitar"), [drums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drums "Drums"), [pianos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piano "Piano"), [vocals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vocal "Vocal") and [electric organs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_organ "Electric organ"). Its origins were in the 1950s and early 1960s, with its heyday with popular audiences preceding the rise of [jazz fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz_fusion "Jazz fusion") in the late 1960s and 1970s. Prominent names in fusion ranged from bop pianists including [Bobby Timmons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Timmons "Bobby Timmons") and [Junior Mance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junior_Mance "Junior Mance") to a wide range of organists, saxophonists, pianists, drummers and electric guitarists including [Jack McDuff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_McDuff "Jack McDuff"), [Eddie "Lockjaw" Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eddie_%22Lockjaw%22_Davis "Eddie \"Lockjaw\" Davis"), and [Grant Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grant_Green "Grant Green").[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-1)

## Musical style

Soul jazz is often associated with [hard bop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_bop "Hard bop").[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-gridley-2)[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-tanner-3)[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-carr-4) Mark C. Gridley, writing for the _[All Music Guide to Jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Music_Guide_to_Jazz "All Music Guide to Jazz")_, explains that soul jazz more specifically refers to music with "an earthy, bluesy melodic concept" and "repetitive, dance-like rhythms.... Note that some listeners make no distinction between 'soul-jazz' and 'funky hard bop,' and many musicians don't consider 'soul-jazz' to be continuous with 'hard bop.'"[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-gridley-2)

According to Nick Morrison, the subgenre "usually begins with the bass player" who "take\[s\] a strong bass line, establish\[es\] a steady groove between the bass and drums", before the band can "embellish that groove with riffs and melody lines".[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-:0-5)

Jazz pianist Horace Silver stated that "\[f\]unky means earthy and blues-based. It might not be blues itself, but it does have that 'down-home' feel to it. Soul is basically the same, but there's an added dimension of feeling and spirit."[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-6)

## History

### Origins

[Roy Carr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Carr "Roy Carr") has described soul jazz as an outgrowth of hard bop, with the terms "funk" and "soul" appearing in a jazz context as early as the mid-1950s to describe "gospel-informed, down-home, call-and-response blues".[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-carr-4) Carr has also noted the influence of [Ray Charles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Charles "Ray Charles")' small group recordings (which included saxophonists [David "Fathead" Newman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_%22Fathead%22_Newman "David \"Fathead\" Newman") and [Hank Crawford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hank_Crawford "Hank Crawford")) on [Horace Silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace_Silver "Horace Silver"), [Art Blakey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Blakey "Art Blakey"), [Cannonball Adderley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannonball_Adderley "Cannonball Adderley").[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-carr-4) In his view, [David Sanborn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Sanborn "David Sanborn") and [Maceo Parker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maceo_Parker "Maceo Parker") are in a line of [alto saxophonists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alto_saxophone "Alto saxophone") that includes [Earl Bostic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_Bostic "Earl Bostic"), [Tab Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tab_Smith "Tab Smith"), Adderley, and [Lou Donaldson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lou_Donaldson "Lou Donaldson") as the strongest links in the chain of the genre's evolution.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-carr-4)

In the early to mid-1950s, after he left the [Count Basie Orchestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count_Basie_Orchestra "Count Basie Orchestra"), saxophonist Eddie "Lockjaw" Davis was among the first to form a jazz group with both organ and saxophone, first with [Bill Doggett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Doggett "Bill Doggett") and later [Shirley Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shirley_Scott "Shirley Scott").[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-7) For this and his "full bodied yet reedy tone that was equally at home in rhythm & blues settings as more modern contexts," he "provided a link" between big band swing and soul jazz.[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-8)

Soul jazz continued to develop in the late 1950s, reaching public awareness with the release of _[The Cannonball Adderley Quintet in San Francisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cannonball_Adderley_Quintet_in_San_Francisco "The Cannonball Adderley Quintet in San Francisco")_.[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-NPR-9)[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-10) Cannonball Adderley noted: "We were pressured quite heavily by [Riverside Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riverside_Records "Riverside Records") when they discovered there was a word called 'soul'. We became, from an image point of view, soul jazz artists. They kept promoting us that way and I kept deliberately fighting it, to the extent that it became a game."[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-11) Late 1950s, soul jazz artist [Curtis Fuller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtis_Fuller "Curtis Fuller") recorded "Five Spot after Dark".[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-12)

### Mainstream

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2e/Het_Less_McCann_trio_op_Schiphol%2C_vlnr_Less_McCann_%28piano%29%2C_Hurby_Loui_%28bas%29%2C_Bestanddeelnr_914-5091.jpg/500px-Het_Less_McCann_trio_op_Schiphol%2C_vlnr_Less_McCann_%28piano%29%2C_Hurby_Loui_%28bas%29%2C_Bestanddeelnr_914-5091.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Het_Less_McCann_trio_op_Schiphol,_vlnr_Less_McCann_(piano),_Hurby_Loui_(bas),_Bestanddeelnr_914-5091.jpg) [Les McCann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_McCann "Les McCann") Trio

Jimmy Smith's shift into soul jazz demonstrated the organ's potential within the genre with his albums _[Home Cookin'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Cookin'_(album) "Home Cookin' (album)")_ (1961) and _[Back at the Chicken Shack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Back_at_the_Chicken_Shack "Back at the Chicken Shack")_ (1963). Other organists who recorded in the soul jazz genre during this period include [Jack McDuff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_McDuff "Jack McDuff"), [Shirley Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shirley_Scott "Shirley Scott"), and [Charles Earland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Earland "Charles Earland").[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-13) With the addition of former bebop and hard-bop musicians to the genre, the number of musicians within various facets of the style increased as soul jazz became a subgenre in its own right; and like its bop predecessor, the new genre of jazz reworked popular songs, such as " [Got My Mojo Workin'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Got_My_Mojo_Working "Got My Mojo Working")," while saxophone and trumpet players recently converted to the genre composed hits including " [The Sidewinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sidewinder_(composition) "The Sidewinder (composition)")", " [Mercy, Mercy, Mercy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercy,_Mercy,_Mercy "Mercy, Mercy, Mercy")" and "Listen Here" during the mid-1960s.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-:0-5)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d7/Jimmy_Oscar_Smith_Hammond.JPG)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Jimmy_Oscar_Smith_Hammond.JPG) [Jimmy Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Smith_(musician) "Jimmy Smith (musician)") on a [Hammond organ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammond_organ "Hammond organ")

An accelerating factor in soul jazz's development was the [Black Power movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Power_movement "Black Power movement"), which led African-American musicians to return to the African roots of their music. Tunes recorded within the genre, including "Mercy, Mercy, Mercy" and "Let My People Go" were direct references to the [civil rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_rights_movement "Civil rights movement") and Black Power movements.[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-14) For instance, Adderley's music from the period has been described as containing an "irrepressible exuberance". Interest in the genre broadened when Adderley introduced Austrian-born keyboardist [Joe Zawinul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Zawinul "Joe Zawinul") to soul jazz through his Quintet, with Zawinul contributing to its repertoire with his own compositions.[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-15)

Likewise [Stanley Turrentine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Turrentine "Stanley Turrentine"), who began recording with Jimmy Smith in 1960 and rapidly expanded his audience, lamented the lack of jazz on radio and TV, saying that more people, and particularly African Americans, would have listened to soul jazz had it received as much exposure as rock music.[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-16) The tenor saxophone player recorded a number of soul jazz albums throughout the 1960s, including _[Never Let Me Go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Never_Let_Me_Go_(Stanley_Turrentine_album) "Never Let Me Go (Stanley Turrentine album)")_ (1963) with his wife Shirley Scott. Jazz critic Thom Jurek noted that on the recording "the organ acts as the testifying pulpit from which to speak, and Turrentine not only speaks, he weeps and whispers and wails."[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-17) Turrentine's following album, _[A Chip Off the Old Block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Chip_Off_the_Old_Block_(album) "A Chip Off the Old Block (album)")_ (1963), marked the first shift in influence, in this case from the swing/big band era with compositions by [Count Basie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count_Basie "Count Basie") and [Neal Hefti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neal_Hefti "Neal Hefti"). Turrentine's wife again contributed in addition to hard bop trumpeter [Blue Mitchell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Mitchell "Blue Mitchell").[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-18) In the mid- to late-1960s, Turrentine evolved his soul jazz style from the small-group setting traditionally associated with the subgenre to an ensemble format,[\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-:1-19) with _[Joyride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joyride_(Stanley_Turrentine_album) "Joyride (Stanley Turrentine album)")_ (1965) arranged by fellow jazz saxophonist [Oliver Nelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Nelson "Oliver Nelson").[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_jazz#cite_note-20)
