**Modal jazz** is [jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz "Jazz") that makes use of [musical modes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mode_(music) "Mode (music)"), often [modulating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulation_(music) "Modulation (music)") among them to accompany the [chords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chord_(music) "Chord (music)") instead of relying on one [tonal center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonic_(music) "Tonic (music)") used across the piece.

Though exerting influence to the present, modal jazz was most popular in the 1950s and 1960s, as evidenced by the success of [Miles Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miles_Davis "Miles Davis")'s 1958 composition " [Milestones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milestones_(composition) "Milestones (composition)")" and 1959 album _[Kind of Blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kind_of_Blue "Kind of Blue")_, and [John Coltrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Coltrane "John Coltrane")'s quartet from 1960 to 1965. Other performers of modal jazz include [Chick Corea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chick_Corea "Chick Corea"), [Bill Evans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Evans "Bill Evans"), [Herbie Hancock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbie_Hancock "Herbie Hancock"), [Joe Henderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Henderson "Joe Henderson"), [Bobby Hutcherson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Hutcherson "Bobby Hutcherson"), [Pharoah Sanders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharoah_Sanders "Pharoah Sanders"), [Woody Shaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woody_Shaw "Woody Shaw"), [Wayne Shorter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayne_Shorter "Wayne Shorter"), [McCoy Tyner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McCoy_Tyner "McCoy Tyner"), and [Larry Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larry_Young_(musician) "Larry Young (musician)").[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz#cite_note-Essential-1)

## History

In [bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop "Bebop") as well as in [hard bop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_bop "Hard bop"), musicians use chords to provide the background for solos. A piece starts out with a theme that introduces a series of chords for the solos. These chords repeat throughout the whole piece, while the soloists play new, improvised themes over the repeated [chord progression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chord_progression "Chord progression"). By the 1950s, improvising over chords had become such a dominant part of jazz that sidemen at recording dates were sometimes given nothing more than a list of chords to play from.[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz#cite_note-2)

[Mercer Ellington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercer_Ellington "Mercer Ellington") has stated that [Juan Tizol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Tizol "Juan Tizol") conceived the melody to " [Caravan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caravan_(1937_song) "Caravan (1937 song)")" in 1936 as a result of his days studying music in Puerto Rico, where they could not afford much sheet music so the teacher would turn the music upside down after they had learned to play it right-side up.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz#cite_note-mercer1994-3) This "inversion" technique led to a modal sound throughout Tizol's work.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz#cite_note-mercer1994-3)

[Sun Ra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Ra "Sun Ra") reportedly rehearsed a small group consisting of [Harold Ousley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Ousley "Harold Ousley"), [Vernel Fournier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernel_Fournier "Vernel Fournier"), and [Wilbur Ware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilbur_Ware "Wilbur Ware") in 1950 that played original songs that were modal in which the melody was based on a single chord or [vamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostinato#Vamp "Ostinato") – ten years before this approach became popular in jazz.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz#cite_note-4)

Saxophonist [Wayne Shorter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayne_Shorter "Wayne Shorter") has noted that the 1953 composition " [Glass Enclosure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass_Enclosure "Glass Enclosure")" by pianist Bud Powell was one of the earliest jazz compositions to make use of [Lydian chords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lydian_chord "Lydian chord"), based on the [Lydian mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lydian_mode "Lydian mode") that was not widely used in jazz until about a decade later.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz#cite_note-5) Powell's 1951 [Un Poco Loco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Un_Poco_Loco "Un Poco Loco") uses the Lydian chords and even uses Lydian chords stacked on top of each implying a [polytonality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytonality "Polytonality") (D [major 7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_seventh_chord "Major seventh chord") over C major 7: CEGBDF#AC#) with the improvisation based on alternating Lydian-ish polytonality and an [altered dominant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altered_dominant "Altered dominant") chord.[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz#cite_note-6)

Towards the end of the 1950s, spurred by the experiments of composer and bandleader George Russell, musicians began using a modal approach. They chose not to write their pieces using conventional chord changes, but instead using modes. Musicians employing this technique include Miles Davis, [Freddie Hubbard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freddie_Hubbard "Freddie Hubbard"), Bill Evans, [Herbie Hancock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbie_Hancock "Herbie Hancock"), and Wayne Shorter.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/47/Minor_eleventh_chord_Herbie_Hancock_Maiden_Voyage.png/500px-Minor_eleventh_chord_Herbie_Hancock_Maiden_Voyage.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Minor_eleventh_chord_Herbie_Hancock_Maiden_Voyage.png) Opening chord to " [Maiden Voyage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maiden_Voyage_(composition) "Maiden Voyage (composition)")": minor [eleventh chord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleventh_chord "Eleventh chord") (Am9/D).[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz#cite_note-What-7)[Play](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/9/9e/Minor_eleventh_chord_Herbie_Hancock_Maiden_Voyage.mid/Minor_eleventh_chord_Herbie_Hancock_Maiden_Voyage.mid.mp3 "Play audio")[ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Minor_eleventh_chord_Herbie_Hancock_Maiden_Voyage.mid "File:Minor eleventh chord Herbie Hancock Maiden Voyage.mid") Using D [Dorian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorian_mode#Modern_Dorian_mode "Dorian mode").[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz#cite_note-8)

Among the significant compositions of modal jazz were " [So What](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/So_What_(composition) "So What (composition)")" by Miles Davis and " [Impressions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impressions_(composition) "Impressions (composition)")" by John Coltrane.[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz#cite_note-Dummies-9) "So What" and "Impressions" follow the same AABA structure and were in D Dorian for the A sections and modulated a half step up to E-flat Dorian for the B section. The [Dorian mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorian_mode "Dorian mode") is the [natural minor scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_scale#Natural_minor "Minor scale") with a raised sixth. Other compositions include Davis's " [Flamenco Sketches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flamenco_Sketches "Flamenco Sketches")", Bill Evans's " [Peace Piece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_Piece "Peace Piece")", and Shorter's " [Footprints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Footprints_(composition) "Footprints (composition)")".[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz#cite_note-Dummies-9)

Davis recorded one of the best-selling jazz albums of all time in this modal framework. _Kind of Blue_ is an exploration of the possibilities of modal jazz.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz#cite_note-Complete-10)[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz#cite_note-Dummies-9) Although his explorations of modal jazz were sporadic throughout the 1960s, he included several of the tunes from _Kind of Blue_ in the repertoire of his second quintet.

Coltrane took the lead in extensively exploring the limits of modal improvisation and composition with his quartet, featuring [Elvin Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elvin_Jones "Elvin Jones") (drums), [McCoy Tyner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McCoy_Tyner "McCoy Tyner") (piano), and [Reggie Workman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reggie_Workman "Reggie Workman") and [Jimmy Garrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Garrison "Jimmy Garrison") (bass). Several of Coltrane's albums from the period are recognized as examples of modal jazz: _[Africa/Brass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa/Brass "Africa/Brass")_ (1961),[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz#cite_note-Dummies-9) _[Live! at the Village Vanguard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live!_at_the_Village_Vanguard "Live! at the Village Vanguard")_ (1962), _[Crescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crescent_(John_Coltrane) "Crescent (John Coltrane)")_ (1964), _[A Love Supreme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Love_Supreme "A Love Supreme")_ (1964),[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz#cite_note-Dummies-9) and _[Meditations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meditations_(John_Coltrane_album) "Meditations (John Coltrane album)")_ (1965). Coltrane's compositions from this period such as "India", "Chasin' the Trane", "Crescent", and "Impressions" have entered the jazz repertoire, along with his interpretations of standards like Richard Rodgers's " [My Favorite Things](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/My_Favorite_Things_(song) "My Favorite Things (song)")", and the traditional " [Greensleeves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greensleeves "Greensleeves")".
