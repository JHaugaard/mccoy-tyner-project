- What is free jazz?







Free jazz emerged in the late 1950s as an approach to jazz improvisation that reached its height in the 60s and has remained a major development since then. The main characteristic of [free jazz](https://www.britannica.com/art/free-jazz/Introduction#ref1) is that there are no rules. Musicians don’t adhere to a fixed harmonic structure as they improvise; instead, they modulate at will.



Free jazz performers often improvise without observing fixed metres or tempos, and solo and accompaniment roles tend to be fluid. Effectively, free jazz began with the small groups led in 1958–59 by alto saxophonist [Ornette Coleman](https://www.britannica.com/art/jazz/Free-jazz-the-explorations-of-Ornette-Coleman#ref215432), from whose album _Free Jazz_ (1960) the idiom received its name.

- How is free jazz different from other types of jazz?







Free jazz distinguishes itself from other jazz forms through its rejection of fixed structures. Unlike traditional jazz, free jazz musicians do not adhere to predetermined [chord progressions](https://www.britannica.com/art/free-jazz/Introduction#ref1) or [time signatures](https://www.britannica.com/art/jazz/Free-jazz-the-explorations-of-Ornette-Coleman#ref215432). Improvisation is central, with musicians modulating keys at will and phrasing in chromatic intervals and harmonies, sometimes achieving atonality.



Roles are fluid, and the balance between composition and improvisation shifts. Free improvisation, the ultimate form of free jazz, abandons fixed instrumental roles and harmonic, rhythmic, and melodic structures, eliminating composition altogether. [Ornette Coleman](https://www.britannica.com/art/jazz/Free-jazz-the-explorations-of-Ornette-Coleman#ref215432)'s work in the late 1950s and early 1960s significantly influenced this movement, with his album _Free Jazz_ (1960) giving the idiom its name.

- Who were some important musicians in free jazz?







Free jazz emphasizes improvisation without fixed rules, allowing musicians to freely modulate keys and tempos.



Key figures include [Ornette Coleman](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ornette-Coleman/Introduction), whose album _Free Jazz_ (1960) gave the movement its name, and who developed the "harmolodic theory" that released jazz from fixed harmonic structures. [John Coltrane](https://www.britannica.com/biography/John-Coltrane/Introduction) and [Eric Dolphy](https://www.britannica.com/art/free-jazz/Introduction#ref1) also created individual versions of free jazz. [Cecil Taylor](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Cecil-Taylor/Introduction) and [Albert Ayler](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Albert-Ayler/Introduction) were also innovators in free jazz.

- What instruments are commonly used in free jazz?







Free jazz emphasizes improvisation without fixed rules, and any instrument can be used. Common instruments include saxophones, especially alto and tenor saxophones, as exemplified by [John Coltrane](https://www.britannica.com/biography/John-Coltrane/Introduction) and [Ornette Coleman](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ornette-Coleman/Introduction), who were pioneers in the genre.



Other instruments frequently used are the trumpet, clarinet, trombone, piano, and drums. Some free jazz groups may exclude rhythm section instruments entirely.

- How do musicians improvise in free jazz?







In free jazz, improvisation throws out the rule book, freeing musicians from fixed harmonic structures and predetermined chord progressions. Instead, they can modulate keys at will, using chromatic intervals and harmonies, sometimes even exploring atonality through microtones, overtones, multiphonics, and tone clusters.



Improvisation in free jazz also often disregards fixed meters or tempos, with solo and accompaniment roles becoming fluid, as does the balance between composition and improvisation. This leads to free improvisation, which abandons fixed instrumental roles, harmonic, rhythmic, or melodic structures, and even composition itself.

- How did free jazz influence later styles of music?







Free jazz, which emerged in the late 1950s, has significantly impacted later music styles by breaking traditional jazz rules. This genre, characterized by the absence of fixed harmonic structures, allows musicians to modulate keys freely and improvise without adhering to set meters or tempos.



One notable influence of free jazz is its contribution to the development of [jazz-rock](https://www.britannica.com/art/jazz-rock/Introduction#ref1), where the improvisation of modern jazz combines with rock music's instrumentation, drumming, and bass lines. Free jazz also stimulated composers to explore new sounds and melodic expressions, enriching ensemble music. However, the recurring patterns of rock tended to dominate, reducing jazz improvisation.

**free jazz**, an approach to jazz [improvisation](https://www.britannica.com/art/improvisation-music) that emerged during the late 1950s, reached its height in the ’60s, and remained a major development in [jazz](https://www.britannica.com/art/jazz) thereafter.

The main characteristic of free jazz is that there are no rules. Musicians do not adhere to a fixed harmonic structure (predetermined [chord](https://www.britannica.com/art/chord-music) progressions) as they improvise; instead, they modulate (i.e., change keys) at will. Free jazz improvisers typically phrase in chromatic intervals and harmonies, and some achieve [atonality](https://www.britannica.com/art/atonality) while playing in microtones, overtones, multiphonics (simultaneous notes played on one horn), and tone clusters. Free jazz performers often improvise without observing fixed metres or tempos. Solo and accompaniment roles tend to be fluid, as does the balance of [composition](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/composition) and improvisation in a performance. The ultimate development of free jazz is free improvisation, which combines all these qualities—using no fixed instrumental roles or harmonic, rhythmic, or melodic structures and abandoning composition altogether.

As early as the 1940s, jazz musicians, most notably pianist [Lennie Tristano](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lennie-Tristano) and composer Bob Graettinger, created a handful of works using free jazz elements. Effectively, free jazz began with the small groups led in 1958–59 by alto saxophonist [Ornette Coleman](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ornette-Coleman), from whose album _Free Jazz_ (1960) the [idiom](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/idiom) received its name. Shortly afterward, saxophonists [John Coltrane](https://www.britannica.com/biography/John-Coltrane) and [Eric Dolphy](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Eric-Dolphy) and pianist [Cecil Taylor](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Cecil-Taylor) began creating individual versions of free jazz. “Energy music,” later called “noise,” became an identifying label for high-energy, [collective](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/collective) improvisations in which dense sound textures were created from furiously generated note sequences. In the mid-1960s Coltrane and fellow saxophonist Pharaoh Sanders adopted styles using soaring runs and distorted wails and shrieks, and [Albert Ayler](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Albert-Ayler) played saxophone solos using indeterminate pitches, multiphonic honks, and overtone screams. Such drummers as Sunny Murray and Andrew Cyrille accompanied these improvisations with pure accent and without direct reference to tempo or metre. [Sun Ra](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Sun-Ra)’s Arkestra, with instrumentalists, singers, and dancers, enriched free jazz with a colourful sense of spectacle, and the [Art Ensemble of Chicago](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Art-Ensemble-of-Chicago) and other musicians [affiliated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/affiliated) with that city’s [Association for the Advancement of Creative Musicians](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Creative-Musicians) explored new sound colours and melodic expressions that returned an emphasis on lyricism to free jazz.

There were other [innovations](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/innovations) as well: saxophonists [Anthony Braxton](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Anthony-Braxton), Steve Lacy, and Evan Parker performed unaccompanied improvisations at their solo concerts, and unprecedented groups began to appear that had no [rhythm](https://www.britannica.com/art/rhythm-music) section instruments whatsoever. Free improvisation also flourished in Europe and Great Britain, where native musical traditions often influenced the players as much as did traditional jazz. The Ganelin Trio from the [Soviet Union](https://www.britannica.com/place/Soviet-Union) improvised on Russian folk songs, and exiles from [South Africa](https://www.britannica.com/place/South-Africa) in the Brotherhood of Breath fused free jazz with [kivela](https://www.britannica.com/art/kwela) (kwela) [music](https://www.britannica.com/art/music). **improvisation**, in [music](https://www.britannica.com/art/music), the [extemporaneous](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/extemporaneous) [composition](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/composition) or free performance of a [musical](https://www.britannica.com/art/musical) passage, usually in a manner conforming to certain stylistic norms but unfettered by the [prescriptive](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/prescriptive) features of a specific musical text. Music originated as improvisation and is still extensively improvised in Eastern traditions and in the modern Western tradition of jazz.

Many of the great composers of Western classical music were masters of improvisation, especially on keyboard instruments, which offered such celebrated composer-performers as [Johann Sebastian Bach](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Johann-Sebastian-Bach), W.A. Mozart, [Felix Mendelssohn](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Felix-Mendelssohn), and [Frédéric Chopin](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Frederic-Chopin) virtually boundless opportunities for the spontaneous unfolding of their rich musical imaginations. Many an idea so generated eventually appeared in a written composition. Some composers have regarded improvisation as an indispensable warm-up for their creative task.

Prior to the instrumental era in the West, improvisation within the systematized musical [context](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/context) was largely limited to ornamental variants of vocal parts in polyphonic [compositions](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/compositions) and to instrumental [adaptations](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/adaptations) of vocal compositions, especially by lute and keyboard virtuosos. The monodic textures that originated about 1600, on the other hand, were ready-made, indeed in large measure intended, for improvisational enhancement, not only of the treble parts but also, almost by definition, of the bass, which was figured to suggest no more than a minimal chordal outline.

It is generally assumed that early European music from Roman Catholic chant to [medieval](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/medieval) polyphony was rooted in such improvisational practices as the exploration of motivic possibilities in the church modes ( _see_ [church mode](https://www.britannica.com/art/church-mode)) and the addition of a second melody to a preexisting melody or [cantus firmus](https://www.britannica.com/art/cantus-firmus). Modal improvisations have remained central to much non-Western music, including the chant of the Jewish synagogue, Islāmic _maqām_ elaborations, and Indian [raga](https://www.britannica.com/art/raga) performances.

In the West, cantus firmus improvisation inspired a great deal of instrumental music as well, beginning with late Renaissance improvisations over ostinato basses (relatively short repeated bass patterns) and maintained through the centuries especially by organists who embraced such popular ostinato [genres](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/genres) as the passacaglia and chaconne. Organists have remained in the forefront of improvisation as a primary musical activity in no way at odds with written composition, while keyboard improvisation in turn has been responsible for compositions of a freely associative character of the sort found among hundreds of preludes, toccatas, and fantasies written during the past three centuries. Improvisations on Protestant hymn tunes gave birth to the important 17th- and 18th-century [genre](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/genre) the [chorale prelude](https://www.britannica.com/art/chorale-prelude). In the later 18th century, improvisation, often based on variation techniques but not excluding the strictly polyphonic procedures of canon and fugue, challenged the ingenuity of virtuoso-composers repeatedly in public improvisational contests, such as those that pitted Mozart against [Muzio Clementi](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Muzio-Clementi) and [Ludwig van Beethoven](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ludwig-van-Beethoven) against Joseph Wölfl.

In modern times, improvisation survives as one of the chief distinguishing characteristics of [jazz](https://www.britannica.com/art/jazz). Here, too, the process is usually inspired by, and structured (however loosely) in accordance with, [salient](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/salient) characteristics of the model in question, be it a well-known show tune or a [ground bass](https://www.britannica.com/art/ground-bass). During the second half of the 20th century, there arose among certain avant-garde composers and performers a tendency away from any vestige of traditional structure. A “composition” from this experimental school might be entirely devoid of conventional notation, consisting rather of a verbal instruction, a prescription for duration, or an [idiosyncratic](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/idiosyncratic) graphic code. Some works required performers to combine at random “building blocks” of brief musical phrases or entire sections presented by the composer; it has been asserted that such a process embodies a more profound creative collaboration between composer and performer than does the interpretation of a fully notated work or the express but limited freedom accorded performers at crucial moments in certain fixed compositions ( _e.g.,_ the da capo section of an 18th-century aria or the cadenza near the end of a solo concerto movement). _See also_ [aleatory music](https://www.britannica.com/art/aleatory-music).
