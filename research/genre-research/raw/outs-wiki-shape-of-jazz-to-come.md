_**The Shape of Jazz to Come**_ is the third studio album by the American [jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz "Jazz") musician [Ornette Coleman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ornette_Coleman "Ornette Coleman"). Released on [Atlantic Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_Records "Atlantic Records") in November 1959, it was his debut on the label and his first album featuring the quartet of himself, trumpeter [Don Cherry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Cherry_(trumpeter) "Don Cherry (trumpeter)"), bassist [Charlie Haden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Haden "Charlie Haden"), and drummer [Billy Higgins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billy_Higgins "Billy Higgins").[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-3) The recording session for the album took place on May 22, 1959, at [Radio Recorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Recorders "Radio Recorders") in [Hollywood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hollywood,_Los_Angeles "Hollywood, Los Angeles").[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-Kaplan,_Fred_M._2009-4) Although Coleman initially wished for the album to be titled _Focus on Sanity_ after its fourth track, producer [Nesuhi Ertegun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nesuhi_Ertegun "Nesuhi Ertegun") suggested the final title, feeling that it would give consumers "an idea about the uniqueness of the LP."[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-Kaplan,_Fred_M._2009-4)

In 2012, the [Library of Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Congress "Library of Congress") added _The Shape of Jazz to Come_ to the [National Recording Registry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Recording_Registry "National Recording Registry").[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-5) The album was included in all three editions of the _[Rolling Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolling_Stone "Rolling Stone")_ list of [the 500 greatest albums of all time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolling_Stone's_500_Greatest_Albums_of_All_Time "Rolling Stone's 500 Greatest Albums of All Time").[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-genius.com-6)[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-:0-7)[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-:1-8) [AllMusic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AllMusic "AllMusic") called it one of the 20 essential [free jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz "Free jazz") albums.[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-Free_Jazz:_A_Subjective_History-9) The album was inducted into [the Grammy Hall of Fame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammy_Hall_of_Fame_Award "Grammy Hall of Fame Award") in 2015.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-GRAMMY_Hall_Of_Fame-10)

## Background

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Background")\]

From 1948 to 1958, Coleman moved between [New Orleans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Orleans "New Orleans"), his hometown of [Fort Worth, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Worth,_Texas "Fort Worth, Texas"), and [Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles "Los Angeles"), working various jobs and developing his own unique sound that was often met with hostility.[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-pbs-grove-11)[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-12) His approach initially made it difficult for him to make ends meet by playing music.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-Johnson,_Martin_2009-13) While employed as an elevator operator in Los Angeles, he studied [music theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_theory "Music theory") and harmony and developed an idiosyncratic take on [country blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Country_blues "Country blues") and [folk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_music "Folk music") forms.[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-pbs-grove-11)

Coleman's big break came in Los Angeles when he caught the attention of bassist [Percy Heath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percy_Heath "Percy Heath") and pianist [John Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lewis_(pianist) "John Lewis (pianist)") of the [Modern Jazz Quartet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Jazz_Quartet "Modern Jazz Quartet"). Lewis encouraged Coleman and his trumpeter, Don Cherry, to attend the [Lenox School of Jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenox_School_of_Jazz "Lenox School of Jazz") summer program in Massachusetts in 1959, at which Lewis was the director. Lewis also secured Coleman a deal with Atlantic Records, which paid his tuition at the Lenox School.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-Kaplan,_Fred_M._2009-4)[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-pbs-grove-11)[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-Johnson,_Martin_2009-13) Though both Coleman and Cherry were already rather accomplished by this point in their careers, Lewis wanted to use their attendance at Lenox to generate buzz amongst jazz circles. Their presence at the school was not without friction amongst students and faculty alike, but in the end, their attending the school accomplished what Lewis hoped to achieve. Coleman was subsequently booked by Lewis to play at the 1959 Monterey Jazz Festival in California.[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-14)

In June 1959, Coleman suggested to Atlantic jazz producer [Nesuhi Ertegun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nesuhi_Ertegun "Nesuhi Ertegun") that he was considering abandoning music in order to study religion. Ertegun, confident of Coleman's potential, urged him to reconsider.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-Kaplan,_Fred_M._2009-4)

## Content

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Content")\]

Coleman's quartet, like [Gerry Mulligan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerry_Mulligan "Gerry Mulligan")'s, was unusual in that it did not employ a chordal instrument such as a piano or guitar. Each composition contains a brief thematic statement, then several minutes of free improvisation, followed by a repetition of the main theme. While this resembles the conventional head-solo-head structure of [bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop "Bebop"), it abandons the use of chord structures. _The Shape of Jazz to Come_ found Coleman and his quartet elaborating on the sound and themes he had been developing throughout his career.

One prominent feature of Coleman's signature sound was that he played a plastic [Grafton saxophone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grafton_saxophone "Grafton saxophone"), which contributed to the harshness of his timbre. He coined the term " [harmolodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmolodics "Harmolodics")", a combination of harmony, movement, and melody, to describe his philosophy of improvisation which heavily emphasized melody rather than harmony. It was early in his career, in an attempt to further emphasize focus on melody over harmony, that he stopped including a piano as a part of his ensembles.

Coleman continues with this tradition on _The Shape of Jazz to Come_, dispensing with harmonic accompaniment and focusing solely on improvised melodies and variations on themes and motifs. His use of microtonal intervals was central to his sound, and he even went as far as to suggest that the same pitch should sound different when played in different contexts, stating that "jazz is the only music in which the same note can be played night after night, but differently each time".[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-15) The album was a breakthrough and helped to establish the [free jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz "Free jazz") movement. Later [avant-garde jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz "Avant-garde jazz") was often very different from Coleman's work, but the work helped to lay the foundation upon which much subsequent avant-garde and free jazz would be built.

### "Lonely Woman"

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: \"Lonely Woman\"")\]

The album contains one of the few Coleman compositions to achieve [jazz standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz_standard "Jazz standard") status, " [Lonely Woman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lonely_Woman_(composition) "Lonely Woman (composition)")". Coleman was moved to compose the song when, while on a lunch break from his job in a department store stock room in Los Angeles in the early 1950s, he came across a photograph of a woman in a gallery. Coleman describes the photograph as follows:

> In the background there was everything you could imagine that was wealthy – all in her background – but she was so sad. And I said, 'Oh my goodness. I understand this feeling. I have not experienced this wealth, but I understand the feeling.' I went home and wrote 'Lonely Woman'... I related the condition to myself, wrote this song, and ever since it has grown and grown and grown.[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-16)

Fred Kaplan wrote:

> 'Lonely Woman' begins with Haden playing a slow bass dirge. Higgins follows with a fast drum riff (a pairing of slow bass and fast drums was unusual enough). Then Coleman and Cherry, in unison, blow a sorrowful melody, both of them bending notes, wailing, so naked with emotion that it still raises shivers a half century later. After reciting the theme a couple times, Coleman takes his solo, which wanders off in a different direction; if you were expecting to hear an improvisation on harmony, it might seem like a different song. But he's improvising on other aspects of the song, especially its emotion. The other players do the same. Somehow it all hangs together, and toward the end, they come back to the theme, come back down to Earth, with aplomb.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-Kaplan,_Fred_M._2009-4)

Professional ratings

On November 17, 1959, shortly after the release of the album, Coleman's quartet began a residency at the [Five Spot Café](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Spot_Caf%C3%A9 "Five Spot Café") in Manhattan.[\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-Davis,_Francis_1985-22) Arranged by John Lewis, it was initially scheduled to last two weeks, but was eventually extended to 2+1⁄2 months.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-Johnson,_Martin_2009-13)[\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-Davis,_Francis_1985-22) The performances were well-attended and generated controversy amongst attendees, critics, and jazz musicians alike. Some musicians and critics praised Coleman for an inventiveness not seen since the emergence of bebop, including [Charles Mingus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Mingus "Charles Mingus"), who said: "It’s like organized disorganization or playing wrong right. It gets to you emotionally like a drummer." Others, including [Miles Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miles_Davis "Miles Davis"), were unimpressed.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-Johnson,_Martin_2009-13)[\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-Davis,_Francis_1985-22)

The album was ranked number 246 in _[Rolling Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolling_Stone "Rolling Stone")_ magazine's 2003 list of [the 500 greatest albums of all time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolling_Stone's_500_Greatest_Albums_of_All_Time "Rolling Stone's 500 Greatest Albums of All Time").[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-genius.com-6) The album's rank dropped to number 248 in the 2012 update of the list, and to number 417 in the 2020 update.[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-:0-7)[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-:1-8) In 2024, _Paste_ magazine ranked _The Shape of Jazz To Come_ number 231 on its list of the 300 Greatest Albums of All Time.[\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-23) The album was identified by Chris Kelsey in his [AllMusic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AllMusic "AllMusic") essay "Free Jazz: A Subjective History" as one of the 20 Essential Free Jazz Albums.[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-Free_Jazz:_A_Subjective_History-9) In its ninth edition, _[The Penguin Guide to Jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Penguin_Guide_to_Jazz "The Penguin Guide to Jazz")_ awarded the album a "crown" accolade, in addition to a four star rating.[\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-penguin-19) The album was inducted into [the Grammy Hall of Fame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammy_Hall_of_Fame_Award "Grammy Hall of Fame Award") in 2015.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come#cite_note-GRAMMY_Hall_Of_Fame-10)

Two outtakes from the session, "Monk and the Nun" and "Just for You", would later be released respectively on the 1970s compilations _[Twins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twins_(Ornette) "Twins (Ornette)")_ and _[The Art of the Improvisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Art_of_the_Improvisers "The Art of the Improvisers")_.
