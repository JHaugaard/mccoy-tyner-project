| **_The_**<br>**_Battle of the Five Spot_**<br>**_Ornette Coleman and the New York Jazz Field_**<br>by<br>David Lee<br>(The Mercury Press; Toronto)<br>![](https://www.pointofdeparture.org/archives/PoD-10/img/Book10Lee_Ornette.jpg)<br>Ornette Coleman                              Ray Avery©2006 <br>(Courtesy of The Mercury Press)<br>an excerpt from:<br>_**Chapter 7: The Musicians and the Critics**_<br>Bernard Gendron<br>identifies jazz criticism as an ongoing conflict<br>between two opposing sets of values -- two<br>sets of antagonists who were originally identified<br>and labeled as such during “the first<br>jazz war” fought between New Orleans<br>revivalists (known to their detractors as “moldy<br>figs”) and modernists (advocates of the<br>new “swing” styles) in the 1940s.(1) Their heated debates introduced to jazz the<br>type of aesthetic discourse within which the<br>Coleman controversy was conducted. As Gendron<br>writes:<br>The unity of this new aesthetic<br>discourse was a “unity in dispersion,” to<br>use Foucault’s phrase—that is,<br>a unity that propagated discursive opposition,<br>that created points of discursive repulsion.<br>As such, it was organized primarily around<br>a group of interconnected binary oppositions:<br>_art–commerce, authenticity–artificiality,_<br>_swing–jazz, European–native, folk_<br>_culture–refined culture, technique–affect,_<br>_modern–traditional, black–white,_<br>_fascism–communism_, and _right_<br>_wing–left_<br>_wing_.(2) Bearing<br>in mind the controversy around Coleman and<br>the emerging “free jazz” style,<br>one might also add to these binaries improvised–composed<br>and freedom– _oppression_. <br>Gendron describes how these discursive practices<br>became entrenched during the first “jazz<br>war” between revivalists and swing modernists,<br>and were carried over intact into the second “jazz<br>war,” where swing now represented stodgy<br>tradition, and bebop the new, threatening avant-garde.<br>Gendron points out that:<br>The revivalists were as much “modernists” as<br>were their swing adversaries. They simply accentuated<br>certain tendencies of the “modernist” impulse<br>at the expense of others. We need to remember,<br>for example, that the concepts of the _folkloric_ and<br>the _primitive_ were crucially involved<br>in the “modernist” practices of<br>Picasso, Bartók, Milhaud, and the Surrealists,<br>while the notion of _reactionary_ and<br>art/commerce dichotomy entered crucially into<br>the avant-garde terminologies of opprobrium.(3)<br>In short, by 1959 jazz criticism<br>had evolved a set of expectations regarding<br>the music, and a way of perceiving and discussing<br>it, that was, in Gendron’s words, “lifted<br>out of the various European avant-garde and<br>modernist discourses.”(4)<br>It seems likely that such a _habitus_, certainly<br>among New York jazz critics circa 1959, was<br>also influenced by the era’s literary<br>writers. To started with, they shared a common<br>geography in the neighbourhoods of the East<br>and Greenwich Village, a sprawling intellectual<br>milieu in which the memberships of the literary<br>and jazz fields met and overlapped. Such a<br>confluence, and influence, is certainly implied<br>in first-hand accounts of the Five Spot audience<br>of the time, where jazz critics such as Nat<br>Hentoff and Martin Williams mingled with literary<br>writers, including Mailer and Kerouac. If the<br>fields themselves overlapped, it is not unreasonable<br>to speculate that the agendas of the fields’ members<br>overlapped as well, and certainly that the<br>discursive practices described by Gendron were<br>very much at work.<br>Discussing critical reactions<br>to Miles Davis’ music<br>in the 1950s, John Szwed writes, “Jazz<br>critics were high modernists, looking for originality,<br>influence, a certain toughness of self-expression<br>in their heroes.”(5) In<br>writing about the same period -- the declining<br>years of bebop -- Scott DeVeaux has identified<br>the work of “a<br>shifting (and often uneasy) coalition of musicians<br>and critics” who campaigned to position<br>jazz “on the far side of the ‘Great<br>Divide’ separating art in the modernist<br>mold from ‘an increasingly consuming<br>and engulfing mass culture.’”(6)<br>Of course individuality, tough and even idiosyncratic<br>self-expression, and an identification with “art<br>in the modernist mold,” rather than with<br>the products of mass culture, are precisely<br>the positions that Norman Mailer attributed<br>to the hipster in “The White Negro.” Taken<br>together, they can be summed up under a single<br>label: _authenticity_. <br>Gendron identifies authenticity<br>as an important part of the 1940s revivalist–swing<br>debate, and in fact it also played a big part<br>in critical assessments of Coleman. Authenticity<br>is, if anything, resolutely anti-commercial;<br>as Bourdieu writes, “A heretical break<br>with the prevailing artistic traditions proves<br>its claim to authenticity by its disinterestedness.”(7) A key word here is “prevailing”;<br>a recidivist movement can be just as aggressive,<br>just as heretical, just as disdainful of the<br>status quo, as an avant-garde movement.<br>The “high modernist” search for<br>authenticity can clearly be seen at work in<br>jazz criticism at the time of Coleman’s<br>debut.<br>For example, during the first<br>week of Coleman’s<br>Five Spot engagement, the _New_<br>_York Times_ critic<br>John S. Wilson reviewed a Town Hall jazz concert<br>that included sets by Chico Hamilton and Dave<br>Brubeck--both respected artists who had experimented<br>with classical forms (Hamilton’s quintet,<br>uniquely among jazz groups, even included a<br>cellist). Both had achieved critical praise<br>as well as popular success. In reviewing the<br>evening’s performances, however, Wilson<br>found that “the only jazz of merit… came<br>in two brief solos by Mr. Hamilton’s<br>versatile reedman, Eric Dolphy, one on alto<br>saxophone and the other a remarkably virtuosic<br>and swinging spree on bass clarinet.”(8)<br>At the time Dolphy (1928–1964)<br>was still based in Los Angeles, where he had<br>been acquainted with Coleman (and evidently<br>joined the jazz majority in dismissing him)<br>during the 1950s.(9) Dolphy,<br>however, did not share Coleman’s<br>problems of acceptance with other musicians,<br>because of his thorough conventional training,<br>his excellent sight-reading, and his willingness<br>and ability to improvise within song forms.(10) Nevertheless, toward the end of the decade<br>Dolphy’s techniques on saxophone, bass<br>clarinet and flute were becoming increasingly<br>vocalized, full of unexpected manipulations<br>of pitch and timbre and a freeness of phrasing.<br>In the next few years, he collaborated with<br>Coleman as well as such innovators as Charles<br>Mingus and John Coltrane. <br>It is noteworthy that in a concert featuring<br>some of the era’s most successful and<br>acclaimed jazz artists, a critic for a major<br>newspaper would credit a player as challenging<br>as Dolphy with providing “the only jazz<br>of merit.” Perhaps this confirms Szwed’s<br>description of the critical search for “heroes” rather<br>than entertainers, as well as an example of<br>what DeVeaux terms the positioning of jazz<br>as “art in the modernist mold” rather<br>than as a part of “mass culture.” <br>This agenda can be seen again<br>in the December 1959 _Down Beat_, an<br>issue of the magazine that would have been<br>on the newsstands at the time of Coleman’s<br>Five Spot debut. Among the record reviews,<br>respected jazz musicians get short shrift from<br>a range of well-known jazz critics. Jimmy Cleveland’s<br>all-star sextet, claims critic Don DeMicheal, “rarely<br>gets off the ground.” Buddy Collette’s<br>four-flute record is “all but a waste<br>of time,” writes Ralph Gleason (later<br>a co-founder of _Rolling_<br>_Stone_ magazine).(11)<br>For different reasons, DeMicheal seems prepared<br>to dismiss Ornette Coleman’s second Contemporary<br>record, “Tomorrow is the Question.” The<br>reviewer takes note of the music’s “wild,<br>incoherent solos… marked by extremely<br>bad intonation and sloppy execution.” However,<br>rather than dismissing the record for<br>its wildness and incoherence, DeMicheal rates<br>the record as “astonishing”--a record that “must<br>be listened to many times… Coleman may<br>be the next great influence.” In contrast<br>to the responses to Cleveland and Collette,<br>DeMicheal seems to be saying that Coleman may<br>not be competent--but at least he is original,<br>at least he is _authentic_. The same devaluation<br>of what one might call “professionalism” can<br>also be read into Paul Bley’s estimation<br>of how the Farmer–Golson Jazztet fared<br>in the new context implied by Coleman’s<br>music.<br>This modernist need for the<br>transgressive, the authentic, even the primitive,<br>pervades a subsequent _Down Beat_ account<br>of the press preview that the Termini brothers<br>held at the Five Spot on the evening of Coleman’s<br>opening night. The need is so implicit that<br>the writer, George Hoefer, does not even find<br>it necessary to discuss or describe the music<br>itself. His perspective on the audience is<br>quite different than that of Hobsbawm, or Kotlowitz,<br>and he refers to the music only in terms of<br>the extent to which it fulfills the modernist<br>agenda: “Jazz can well use a new thrill,<br>idea, or sound, something similar to what happened<br>when a jaded swing era spawned Charlie Parker,<br>Thelonious Monk and Dizzy Gillespie in the<br>early 1940s.”(12)<br>This agenda pervades the work<br>of all four of these critics. “Ah conformity!” DeMicheal<br>writes despairingly in his Jimmy Cleveland<br>review. Coleman’s record, on the other<br>hand, “must be listened to many times.” Part<br>of the “high modernist” agenda<br>was an aversion to “conformity,” which<br>Eric Lott has identified as part of “an<br>American tradition of racial abdication.”(13)<br>On the other hand, because it was also accepted<br>as a symptom of creative genius, “non-conformity” could<br>be used to excuse a wide range of idiosyncratic,<br>even aberrant behaviour. As Ingrid Monson writes, “To<br>the extent that the romantic conception of<br>the artist linked the notion of genius with<br>madness and pathology, and entitled the artist<br>to behave in an unorthodox manner as well,<br>it opened an interpretive space in which supposedly<br>negative social behaviours could be transformed<br>into positive markers of artistic genius.”(14)<br>It is revealing that Hoefer’s review<br>offers virtually no comment on how the music<br>sounded to him as a listener. Instead, he follows<br>his writer’s intuition that his readers<br>can best assess the music’s value by<br>surveying the reactions of the other artistic<br>mediators in the audience. Hoefer’s few<br>references to the quartet’s actual music<br>are vague and casual—but his description<br>of the clientele is thoroughly engaged:<br>Some walked in and out before they could finish<br>a drink, some sat mesmerized by the sound,<br>others talked constantly to their neighbors<br>at the table or argued with drink in hand at<br>the bar. It was, for all this, the largest<br>collection of VIPs the jazz world has seen<br>in many a year. A sampling included John Hammond,<br>John Mehegan, Marshall Stearns, Jack Lewis,<br>Burt Korall, Eric Vogel (American correspondent<br>for Germany’s _Jazz_<br>_Podium_ magazine),<br>Hsio Wen Shih, Gunther Schuller, Symphony Sid<br>Torin, Pete Long, Bob Reisner, and the Ertegun<br>brothers….<br>This special preview for the press brought<br>forth mixed-up comments:<br>“He’ll change the entire course<br>of jazz.” “He’s a fake.” “He’s<br>a genius.” “I can’t say;<br>I’ll have to hear him a lot more times.” “He<br>has no form.” “He swings like HELL.” “I’m<br>going home and listen to my Benny Goodman trios<br>and quartets.” “He’s out,<br>real far out.” “I like him, but<br>I don’t have any idea what he is doing.”<br>Finally, one A &R(15) man<br>made the simple statement “I’ve<br>got a recording date” and left.(16)<br>It is worth noting that, in order to impress<br>the reader, Hoefer chooses the names of producers,<br>educators, critics and broadcasters. Only two<br>of these “very important persons” from<br>the jazz world might actually be described<br>as jazz musicians (pianist/educator John Mehegan,<br>and the British alto saxophonist Pete King).<br>The list of vocations confirms Bourdieu’s<br>depiction of a “field of cultural production” in<br>which, although not all the field’s members<br>actually produce the art, each of their efforts<br>must conjoin to validate, to literally _substantiate_ the art, to agree for their own sakes that<br>it is indeed art.<br>In Bourdieu’s terms,<br>Coleman could have asked no better ticket of<br>admission to a jazz field consisting of “positions<br>and a field of position-takings” ( _Down_<br>_Beat_ was the leading US jazz magazine,<br>and Hoefer had been a contributor for over<br>twenty years) than this confirmation that his<br>art’s<br>importance would ultimately be determined by<br>the positions taken towards it by the field’s<br>members.(17) All Hoefer needs to do is (A)<br>establish that other influential field members<br>attended the event and then (B) log the positions,<br>pro and con, of a cross-section of these attendees,<br>diplomatically refraining from naming each<br>speaker or indeed, committing himself to a<br>position of his own. No superlatives were needed<br>for the music itself when superlatives could<br>be applied so readily to the event’s<br>clientele. <br>_The Reactions of Jazz Musicians_<br>The response to Coleman’s<br>music from New York’s jazz musicians<br>seems to have been, at least at first, overwhelmingly<br>negative. One of the era’s most progressive<br>and proactive critics, Nat Hentoff, recorded<br>some of their responses:(18)<br>Roy Eldridge: “I think he’s jiving,<br>baby. He’s putting everybody on.”<br>Coleman Hawkins: “Now, you know that<br>I never like to criticize anyone publicly.<br>Just say I think he needs seasoning. A lot<br>of seasoning.”<br>Red Garland: “Nothing’s<br>happening… Coleman<br>is faking. He’s being very unfair to<br>the public.”(19)<br>Miles Davis went to the Five<br>Spot accompanied by his sextet’s tenor<br>saxophonist, John Coltrane. Coltrane was intrigued,<br>later played with Coleman privately, and was<br>soon to follow many of Coleman’s leads<br>in developing his own music. It was Davis,<br>however, riding the crest of a wave of critically<br>acclaimed and commercially successful LPs,<br>whose comments were picked up by the press: “Hell,<br>just listen to what he writes and how he plays<br>it,” he<br>said of Coleman. “If you’re talking<br>psychologically, the man is all screwed up<br>inside.”(20)<br>In these comments we can read an enactment<br>of what Bourdieu would identify as a struggle<br>between artistic generations:<br>The structure of the field<br>of cultural production is based on two fundamental<br>and quite different oppositions: first, the<br>opposition between the sub-field of restricted<br>production and the sub-field of large-scale<br>production, i.e. between two economies, two<br>time-scales, two audiences, which endlessly<br>produces and reproduces the negative existence<br>of the sub-field of restricted production and<br>its basic opposition to the bourgeois economic<br>order; and secondly, the opposition, within<br>the sub-field of restricted production, between<br>the consecrated avant-garde and the avant-garde,<br>the established figures and the newcomers,<br>i.e. between _artistic_<br>_generations_,<br>often only a few years apart, between the “young” and<br>the “old,” the “neo” and<br>the “paleo,” the “new” and<br>the “outmoded,” etc.; in short,<br>between cultural orthodoxy and heresy.(21)<br>Appearing suddenly in a prestigious venue<br>for modern jazz, Coleman was just such a newcomer,<br>an un-consecrated avant-garde figure. He became<br>a clear target for the opposition that Bourdieu<br>identifies, and the focus of the conflict “between<br>artistic generations, often only a few years<br>apart,” that Bourdieu describes. Seen<br>in this light, perhaps we can better understand<br>the vehemence of so many of Coleman’s<br>detractors among jazz musicians. Miles Davis,<br>for example, was the reigning jazz star of<br>the time. One can read in his comments on Coleman<br>a bitterness at being displaced--sent down the<br>ranks, as it were--from his prevailing, pre-eminent<br>position. To do such a thing, Coleman’s<br>shortcomings must be more than just musical<br>shortcomings; the man must be “all screwed<br>up inside.” <br>Milt Jackson, vibraphonist<br>in the popular and respected Modern Jazz Quartet<br>(which ironically, he co-led with Coleman’s<br>advocate John Lewis), stated that the music<br>was “nothing--there’s<br>no such thing as free form.”(22) Drummer<br>Max Roach, a bebop pioneer and a successful<br>leader and innovator in his own right, objected<br>to the music so strongly that one night he<br>followed Coleman into the Five Spot kitchen<br>between sets, punched him in the mouth, and<br>later harangued him from the street outside<br>his apartment.(23)<br>It is worth noting that despite their initial<br>revulsion, many of these musicians made sincere<br>efforts to come to terms with this new music.<br>Within a few years of Coleman’s Five<br>Spot debut, many of those who had rejected<br>Coleman, perhaps with some qualifications,<br>now accepted him, though often with some reservations.<br>It seems possible that professional jazz musicians’ thorough<br>grounding in conventional harmony and the chord<br>changes of the song form did not equip them<br>for hearing--much less playing--this new music,<br>and they needed time to rethink Coleman’s<br>approach in terms of their own. Admittedly,<br>once musicians have been schooled and experienced<br>in navigating the more rigid constraints of<br>the song form, it is perhaps not completely<br>fair to expect them to immediately absorb the<br>style pioneered by Coleman, with its shifting<br>tonality, free rhythms and restless, dancelike<br>exchange of voices.<br>By the following summer, Roach’s<br>antagonism had eased enough for him to play<br>with Coleman at the rival Newport festival<br>that Roach and Charles Mingus had organized.(24) Both<br>the drummer’s and Mingus’ subsequent<br>recordings introduced “free” elements<br>that had not been heard before in their music;<br>the same is true for Miles Davis, and certainly<br>for John Coltrane. By 1962, even Coleman Hawkins<br>and Shelly Manne felt permitted—perhaps<br>even obliged—to insert a freely-improvised<br>tenor saxophone–drums duet into an album<br>of jazz standards.(25)<br>Among musicians newly exposed to Ornette Coleman,<br>there was a pattern of resistance, then acceptance.<br>The resistance stage can be easily understood<br>in terms of the impact of an avant-garde newcomer<br>on the jazz field. In a “self-contained<br>universe,” where the most valued currency<br>is symbolic capital, the avant-garde is in<br>a high position, possessing a uniquely precious<br>capital that cannot be shared until it is more<br>widely understood.<br>Milt Jackson, an active member of the bebop<br>generation in the 1940s when they were the<br>jazz avant-garde, was still “modern,” but<br>was associated already with the Modern Jazz<br>Quartet’s “cool,” sedate<br>brand of modernism, successfully marketed to<br>predominantly-white bourgeois audiences in<br>concerts and the pricier nightclubs. It seems<br>feasible to surmise that Jackson would not<br>disdain or resist his relegation to the jazz<br>field’s “old guard.” Such<br>positioning would present no professional disadvantage,<br>and would classify him along with artists he<br>considered to be more rightfully his peers.<br>Jackson’s career was built primarily<br>on his reputation as the finest vibraphonist<br>in jazz, and Coleman’s music was making<br>no claims on that particular territory. With<br>these factors taken together, it was just as<br>easy for Jackson, having made his statements<br>on Coleman’s music, to withdraw, claiming<br>no further investment in the controversy.<br>Davis, however, was the leading<br>figure in what Bourdieu would term the consecrated<br>avant-garde. He had followed the protocols<br>of apprenticeship, establishing his musical<br>credentials working with such artists as Coleman<br>Hawkins and Charlie Parker, who had long since<br>been accepted into the canon of the jazz tradition.<br>He had just recorded his ground-breaking, modally-influenced<br>recording “Kind of Blue,” and his<br>group included musicians such as Bill Evans<br>and John Coltrane, who were already becoming<br>major influences in their own rights. In every<br>way a perfect example of Szwed’s “high<br>modernism,” Davis was musically innovative<br>in a modernist sense: modestly extending conventional<br>norms, commercially successful, handsome, aloof<br>and always fashionably dressed--an African-American “hipster” of<br>the model given such exalted symbolic capital<br>in Mailer’s “The White Negro.”(26)<br>It is no wonder, then, that Davis--who unlike<br>Milt Jackson, had a considerable professional<br>investment in being seen as the music’s<br>cutting edge--criticized Coleman so bitterly.<br>Any objections to Coleman’s<br>music that were in fact, “strictly musical” could<br>possibly be related to Spellman’s examples<br>of jazz musicians who felt they must prove<br>themselves “capable of playing classical<br>music to show that playing the blues was a<br>matter of choice.” The music of Coleman’s<br>quartet presented a similar challenge: to say<br>that because one disliked it, one was unwilling<br>to play it carried the unspoken implication<br>that one was unable to play it. (27) As Coleman<br>himself put it, his music challenged jazz musicians<br>to question the practices they had worked so<br>long and hard to master:<br>When I arrived in New York… from<br>most of the jazz musicians, all I got was a<br>wall of hostility… I guess it’s<br>pretty shocking to hear someone like me come<br>on the scene when they’re already comfortable<br>in Charlie Parker’s language. They figure<br>that now they may have to learn something else.(28)<br>Other musicians on Coleman’s<br>side echo this view. “When we played,<br>there were those who really loved it, the growth<br>and the spirit of it,” said Don Cherry. “And<br>then there were those who didn’t like<br>it because they felt it was jeopardizing their<br>position in life.”(29) Buell<br>Neidlinger described the musicians at the Five<br>Spot as “scared<br>to death Ornette was going to be the thing<br>and that they couldn’t make it.”(30)<br>It is perhaps instructive that Neidlinger presents<br>the problem as an imperative: if this music<br>is “going to be the thing,” then<br>they are offered no alternatives. Similarly,<br>Coleman asserted that having heard his music, “they<br>may _have_ to learn something else.” <br>For its detractors, it was easy to turn their<br>backs on Coleman, but demonstrating that they<br>could master his idiom--in order to prove that<br>they were ignoring him as a matter of choice--was<br>not so easy. Despite the wide margins for error<br>that were apparent in this new freedom, the<br>Coleman group had a repertoire of pieces, to<br>be played without reliance on chord changes,<br>that they had thoroughly rehearsed. The leading<br>names in jazz, generally skilled sight-readers<br>thoroughly schooled in musical theory, and<br>with enormous knowledge of conventional jazz<br>performance practice, could learn the tunes,<br>but the group’s way of playing them was<br>something unique and new.<br>Possibly this reveals something<br>more about the intrinsic value of the symbolic<br>capital possessed by each member of the jazz<br>field. If a member could not countenance the<br>procedures of the incoming avant-garde, they<br>were fortunate if, like Milt Jackson, they<br>could move gracefully from an understated position<br>in the modernist camp to the mainstream jazz<br>canon which stood ready and willing to embrace<br>and enshrine them.(31)<br>Not all jazz virtuosi were that lucky. Their<br>inability to come to terms with Coleman’s<br>style meant that they effectively lost rank<br>within the jazz field. The awful truth, as<br>revealed in the acclaim bestowed on Coleman<br>by the field’s mediators, was that the<br>positions of these musicians was no longer<br>determined solely by the merit of their accomplishments--the<br>extent to which, in the course of their careers,<br>they had improved on the music they had been<br>given. The paradigm of jazz performance had<br>shifted, and it was made clear that an artist’s<br>future prospects would be judged on how much,<br>or how little, it resembled that of the incoming<br>avant-garde, who had now been boosted to the<br>peak of the field’s hierarchy. <br>In other words: in terms of symbolic capital,<br>if the most valuable currency within the field<br>was possessed by those musicians seen as the<br>most _au courant_, then after decades of working<br>their way up through the hierarchy, the most<br>respected artists in jazz were now clearly<br>being sent back down. As Bley suggests in his<br>remarks about the Jazztet, before Coleman’s<br>advent, the jazz virtuoso who had best mastered<br>the song form occupied the field’s most<br>honoured position. Post-Coleman, it could be<br>seen as a default position--the high modernist<br>agenda now strongly suggested that musicians<br>who continued to play within the song form<br>did so only because they were not sophisticated<br>or imaginative enough to grasp, much less embrace,<br>the new style. <br>In these terms, it is possible that Coleman’s<br>fiercest detractors among the era’s jazz<br>musicians were reacting not to him or to his<br>music, but to what they saw as a devaluing<br>of their entire body of work and a threat to<br>their status within the jazz field, a threat<br>that menaced the integrity of the field as<br>a whole. This threat came from both inside<br>and outside the field.<br>Although many of the jazz musicians who objected<br>the most strongly to Coleman’s music<br>eventually modified their positions, most historical<br>accounts have recorded only those objections<br>expressed on a strictly musical basis, and<br>expressed in strictly musical language. There<br>were another set of objections, raised by members<br>of the jazz field who felt that Coleman was<br>a pawn being manipulated by forces determined<br>to dominate the field.(32)<br>_The Mediators of the Jazz Field_<br>For all these mixed reactions,<br>Paul Bley has said that jazz critics of the<br>time: <br>performed a yeoman service<br>in quickly identifying Ornette’s validity to the<br>skeptics… The<br>critics did more than their job of acquainting<br>the public with the music. They acquainted<br>the _musicians_ with the music. They<br>acted as liaisons between the avant-garde and<br>the musical community.(33)<br>Bley differentiates clearly between the avant-garde<br>and the “musical community” at<br>large, emphasizing the distance felt by jazz<br>musicians between their music and Coleman’s<br>(if Coleman indeed began the “free jazz” revolution<br>of the 1960s, he also began the still-ongoing<br>problem of differentiating between “jazz” and “improvised<br>music”). However, not everyone felt that<br>the mediation of the critics was a good thing.<br>In a letter to _Down_<br>_Beat_ late in 1959, John<br>Mehegan condemned the part that critics were<br>playing in the public reception of the music: <br>What \[Coleman\] is doing certainly has nothing<br>to do with jazz and, I’m afraid, very<br>little to do with music in any form….<br>His reputation is completely the result of<br>artificial promotion by a small group of king-makers….<br>The frightening thing here is that a small<br>group of writers can “launch” a<br>young musician on a path that can only end<br>in personal defeat and bitterness for the persons<br>involved.(34)<br>Mehegan’s letter is valuable less for<br>its vehement objections to Coleman’s<br>music as for the precise placement of the author’s<br>non-musical objections in the laps of “a<br>small group of king-makers.” Even if<br>Coleman’s detractors could accept the<br>sincerity of Coleman himself, they could not<br>accept the exercise of power they saw in judgements<br>of jazz critics, however well-intentioned might<br>be the latter’s “yeoman service.”<br>Leonard Feather voiced similar reservations<br>when, for 1960’s first issue of _Down_<br>_Beat_, he subjected Ornette Coleman to a Blindfold<br>Test. In this regular _Down_<br>_Beat_ feature, a<br>musician would be asked to identify and comment<br>on recordings by unnamed jazz artists. Over<br>the years the Blindfold Test served as an entertaining,<br>if highly unscientific, litmus test of a musician’s<br>ability to recognize different artists and<br>styles, a test which evoked unpredictable responses<br>from jazz artists in all genres. <br>In Coleman’s case, the allegedly rootless<br>avant-gardist astutely identified most of the<br>artists that he heard, and criticized their<br>music precisely if idiosyncratically (Feather<br>remarked that Coleman is “no less unusual<br>in his verbal than in his musical expression”).<br>But first, Feather prefaced the test with an<br>introduction that took a firm critical stance<br>on Coleman’s notoriety, and in doing<br>so echoed Mehegan in identifying the problem<br>of power.<br>In the early days, jazz talent took its natural<br>course. Anybody with something new and important<br>to say would find his way to the surface of<br>public acceptance, simply on the strength of<br>the stir he had created among fellow musicians.<br>Today the situation is very different. The<br>initiative in molding new stars has been seized<br>by other experts, including some who were among<br>the slowest to accord reluctant recognition<br>to Dizzy Gillespie and Charlie Parker. Ornette<br>Coleman, an alto saxophonist, who, until a<br>few months ago, was virtually unknown, must<br>suffer the judgements applied by the contemporary<br>method.(35)<br>In the past, Feather is saying, established<br>musicians were the first to acknowledge original<br>new artists, by testing and tempering their<br>abilities in the nightly workshop of the jazz<br>engagement. Now, because the consensus of critics<br>was supplanting this “natural” method,<br>musicians might find themselves in the spotlight<br>before their abilities had earned them a place<br>there.<br>At the time he wrote this, Feather himself<br>had been the USA’s pre-eminent jazz critic<br>for over twenty years. Since his arrival from<br>England in the mid-1930s, his influence had<br>been felt in the careers of countless American<br>jazz musicians. Feather wrote reviews and books<br>on jazz, and produced concerts and records.<br>Although he also wrote songs that would occasionally<br>be performed by jazz musicians, it was as a<br>critic that he was principally known—a<br>critic who did not hesitate to use his influence<br>on behalf of music that he liked, or to denigrate<br>or overlook music that he didn’t like.<br>So there is a disingenuousness in his complaint<br>about the power of critics--presumably critics<br>other than himself--to alter the “natural<br>course” of jazz talent.(36)<br>Despite this, Feather’s comments make<br>a useful starting point for examining the workings<br>of power within the jazz field. How in fact<br>did Coleman—in person quiet and self-effacing—manage<br>to make such a noisy, spectacular entrance<br>into the jazz field? Was it in fact not just<br>the power of his music, but the power of a “small<br>group of king-makers” that engineered<br>his leap into the spotlight? <br>In fact, some of the era’s most influential<br>and powerful individuals and institutions helped<br>to boost Coleman into prominence. The chain<br>of events that brought Ornette Coleman to the<br>Five Spot combined the best efforts of critics,<br>record producers, academics and the promotional<br>forces of the music industry. It was a chain<br>of events very different from the organic process<br>that Feather champions as “natural,” but<br>one that plainly revealed the currents of power<br>that are constantly at work within artistic<br>fields. As Joe Goldberg wrote in 1965, recalling<br>Coleman’s first Five Spot engagement:<br>Unfortunately, Coleman immediately became<br>a scapegoat; critics used him as a shield behind<br>which to take potshots at other critics. Two<br>of Coleman’s staunchest admirers were<br>Nat Hentoff and Martin Williams, co-editors<br>of The _Jazz Review_. The publisher of that magazine,<br>Hsio Wen Shih, became Coleman’s manager<br>for a while.
