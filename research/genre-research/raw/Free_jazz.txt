**Free jazz** (also known as **free form jazz[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-1)**) is a style of [avant-garde jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz "Avant-garde jazz") or an experimental approach to [jazz improvisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz_improvisation "Jazz improvisation") that developed in the late 1950s and early 1960s, when musicians attempted to change or break down jazz conventions, such as regular [tempos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tempos "Tempos"), [tones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_tone "Musical tone"), and [chord changes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chord_changes "Chord changes"). Musicians during this period believed that the [bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop "Bebop") and [modal jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz "Modal jazz") that had been played before them was too limiting, and became preoccupied with creating something new. The term "free jazz" was drawn from the 1960 [Ornette Coleman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ornette_Coleman "Ornette Coleman") recording _[Free Jazz: A Collective Improvisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Jazz:_A_Collective_Improvisation "Free Jazz: A Collective Improvisation")_. Europeans tend to favor the term " [free improvisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_improvisation "Free improvisation")". Others have used "modern jazz", "creative music", and "art music".

The ambiguity of free jazz presents problems of definition. Although it is usually played by small groups or individuals, free jazz [big bands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_band "Big band") have existed. Although musicians and critics claim it is innovative and forward-looking, it draws on early styles of jazz and has been described as an attempt to return to primitive, often religious, roots. Although jazz is an American invention, free jazz musicians drew heavily from [world music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_music "World music") and ethnic music traditions from around the world. Sometimes they played African or Asian instruments, unusual instruments, or invented their own. They emphasized emotional intensity and sound for its own sake, exploring [timbre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timbre "Timbre").

## Characteristics

Free jazz was a reaction to the convolution of bop. Conductor and jazz writer Loren Schoenberg wrote that free jazz "gave up on functional harmony altogether, relying instead on a far ranging, stream-of-consciousness approach to melodic variation". The style was largely inspired by the work of jazz saxophonist [Ornette Coleman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ornette_Coleman "Ornette Coleman").[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-2)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ac/Pharoah_Sanders_photo.jpg/500px-Pharoah_Sanders_photo.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Pharoah_Sanders_photo.jpg) Pharoah Sanders in 2006

Some jazz musicians resist any attempt at classification. One difficulty is that most jazz has an element of improvisation. Many musicians draw on free jazz concepts and idioms, and free jazz was never entirely distinct from other genres, but free jazz does have some unique characteristics. [Pharoah Sanders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharoah_Sanders "Pharoah Sanders") and [John Coltrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Coltrane "John Coltrane") used harsh overblowing or other extended techniques to elicit unconventional sounds from their instruments. Like other forms of jazz it places an aesthetic premium on expressing the "voice" or "sound" of the musician, as opposed to the classical tradition in which the performer is seen more as expressing the thoughts of the composer.

Earlier jazz styles typically were built on a framework of song forms, such as [twelve-bar blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve-bar_blues "Twelve-bar blues") or the [32-bar AABA popular song form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty-two-bar_form "Thirty-two-bar form") with chord changes. In free jazz, however, the dependence on a fixed and pre-established form is often eliminated, and the role of [improvisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Improvisation "Improvisation") is correspondingly increased.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-3)

Other forms of jazz use regular [meters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre_(music) "Metre (music)") and pulsed rhythms, usually in 4/4 or (less often) 3/4. Free jazz retains pulsation and sometimes [swings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_music "Swing music") but without regular meter. Frequent [accelerando](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/accelerando "wikt:accelerando") and [ritardando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritardando "Ritardando") give an impression of rhythm that moves like a wave.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Litweiler-4)

Previous jazz forms used [harmonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic "Harmonic") structures, usually cycles of [diatonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diatonic "Diatonic") chords. When improvisation occurred, it was founded on the notes in the chords. Free jazz almost by definition is free of such structures, but also by definition (it is, after all, "jazz" as much as it is "free") it retains much of the language of earlier jazz playing. It is therefore very common to hear diatonic, [altered dominant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altered_dominant "Altered dominant") and [blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blues "Blues") phrases in this music.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

Guitarist [Marc Ribot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marc_Ribot "Marc Ribot") commented that [Ornette Coleman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ornette_Coleman "Ornette Coleman") and [Albert Ayler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Ayler "Albert Ayler") "although they were freeing up certain strictures of bebop, were in fact each developing new structures of composition."[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Ribot-5) Some forms use composed melodies as the basis for group performance and improvisation. Free jazz practitioners sometimes use such material. Other compositional structures are employed, some detailed and complex.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Litweiler-4):276

The breakdown of form and rhythmic structure has been seen by some critics to coincide with jazz musicians' exposure to and use of elements from non-Western music, especially African, Arabic, and Indian. The atonality of free jazz is often credited by historians and jazz performers to a return to non-tonal music of the nineteenth century, including [field hollers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_holler "Field holler"), street cries, and jubilees (part of the "return to the roots" element of free jazz). This suggests that perhaps the movement away from tonality was not a conscious effort to devise a formal atonal system, but rather a reflection of the concepts surrounding free jazz. Jazz became "free" by removing dependence on chord progressions and instead using [polytempic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytempic "Polytempic") and [polyrhythmic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyrhythmic "Polyrhythmic") structures.[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Southern-6)

Rejection of the bop aesthetic was combined with a fascination with earlier styles of jazz, such as [dixieland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dixieland "Dixieland") with its [collective improvisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_improvisation?action=edit&redlink=1 "Collective improvisation"), as well as African music. Interest in ethnic music resulted in the use of instruments from around the world, such as [Ed Blackwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ed_Blackwell "Ed Blackwell")'s West African [talking drum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talking_drum "Talking drum"), and [Leon Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Thomas_(jazz_singer) "Leon Thomas (jazz singer)")'s interpretation of pygmy yodeling.[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Robinson-7) Ideas and inspiration were found in the music of [John Cage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cage "John Cage"), [Musica Elettronica Viva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musica_Elettronica_Viva "Musica Elettronica Viva"), and the [Fluxus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluxus "Fluxus") movement.[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Cambridge-8)

Many critics, particularly at the music's inception, suspected that abandonment of familiar elements of jazz pointed to a lack of technique on the part of the musicians. By 1974, such views were more marginal, and the music had built a body of critical writing.[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Jost-9)

Many critics have drawn connections between the term "free jazz" and the American social setting during the late 1950s and 1960s, especially the emerging social tensions of racial integration and the [civil rights movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_rights_movement "Civil rights movement"). Many argue those recent phenomena such as the landmark _[Brown v. Board of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_v._Board_of_Education "Brown v. Board of Education")_ decision in 1954, the emergence of the [Freedom Riders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_Riders "Freedom Riders") in 1961, the 1963 [Freedom Summer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_Summer "Freedom Summer") of activist-supported black voter registration, and the free alternative black [Freedom Schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_Schools "Freedom Schools") demonstrate the political implications of the word "free" in context of free jazz. Thus many consider free jazz to be not only a rejection of certain musical credos and ideas, but a musical reaction to the oppression and experience of [black Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Americans "Black Americans").[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Gioia-10)

## History

Although free jazz is widely considered to begin in the late 1950s, there are compositions that precede this era that have notable connections to the free jazz aesthetic. Some of the works of [Lennie Tristano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lennie_Tristano "Lennie Tristano") in the late 1940s, particularly " [Intuition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuition_(free_improvisation) "Intuition (free improvisation)")", "Digression", and "Descent into the Maelstrom" exhibit the use of techniques associated with free jazz, such as [atonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atonal "Atonal") collective improvisation and lack of discrete chord changes. Other notable examples of proto-free jazz include _City of Glass_ written in 1948 by [Bob Graettinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Graettinger "Bob Graettinger") for the [Stan Kenton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stan_Kenton "Stan Kenton") band and [Jimmy Giuffre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Giuffre "Jimmy Giuffre")'s 1953 "Fugue". It can be argued, however, that these works are more representative of [third stream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_stream "Third stream") jazz with its references to [contemporary classical music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contemporary_classical_music "Contemporary classical music") techniques such as [serialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serialism "Serialism").[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Gioia-10)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/68/Ornette_Coleman_3.JPG/500px-Ornette_Coleman_3.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ornette_Coleman_3.JPG) Coleman in 2009

Keith Johnson of [AllMusic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AllMusic "AllMusic") describes a "Modern Creative" genre, in which "musicians may incorporate free playing into structured modes—or play just about anything".[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Johnson-11) He includes [John Zorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Zorn "John Zorn"), [Henry Kaiser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Kaiser_(musician) "Henry Kaiser (musician)"), [Eugene Chadbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Chadbourne "Eugene Chadbourne"), [Tim Berne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Berne "Tim Berne"), [Bill Frisell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Frisell "Bill Frisell"), [Steve Lacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Lacy_(saxophonist) "Steve Lacy (saxophonist)"), Cecil Taylor, Ornette Coleman, and [Ray Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Anderson_(musician) "Ray Anderson (musician)") in this genre, which continues "the tradition of the '50s to '60s free-jazz mode".[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Johnson-11)

[Ornette Coleman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ornette_Coleman "Ornette Coleman") rejected pre-written chord changes, believing that freely improvised melodic lines should serve as the basis for harmonic progression. His first notable recordings for [Contemporary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contemporary_Records "Contemporary Records") included _[Tomorrow Is the Question!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomorrow_Is_the_Question! "Tomorrow Is the Question!")_ and _[Something Else!!!!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Something_Else!!!! "Something Else!!!!")_ in 1958.[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Anderson-12) These albums do not follow typical 32-bar form and often employ abrupt changes in tempo and mood.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Shipton-13)

The free jazz movement received its biggest impetus when Coleman moved from the west coast to New York City and was signed to [Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_Records "Atlantic Records"). Albums such as _[The Shape of Jazz to Come](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come "The Shape of Jazz to Come")_ and _[Change of the Century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Change_of_the_Century "Change of the Century")_ marked a radical step beyond his more conventional early work. On these albums, he strayed from the tonal basis that formed the lines of his earlier albums and began truly examining the possibilities of atonal improvisation. The most important recording to the free jazz movement from Coleman during this era, however, came with _[Free Jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Jazz:_A_Collective_Improvisation "Free Jazz: A Collective Improvisation")_, recorded in A&R Studios in New York in 1960. It marked an abrupt departure from the highly structured compositions of his past. Recorded with a double quartet separated into left and right channels, _Free Jazz_ brought a more aggressive, cacophonous texture to Coleman's work, and the record's title would provide the name for the nascent free jazz movement.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Gioia-10):314

Pianist [Cecil Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecil_Taylor "Cecil Taylor") was also exploring the possibilities of avant-garde free jazz. A classically trained pianist, Taylor's main influences included [Thelonious Monk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thelonious_Monk "Thelonious Monk") and [Horace Silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace_Silver "Horace Silver"), who prove key to Taylor's later unconventional uses of the piano.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Shipton-13):792_Jazz Advance_, his album released in 1956 for Transition showed ties to traditional jazz, albeit with an expanded harmonic vocabulary. But the harmonic freedom of these early releases would lead to his transition into free jazz during the early 1960s. Key to this transformation was the introduction of saxophonist [Jimmy Lyons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Lyons_(saxophonist) "Jimmy Lyons (saxophonist)") and drummer [Sunny Murray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunny_Murray "Sunny Murray") in 1962 because they encouraged more progressive musical language, such as tone clusters and abstracted rhythmic figures.
On _Unit Structures_ (Blue Note, 1966)[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Allen-14) Taylor marked his transition to free jazz, as his compositions were composed almost without notated scores, devoid of conventional jazz meter, and harmonic progression. This direction influenced by drummer Andrew Cyrille, who provided rhythmic dynamism outside the conventions of bebop and swing[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Gioia-10):319–320 Taylor also began exploring classical avant-garde, as in his use of prepared pianos developed by composer John Cage.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Shipton-13):794

[Albert Ayler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Ayler "Albert Ayler") was one of the essential composers and performers during the beginning period of free jazz. He began his career as a bebop tenor saxophonist in Scandinavia, and had already begun pushing the boundaries of tonal jazz and blues to their harmonic limits. He soon began collaborating with notable free jazz musicians, including Cecil Taylor in 1962. He pushed the jazz idiom to its absolute limits, and many of his compositions bear little resemblance to jazz of the past. Ayler's musical language focused on the possibilities of [microtonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microtonal "Microtonal") improvisation and extended saxophone technique, creating squawks and honks with his instrument to achieve [multiphonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiphonic "Multiphonic") effects. Yet amidst Ayler's progressive techniques, he shows an attachment for simple, rounded melodies reminiscent of [folk music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_music "Folk music"), which he explores via his more avant-garde style.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Shipton-13):795–796

One of Ayler's key free jazz recordings is _[Spiritual Unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiritual_Unity "Spiritual Unity")_, including his often recorded and most famous composition, _Ghosts_, in which a simple spiritual-like melody is gradually shifted and distorted through Ayler's unique improvisatory interpretation. Ultimately, Ayler serves as an important example of many ways which free jazz could be interpreted, as he often strays into more tonal areas and melodies while exploring the timbral and textural possibilities within his melodies. In this way, his free jazz is built upon both a progressive attitude towards melody and timbre as well as a desire to examine and recontextualize the music of the past.[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz#cite_note-Kernfeld-15)
