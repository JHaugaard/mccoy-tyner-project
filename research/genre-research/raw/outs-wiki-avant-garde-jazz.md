**Avant-garde jazz** (also known as **avant-jazz**, **experimental jazz**, or **"new thing"**)[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-2) is a style of music and [improvisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Improvisation "Improvisation") that combines [avant-garde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_music "Avant-garde music") [art music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_music "Art music") and composition with [jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz "Jazz").[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-3) It originated in the early 1950s and developed through the late 1960s.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-RCJE-4) One of the earliest developments within avant-garde jazz was that of [free jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz "Free jazz"), and the two terms were originally synonymous. Much avant-garde jazz is stylistically distinct, however, in that it lacks free jazz's thoroughly improvised nature and is either fully or partially composed.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-5)

## History

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Avant-garde_jazz&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: History")\]

### 1950s

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Avant-garde_jazz&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: 1950s")\]

While some avant-garde jazz concepts were originally developed in the late 1940s (such as the collective [free improvisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_improvisation "Free improvisation") on [Lennie Tristano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lennie_Tristano "Lennie Tristano")'s 1949 works of " [Intuition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuition_(free_improvisation) "Intuition (free improvisation)")" and "Digression"),[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-6)[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-7) the advent of avant-garde jazz (synonymous with free jazz at the time) is usually considered to be sometime in the mid- to late 1950s.[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-:0-8)[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-9) As a genre, avant-garde jazz was founded among a group of improvisors who rejected the conventions of [bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop "Bebop") and [post bop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-bop "Post-bop") in an effort to blur the division between the written and the spontaneous aspects of these genres.[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-:0-8) In addition to continuing the tradition of experimentation within jazz (a phenomenon evidenced by the development of earlier offshoots of bebop, such as [cool jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cool_jazz "Cool jazz"), [modal jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz "Modal jazz"), and [hard bop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_bop "Hard bop")), jazz artists would also begin incorporating [modernist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernism_(music) "Modernism (music)") ideas, such as [atonality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atonality "Atonality") and [serialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serialism "Serialism").[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-:1-10)

With the release of _[The Shape of Jazz to Come](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shape_of_Jazz_to_Come "The Shape of Jazz to Come")_ in 1959, saxophonist [Ornette Coleman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ornette_Coleman "Ornette Coleman") paved the way for free (and avant-garde) jazz.[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-:0-8) Soon thereafter, he was joined by [Cecil Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecil_Taylor "Cecil Taylor"),[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-:0-8) and they formed the "first wave" of avant-garde jazz music.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-:1-10) Eventually, some would come to apply avant-garde jazz differently from [free jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_jazz "Free jazz"); avant-garde jazz emphasizes structure and organization by the use of composed melodies, shifting but nevertheless predetermined meters and tonalities, and distinctions between soloists and accompaniment (rather than a "free" approach to improvisation devoid of predetermined structure).[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-11)

### 1960s

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Avant-garde_jazz&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: 1960s")\]

After the birth of avant-garde jazz in the 1950s, the "second wave" of avant-garde jazz was marked by artists such as [John Coltrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Coltrane "John Coltrane"), [Eric Dolphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Dolphy "Eric Dolphy"), [Charles Mingus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Mingus "Charles Mingus"), and [Albert Ayler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Ayler "Albert Ayler") (among others).[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-:1-10) In Chicago, the [Association for the Advancement of Creative Musicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Creative_Musicians "Association for the Advancement of Creative Musicians") began pursuing their own variety of avant-garde jazz. The AACM musicians ( [Muhal Richard Abrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhal_Richard_Abrams "Muhal Richard Abrams"), [Anthony Braxton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Braxton "Anthony Braxton"), [Roscoe Mitchell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roscoe_Mitchell "Roscoe Mitchell"), [Hamid Drake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamid_Drake "Hamid Drake"), and [the Art Ensemble of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Art_Ensemble_of_Chicago "The Art Ensemble of Chicago")) tended towards [eclecticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclecticism_in_music "Eclecticism in music"). Poet [Amiri Baraka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amiri_Baraka "Amiri Baraka"), an important figure in the [Black Arts Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Arts_Movement "Black Arts Movement") (BAM),[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-12) recorded spoken word tracks with the New York Art Quartet (“Black Dada Nihilismus,” 1964, ESP) and Sunny Murray (“Black Art,” 1965, Jihad).[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-13)

While avant-garde jazz did gain some traction throughout the 1960s (especially with John Coltrane), most avant-garde jazz musicians did not enjoy the same levels of popularity.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-:1-10) Avant-garde jazz gradually shifted from being performed mainly in jazz clubs to other spaces, such as museums and community performance centers, with some artists relocating to Europe.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde_jazz#cite_note-:1-10)
