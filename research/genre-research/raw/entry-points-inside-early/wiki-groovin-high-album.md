_**Groovin' High**_ is a 1955 compilation album of studio sessions by [jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz "Jazz") [composer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composer "Composer") and [trumpeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trumpet "Trumpet") [Dizzy Gillespie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dizzy_Gillespie "Dizzy Gillespie"). _The Rough Guide to Jazz_ describes the album as "some of the key bebop small-group and [big band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_band "Big band") recordings."[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High_(Dizzy_Gillespie_album)#cite_note-rough-1)

## Reception

Jazz critic [Scott Yanow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scott_Yanow "Scott Yanow") concedes that the music included is classic, but dismisses the compilation over-all as "so-so" because of its brevity, because of the outdated and lightweight [liner notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liner_notes "Liner notes") and because the material presented does not represent the complete sessions at which the material was played.[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High_(Dizzy_Gillespie_album)#cite_note-2) The compilation features Gillespie with a number of combinations and other musicians, including his 1946 big band, [Charlie Parker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Parker "Charlie Parker"), a sextet with [Dexter Gordon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dexter_Gordon "Dexter Gordon") and a combo featuring [Sonny Stitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonny_Stitt "Sonny Stitt").

_**Birth of the Cool**_ is a [compilation album](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compilation_album "Compilation album") by the American [jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz "Jazz") trumpeter and bandleader [Miles Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miles_Davis "Miles Davis"). It was released in February or March 1957 on [Capitol Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitol_Records "Capitol Records").[\[nb 1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-6) It compiles twelve tracks recorded by Davis's nonet for the label over the course of three sessions during 1949 and 1950.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-Smith-5)

Featuring unusual instrumentation and several notable musicians, the music consisted of innovative arrangements influenced by Afro-American music and [classical music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_music "Classical music") techniques, and marked a major development in post- [bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop "Bebop") jazz. As the title suggests, these recordings are considered seminal in the history of [cool jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cool_jazz "Cool jazz"). Most of them were originally released in the [10-inch 78-rpm format](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonograph_record#78_rom_disc_developments "Phonograph record") and are all approximately three minutes long.

## Background

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/99/Charlie_Parker%2C_Tommy_Potter%2C_Miles_Davis%2C_Duke_Jordan%2C_Max_Roach_%28Gottlieb_06851%29.jpg/500px-Charlie_Parker%2C_Tommy_Potter%2C_Miles_Davis%2C_Duke_Jordan%2C_Max_Roach_%28Gottlieb_06851%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Charlie_Parker,_Tommy_Potter,_Miles_Davis,_Duke_Jordan,_Max_Roach_(Gottlieb_06851).jpg) Davis (right center) playing in [Charlie Parker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Parker "Charlie Parker")'s quintet, 1947

From 1944 to 1948, [Miles Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miles_Davis "Miles Davis") played in [Charlie Parker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Parker "Charlie Parker")'s quintet. Davis recorded several [sides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A-side_and_B-side "A-side and B-side") with Parker during this period, most released for the [Savoy and Dial labels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Parker's_Savoy_and_Dial_Sessions "Charlie Parker's Savoy and Dial Sessions"). Davis's [first records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Miles "First Miles") released under his own name were recorded with Parker's band, in 1947, and were more arranged and rehearsed than Parker's usual approach to recording.[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-7) By 1948, Davis had three years of [bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop "Bebop") playing under his belt, but he struggled to match the speed and ranges of the likes of Gillespie and Parker, choosing instead to play in the mid range of his instrument.[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-8) In 1948, becoming increasingly concerned about growing tensions within the Parker quintet, Davis left the group and began looking for a new band to work with.[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-9)

At the same time, [arranger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrangement "Arrangement") [Gil Evans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gil_Evans "Gil Evans") began hosting gatherings of like-minded, forward-looking musicians at his small basement apartment on 55th Street in Manhattan, three blocks from the jazz nightclubs of [52nd Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/52nd_Street_(Manhattan) "52nd Street (Manhattan)"). Evans had gained a reputation in the jazz world for his orchestration of bebop tunes for the [Claude Thornhill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Thornhill "Claude Thornhill") orchestra in the mid-1940s. Keeping an open door policy, Evans's apartment came to host many of the young jazz artists of late-1940s New York. The participants discussed the future of jazz, including a proposed group with a new sound. According to jazz historian [Ted Gioia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Gioia "Ted Gioia"):

> \[The participants\] were developing a range of tools that would change the sound of contemporary music. In their work together, they relied on a rich palette of harmonies, many of them drawn from European impressionist composers. They explored new instrumental textures, preferring to blend the voices of the horns like a choir rather than pit them against each other as the big bands had traditionally done with their thrusting and parrying sections. They brought down the tempos of their music ... they adopted a more lyrical approach to improvisation ...[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-10)

Before recording in the studio, the band played a two-week engagement at New York City's [Royal Roost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Roost "Royal Roost") club in September 1948.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-11) In the audience was [Pete Rugolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pete_Rugolo "Pete Rugolo"), who signed the group to record for [Capitol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitol_Records "Capitol Records").[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-12) Recordings from two nights made for a [WMCA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WMCA_(AM) "WMCA (AM)") broadcast were eventually released decades later.

## Recording

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/09/Pete_Rugolo%2C_New_York%2C_N.Y.%2C_between_1946_and_1948_%28William_P._Gottlieb_07531%29.jpg/500px-Pete_Rugolo%2C_New_York%2C_N.Y.%2C_between_1946_and_1948_%28William_P._Gottlieb_07531%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Pete_Rugolo,_New_York,_N.Y.,_between_1946_and_1948_(William_P._Gottlieb_07531).jpg) [Pete Rugolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pete_Rugolo "Pete Rugolo") produced the sessions for _Birth of the Cool_.[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-13)

The nonet recorded 12 tracks for Capitol during three sessions over nearly a year and a half. Davis, [Lee Konitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Konitz "Lee Konitz"), [Gerry Mulligan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerry_Mulligan "Gerry Mulligan"), and [Bill Barber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Barber_(musician) "Bill Barber (musician)") were the only musicians who played on all three sessions, though the instrumental lineup was constant excepting the omission of piano and the addition of [Kenny Hagood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenny_Hagood "Kenny Hagood") on March 9. The first session was on January 21, 1949, recording four tracks: Mulligan's "Jeru" and "Godchild"; [Denzil Best](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denzil_Best "Denzil Best")'s "Move"; and "Budo" by Davis and [Bud Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bud_Powell "Bud Powell"),[\[nb 2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-15) the last two arranged by [John Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lewis_(pianist) "John Lewis (pianist)"). Jazz critic [Richard Cook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Cook_(journalist) "Richard Cook (journalist)") hypothesizes that Capitol, wanting to get a good start, recorded these numbers first because they were the catchiest tunes in the nonet's small repertoire.[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-16) That date [Kai Winding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kai_Winding "Kai Winding") replaced Zwerin on trombone, [Al Haig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Haig "Al Haig") replaced Lewis on piano, and [Joe Shulman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Shulman "Joe Shulman") replaced McKibbon on bass.

The second recording date was April 22, 1949, when Davis substituted for [Fats Navarro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fats_Navarro "Fats Navarro") in [Tadd Dameron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tadd_Dameron "Tadd Dameron")'s band with Charlie Parker during the interim. The band returned to the studio with five changes in personnel: [J. J. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._J._Johnson "J. J. Johnson") on trombone, Sandy Siegelstein on French horn, [Nelson Boyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nelson_Boyd "Nelson Boyd") on bass, [Kenny Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenny_Clarke "Kenny Clarke") on drums, and Lewis on piano. At this session, the nonet recorded Mulligan's "Venus de Milo", Lewis's "Rouge", Carisi's "Israel", and " [Boplicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boplicity "Boplicity")", a collaboration between Davis and Evans, credited to the [pseudonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudonym "Pseudonym") "Cleo Henry".[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-17)

The band did not return to the studio again until March 9, 1950. Davis did not call the band for any rehearsals or live performances between the second and third recording dates. This piano-less date featured Mulligan's arrangement of [Eddie DeLange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eddie_DeLange "Eddie DeLange") and [Jimmy Van Heusen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Van_Heusen "Jimmy Van Heusen")'s "Darn That Dream", "Rocker", and "Deception", and Evans's arrangement of [Chummy MacGregor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chummy_MacGregor "Chummy MacGregor")'s " [Moon Dreams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_Dreams "Moon Dreams")", which had been released in a jazz arrangement by [Glenn Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenn_Miller "Glenn Miller") and the AAF Band in 1944 on [V-Disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V-Disc "V-Disc"). The band saw more substitutions, with [Gunther Schuller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunther_Schuller "Gunther Schuller") on French horn and [Al McKibbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_McKibbon "Al McKibbon") on bass. Hagood sang on "Darn That Dream."

## Composition

### Music and style

One of the features of the Davis Nonet was the use of paired instrumentation. An example of this can be heard on the John Lewis arrangement "Move". In "Move", Lewis gives the melody to the pairing of trumpet and alto saxophone, baritone saxophone and tuba supply counterpoint, and trombone and French horn provide harmonies.[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-Cook,_p.18-18) Mulligan's "Jeru" demonstrates another Nonet hallmark: the use of a [unison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unison "Unison") sound and rich [harmony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmony "Harmony") throughout the horns.[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-Cook,_p.18-18) Davis said, "I wanted the instruments to sound like human voices singing ... and they did."[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-19) Though the album is seen as a departure from traditional bop,[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-Gioia,_The_History_of_Jazz._281-20) the recordings do feature tunes that are considered close to the bop style, such as "Budo", which has the band bookending solos by Davis, Mulligan, Konitz, and Winding, like a bebop [head arrangement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_arrangement "Head arrangement").[\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-21)

### Thornhill's influence

One of the largest stated influences on the sound of _Birth of the Cool_ was band leader Claude Thornhill and his orchestra.[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-ReferenceA-22)[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-Chambers,_94-23)[\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-24) Out of Thornhill's band came Konitz, Barber, [Junior Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junior_Collins "Junior Collins"), Joe Shulman, Sandy Siegelstein, Mulligan, and Evans. Davis called the Konitz-Barber-Collins-Shulman-Siegelstein-Mulligan-Evans incarnation with Thornhill "the greatest band", second only to "the [Billy Eckstine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billy_Eckstine "Billy Eckstine") band with [Bird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Parker "Charlie Parker")".[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-Chambers,_94-23) The Thornhill band was known for its [impressionistic style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impressionist_music "Impressionist music"), innovative use of [instrumentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumentation_(music) "Instrumentation (music)"), such as the use of tuba and French horn, and a non- [vibrato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibrato "Vibrato") playing style, hallmarks that the Miles Davis Nonet adopted for _Birth of the Cool_.[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-ReferenceA-22)[\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-25) According to Evans:

> Miles had liked some of what Gerry and I had written for Claude. The instrumentation for the Miles session was caused by the fact that this was the smallest number of instruments that could get the sound and still express all the harmonies the Thornhill band used. Miles wanted to play his idiom with that kind of sound.[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-26)

Davis saw the full 18-piece Thornhill orchestra as cumbersome and thus decided to split the group in half for his desired sound.[\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-27) As arrangers, both Evans and Mulligan gave Thornhill credit for crafting their sound.[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-ReferenceA-22)[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-Gioia,_The_History_of_Jazz._281-20) Thornhill's band gave Evans the opportunity to try arranging small-group bebop tunes for [big band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_band "Big band"), a practice few others were engaging in. Mulligan recalls Thornhill teaching him "the greatest lesson in dynamics, the art of underblowing".[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-Gioia,_The_History_of_Jazz._281-20) Thornhill has also been credited with launching the move away from call and response between sections and toward unison harmonies.[\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-28)

The four tracks from the January 1949 session were released soon after recording as two 78 singles in 1949. From the April 1949 date, "Israel" and "Boplicity" were doubled together on a [78](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/78_rpm "78 rpm") and released in 1949 as well. "Venus de Milo" from the April 1949 session and "Darn That Dream" from the March 1950 session were then released as a 78 in 1950. In 1954, after persuasion from Rugolo, Capitol released eight of the tracks on a [10" LP record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LP_album "LP album") titled _Classics in Jazz—Miles Davis_, Capitol H-459. In 1957 eleven of the tracks (all except for "Darn That Dream") were released by Capitol as _Birth of the Cool_. The final and only track with a vocal, "Darn That Dream," was included with the other eleven on the 1972 LP _Capitol Jazz Classics, Vol. 1: The Complete Birth Of The Cool_, catalogue M-11026. Subsequent releases have used this 1972 compilation as the template. The album has since been reissued many times in various formats, on [compact disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_disc "Compact disc") in 1989, a further expanded edition in 1998, and again as a [Rudy Van Gelder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudy_Van_Gelder "Rudy Van Gelder") [remaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mastering_(audio) "Mastering (audio)") in 2001.[\[27\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-29) The live recordings of the nonet from its time at the Royal Roost in September of 1948 were released as _Cool Boppin_ in 1991.[\[28\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-30) In 1998, Capitol Records released _The Complete Birth of the Cool_, which was remastered by [Mark Levinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Levinson_(audio_equipment_designer) "Mark Levinson (audio equipment designer)") and collected the nonet's live Royal Roost and studio tracks onto a single disc.

Note from the 2001 Capitol reissue producer [Michael Cuscuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Cuscuna "Michael Cuscuna"):

> All previous reissues of this material have been derived from the 1957 12-inch LP master, which turns out to be second or third generation. The original tapes of each tune were filed individually and sound considerably better. Rudy Van Gelder returned to these masters, transferred them in 24-bit to digital and worked his sonic magic. The result is a clearer and more present sound than ever before on these classic recordings.[\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-31)

Retrospective professional reviews

The band's debut performance at the Royal Roost received positive but reserved reactions.[\[38\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-40) [Count Basie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count_Basie "Count Basie"), the Roost's headliner during the Nonet's brief tenure, was more open to the group's sound, saying, "Those slow things sounded strange and good. I didn't always know what they were doing, but I listened, and I liked it."[\[39\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-41) [Winthrop Sargeant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winthrop_Sargeant "Winthrop Sargeant"), classical music critic at _[The New Yorker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Yorker "The New Yorker")_, compared the band's sound to the work of an "impressionist composer with a great sense of aural poetry and a very fastidious feeling for tone color... The music sounds more like that of a new [Maurice Ravel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Ravel "Maurice Ravel") than it does like jazz ... it is not really jazz."[\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-Gioia,_p._283-42) Though he did not recognize the record as jazz, Sargeant said he found the record "charming and exciting".[\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-Gioia,_p._283-42)

In the short term the reaction to the band was little to none,[\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-Gioia,_p._283-42) but in the long term the recordings' effects have been great and lasting. They have been credited with starting the [cool jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cool_jazz "Cool jazz") movement as well as creating a new and viable alternative to [bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop "Bebop").[\[41\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-43)

In 1957, after the release of _Birth of the Cool_, _[Down Beat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Down_Beat "Down Beat")_ magazine wrote that the album influenced "deeply one important direction of modern chamber jazz".[\[42\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-44) Several tunes from the album, such as Carisi's "Israel", have become [jazz standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz_standards "Jazz standards").[\[43\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-45) The album was included in the book _[1001 Albums You Must Hear Before You Die](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1001_Albums_You_Must_Hear_Before_You_Die "1001 Albums You Must Hear Before You Die")_.[\[44\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-46) _Birth of the Cool_ was voted number 349 in [Colin Larkin's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Larkin_(writer) "Colin Larkin (writer)") _[All Time Top 1000 Albums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Time_Top_1000_Albums "All Time Top 1000 Albums")_ (third edition, 2000).[\[45\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-Larkin-47)

Many members of the Miles Davis Nonet had successful careers in cool jazz, notably Mulligan, Lewis, and Konitz. Mulligan moved to California and joined forces with trumpeter [Chet Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chet_Baker "Chet Baker") in a piano-less quartet before creating his Concert Jazz Band.[\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-48) Lewis became music director of the [Modern Jazz Quartet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Jazz_Quartet "Modern Jazz Quartet"), which became one of the most influential cool jazz groups.[\[47\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-49) Konitz had a long career, including work with [Lennie Tristano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lennie_Tristano "Lennie Tristano") and [Warne Marsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warne_Marsh "Warne Marsh") in the 1950s. Evans collaborated with Davis again on a celebrated series of albums: _[Miles Ahead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miles_Ahead_(album) "Miles Ahead (album)")_, _[Porgy and Bess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porgy_and_Bess_(Miles_Davis_album) "Porgy and Bess (Miles Davis album)")_, _[Sketches of Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sketches_of_Spain "Sketches of Spain")_, and _[Quiet Nights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quiet_Nights_(Miles_Davis_album) "Quiet Nights (Miles Davis album)")_.[\[48\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-50) At the time, Capitol Records was disappointed with the sales of the nonet recordings, and did not offer Davis a contract extension. Instead, Davis signed with the new jazz specialty record label [Prestige](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prestige_Records "Prestige Records"), for which he recorded his [first album](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Sounds_(Miles_Davis_album) "The New Sounds (Miles Davis album)") in 1951.[\[49\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_of_the_Cool#cite_note-51)
