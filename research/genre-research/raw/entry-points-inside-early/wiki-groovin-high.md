" **Groovin' High**" is a 1945 [jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz "Jazz") song by trumpeter and composer [Dizzy Gillespie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dizzy_Gillespie "Dizzy Gillespie"). The song was a [bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop "Bebop") mainstay that became a [jazz standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz_standard "Jazz standard"),[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-1) one of Gillespie's best known hits,[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-Dizzy-2) and according to _Bebop: The Music and Its Players_ author Thomas Owens, it was "the first famous bebop recording".[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-Owens-3)

It is a complex musical arrangement based on the [chord structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chord_progression "Chord progression") of a 1920 standard originally recorded by [Paul Whiteman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Whiteman "Paul Whiteman"), " [Whispering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whispering_(song) "Whispering (song)")", composed by [Vincent Rose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_Rose "Vincent Rose") with lyrics by John Schonberger and [Richard Coburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Coburn "Richard Coburn").[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-Dizzy-2) The biography _Dizzy_ characterizes the song as "a pleasant medium-tempo tune" that "demonstrates ... \[Gillespie's\] skill in fashioning interesting textures using only six instruments".[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-Dizzy-2)

It has been used to title many compilation albums and also the 2001 biography _Groovin' High: The Life of Dizzy Gillespie_.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-4)

## Impact

The song appeared on the debut 1947 album, _Dizzy Gillespie and His All Stars_ and is one of eight tracks on that album that, according to jazz critic [Scott Yanow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scott_Yanow "Scott Yanow"), "shocked" Gillespie's contemporaries, contributing to the album "permanently \[changing\] ... jazz and (indirectly) the entire music world".[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-5) In _Jazz: A Regional Exploration_, Yanow explained that at the time, such songs "were unprecedented ... displaying a radically different language" from contemporary [swing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_music "Swing music").[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-Yanow-6) Although fans and fellow musicians found the material "very strange and difficult", _The Sax & Brass Book_ notes that they were quickly adopted as classics.[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-7) According to Yanow, "\[Charlie\] Parker and Gillespie's solos seemed to have little relation to the melody, but they were connected. It was a giant step forward for jazz."[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-Yanow-6)

Thomas Owens highlighted the innovative use of source material, pointing out that while it was not uncommon for jazz musicians to utilize existing chord structures in their compositions in 1945, Gillespie's "melodic [contrafact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrafact "Contrafact") was the most complex jazz melody superimposed on a pre-existing chordal scheme" and "atypically elaborate".[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-Owens-3)

## Performance history

First performed on February 9, 1945, [Gillespie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dizzy_Gillespie "Dizzy Gillespie") reworked the arrangement for a February 28 performance to allow an [improvisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz_improvisation "Jazz improvisation") by guitarist [Remo Palmier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remo_Palmier "Remo Palmier"),[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-8) and it is this reworking that became so well known.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-Owens-3)

In the book _Yardbird Suite_, music historian Lawrence O. Koch sets forth in detail the structure of the song as performed on December 29, 1945, and preserved by [Armed Forces Radio Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Forces_Network "American Forces Network"), from the two- [bar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bar_(music) "Bar (music)") unison figure by Gillespie and [Charlie Parker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Parker "Charlie Parker") that opens the song to the Gillespie [coda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coda_(music) "Coda (music)") at the end.[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-Koch-9) Not having to conform to [78 rpm technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonograph_record#78_rpm_recording_time "Phonograph record"), Gillespie and his band were able to add several minutes to the song during that performance.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-10) Koch praised the "lovely, logical, melodic construction" of Parker's 16-bar solo as well as singling out performances by Gillespie, [Slam Stewart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slam_Stewart "Slam Stewart"), and Palmieri, as commendable.[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-Koch-9) Noting that the coda "has become a jazz cliché, both in its melody and the chord pattern from which the melody was derived", he also drew attention to Gillespie's "prima donna breath control" on the final E-flat, with only a "slight loss in intonation" in spite of the difficulty of the phrase.[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-11) The book _Charlie Parker: His Music and Life_ describes this performance, along with the three other songs played in that session, as capturing "much of the vitality of the early Gillespie-Parker partnership".[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-12)

Other notable performances of the song took place on September 29, 1947, when Parker and Gillespie reunited in concert at [Carnegie Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Hall "Carnegie Hall")[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-13) and during a 1956 tour sponsored by the [US State Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_State "United States Department of State"). Owens describes the 1947 recording as among the finest of Parker's career.[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-14) During the 1956 tour, Gillespie simultaneously performed "Groovin' High" and "Whispering" to demonstrate the way jazz musicians build on the bones of earlier compositions.[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-15)

## Inspiration

According to the book _Visions of Jazz: The First Century_ by critic [Gary Giddins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Giddins "Gary Giddins"), Gillespie once recounted that he believed the song had been inspired by a [film serial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_film "Serial film") he saw at a matinée as a child that used the song "Whispering" as its theme.[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-Giddins-16) Gillespie offered no details about the serial, except that he believed it might have starred stuntman and rodeo rider [Yakima Canutt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakima_Canutt "Yakima Canutt").[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groovin'_High#cite_note-Giddins-16)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

1945 single by Charlie Parker

Not to be confused with the character in [The Mikado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mikado "The Mikado").

For other uses, see [Koko (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koko_(disambiguation) "Koko (disambiguation)").

" **Ko Ko**" (also spelled " **Ko-Ko**" or " **Koko**") is a [1945](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1945_in_jazz "1945 in jazz") [bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop "Bebop") recording composed by [Charlie Parker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Parker "Charlie Parker"). The original recorded version lists Parker on alto saxophone with [trumpeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trumpet "Trumpet") [Miles Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miles_Davis "Miles Davis"), [double bassist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_bass "Double bass") [Curley Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curley_Russell "Curley Russell") and [drummer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum_kit "Drum kit") [Max Roach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Roach "Max Roach"). Due to the absence of [Bud Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bud_Powell "Bud Powell"), [Dizzy Gillespie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dizzy_Gillespie "Dizzy Gillespie") was enlisted to play piano, instead of his usual trumpet. Pianist [Sadik Hakim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadik_Hakim "Sadik Hakim"), then known as Argonne Thornton, was also known to be present at the session. Rumors persist to this day about precisely who played trumpet and piano on this piece; some say it's young Miles Davis who plays trumpet and Gillespie comping at piano, on both takes;[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko-Ko#cite_note-musicofmiles-1) most say Gillespie plays trumpet[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko-Ko#cite_note-musings_of_miles-2)[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko-Ko#cite_note-3)[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko-Ko#cite_note-yardbird_suite-4) and, or instead of, piano; some say Hakim is the pianist on all or part of one or both of the takes.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko-Ko#cite_note-complete_notes-5)[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko-Ko#cite_note-reig-6)[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko-Ko#cite_note-hakim-7) However, Miles Davis confirms in his autobiography that he did not play trumpet on "Ko Ko":

> "I remember Bird wanting me to play "Ko-Ko," a tune that was based on the changes of "Cherokee." Now Bird knew I was having trouble playing "Cherokee" back then. So when he said that that was the tune he wanted me to play, I just said no, I wasn't going to do it. That's why Dizzy's playing trumpet on "Ko-Ko," "Warmin' up a Riff," and "Meandering" on Charlie Parker’s Reboppers, because I wasn't going to get out there and embarrass myself. I didn't really think I was ready to play tunes at the tempo of "Cherokee" and I didn't make no bones about it."[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko-Ko#cite_note-Miles_Autobiography-8)

Dizzy Gillespie also confirms that he played trumpet on "Ko Ko" in an interview with Stanley Crouch in 1986, and that the trumpet intro was composed by Charlie Parker.[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko-Ko#cite_note-An_Interview_with_Dizzy_Gillespie-9)

The song begins with a harmonically ambiguous introduction but quickly transitions to B flat major at the top of Parker's first solo chorus. At this point, the harmony is now based upon the chord changes of the song " [Cherokee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherokee_(Ray_Noble_song) "Cherokee (Ray Noble song)")" by [Ray Noble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Noble "Ray Noble"). A drum solo by [Max Roach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Roach "Max Roach") ends the transition. The closing material is very similar to the introduction and features an unexpected ending.

## Overview

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ko-Ko&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Overview")\]

A [recording ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1942%E2%80%9344_musicians'_strike "1942–44 musicians' strike"), imposed by the [American Federation of Musicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Federation_of_Musicians "American Federation of Musicians") from 1942 to 1944, prevented musicians in the nascent bebop movement from recording new works during the crucial formative period of this emerging genre. As a result, the "Ko Ko" session, along with the "Shaw 'Nuff" session led by [Dizzy Gillespie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dizzy_Gillespie "Dizzy Gillespie") earlier in the year, is considered by many to be the very first time [Bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop "Bebop") was ever recorded.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko-Ko#cite_note-10)[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko-Ko#cite_note-11)

[Charlie Parker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Parker "Charlie Parker") said that while playing [Ray Noble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Noble_(musician) "Ray Noble (musician)")'s tune " [Cherokee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherokee_(Ray_Noble_song) "Cherokee (Ray Noble song)")", "I found that by using the higher intervals of a chord as a melody line and backing them with appropriately related changes, I could play the thing I'd been hearing."[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko-Ko#cite_note-12) He had played that piece so many times that by the end he hated it, but he had mastered the chords perfectly in all 12 keys. "Ko Ko" has a partially improvised head and the chords are based on "Cherokee".[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko-Ko#cite_note-13)

In 2002, the [Library of Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Congress "Library of Congress") added "Ko Ko" to the [National Recording Registry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Recording_Registry "National Recording Registry").[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko-Ko#cite_note-14)

## Recording session

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ko-Ko&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Recording session")\]

Main article: [The Charlie Parker Story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Charlie_Parker_Story "The Charlie Parker Story")

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/7/79/Ko_Ko_Savoy_78_RPM.jpg/250px-Ko_Ko_Savoy_78_RPM.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ko_Ko_Savoy_78_RPM.jpg) Charlie Parker's Ri Bop Boys – _Ko Ko_

"Ko Ko" was recorded on November 26, 1945, at WOR studios in [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City "New York City"). In the booklet accompanying _Charlie Parker: The Complete Savoy Studio Sessions_ author James Patrick gives a full account of the session, informed by "\[d\]ocuments from the Savoy files and the recollections of Teddy Reig, who produced the session ..."

> A standard three hour/four side session was scheduled for November 26, 1945, at the WOR studios in New York for which Parker would supply original composititions. A Union contract was arranged the preceding week and Parker; [Miles Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miles_Davis "Miles Davis"), [trumpet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trumpet "Trumpet"); [Bud Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bud_Powell "Bud Powell"), [piano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piano "Piano"); [Curly Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curly_Russell "Curly Russell"), [bass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_bass "Double bass"); and [Max Roach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Roach "Max Roach"), [drums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum_kit "Drum kit"), were booked for the date. On the 26th Reig went to Parker's apartment to bring Bird to WOR and was informed the Powell had gone with his mother to Philadelphia where she was buying a house. No need to worry, however; [Dizzy Gillespie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dizzy_Gillespie "Dizzy Gillespie") was present and introduced to Reig: " _Here's your piano player_". Parker also had contacted pianist Argonne Thornton (later a.k.a. [Sadik Hakim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadik_Hakim "Sadik Hakim")) ... and asked that he appear at the studio ...[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko-Ko#cite_note-complete_notes-5)

Other recordings at this session were " [Billie's Bounce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billie's_Bounce "Billie's Bounce")", "Warming Up a Riff", "Now's the Time", " [Thriving on a Riff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thriving_on_a_Riff "Thriving on a Riff")", and "Meandering". The album _[The Charlie Parker Story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Charlie_Parker_Story "The Charlie Parker Story")_ fully documents this session, as does the aforementioned _Complete Savoy Studio Sessions_ box set.

[Duke Ellington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_Ellington "Duke Ellington") also wrote and recorded an unrelated song entitled "Ko-Ko" in 1940 at Victor's studios in Chicago.[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko-Ko#cite_note-15) Ellington's version of the song was inducted into the [Grammy Hall of Fame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammy_Hall_of_Fame "Grammy Hall of Fame") in 2011.[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko-Ko#cite_note-16)

## Structure

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ko-Ko&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Structure")\]

There were two takes:

In both takes the piece starts with a 32-bar introduction that was written by Charlie Parker:

- Bars 1–8 – [Alto saxophone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alto_saxophone "Alto saxophone") and [trumpet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trumpet "Trumpet") in [unison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unison "Unison") octaves
- Bars 9–16 – Brief trumpet solo
- Bars 17–24 – Brief saxophone solo
- Bars 25–32 – Alto saxophone and trumpet in thirds/fourths, then briefly in octaves

Following the intro in the first take, sax and trumpet begin the melody of "Cherokee". They are interrupted by someone clapping and whistling and shouting "You can't play that".

After the introduction in the second take are two 64-bar solo choruses from [Parker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Parker "Charlie Parker") on the saxophone; each chorus follows the [Thirty-two-bar form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty-two-bar_form "Thirty-two-bar form") (AABA), except that the number of bars is doubled to 64, partly due to the extensive importance of [solos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solo_(music) "Solo (music)") in bebop music, and partly due to the extremely fast [300bpm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tempo#Extreme_tempo "Tempo") tempo. The absence of any composed material on this recording, besides the introduction and coda, is a telling example of the bebop musicians' strong emphasis on improvisation first. It is an extremely [virtuosic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtuoso "Virtuoso") solo, incorporating fast eighth note playing and energetic [improvisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_improvisation "Musical improvisation"). Parker's use of accents keep his phrasing from sounding rhythmically monotonous. This phrasing style gives his bebop soloing a vocal and melodic quality even as his tactfully executed lines fly by at nearly imperceptible speed. The second chorus of Parker's solo opens with a two-bar quote from the notably difficult [clarinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarinet "Clarinet") piece " [High Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Society_(composition) "High Society (composition)")", made famous by clarinetist [Alphonse Picou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphonse_Picou "Alphonse Picou"). Charlie Parker was known to quote melodies from a variety of musical traditions in his improvised solos, and this particular solo is no exception.

After the solo from Parker is a 32-bar [drum solo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum_solo "Drum solo") from drummer Max Roach. The drums for the piece are [tuned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum_set_tuning "Drum set tuning") higher than normal, which gives the solo a brighter, livelier feel. Roach also pushes forward the tempo in an exciting fashion, but it pulls back collectively to the original tempo soon after Parker and Gillespie reenter.

The piece finishes with a 28-bar [coda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coda_(music) "Coda (music)"), integrating the main themes from the introduction and improvisation from Parker and Gillespie, and finally an abrupt ending. The ending feels unresolved, like an [imperfect cadence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperfect_cadence "Imperfect cadence"), because the bassist, Curly Russell plays an F as the final note. Even without harmonic context, the F sounds like a [dominant chord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominant_(music) "Dominant (music)") with no [resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resolution_(music) "Resolution (music)") due to the strong [tonicization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonicization "Tonicization") of B flat major throughout the recording.

## Releases

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ko-Ko&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Releases")\]

"Ko Ko" was issued and reissued many times over the years. The original release was as the B-side coupled with [Don Byas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Byas "Don Byas")' rendition of " [How High the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_High_the_Moon "How High the Moon")" on a 78 rpm Savoy 597. This record was reissued as Savoy 916, the Savoy 900 series being dedicated to [Bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop "Bebop"). On both records the group was credited as "Charlie Parker's Ri Bop Boys".

The song was subsequently released on Savoy XP 8001: _Charlie Parker, Vol. 2_ (7 inch EP) and Savoy MG 9000: _Charlie Parker, Vol. 1_ (10 inch LP) when these formats came into vogue in the early 1950s. It was also released on Savoy XP 8097: _Bird – Diz – Bud – Max, Vol. 1_ and Savoy MG 9034: _Bird – Diz – Bud – Max_ on the same formats. These were compilations of bebop tracks by Parker, Gillespie, Powell and Roach; on both of these latter releases the title was listed as "Co-coa".

"Ko Ko"'s next release was on Savoy MG 12014: _The Genius of Charlie Parker_. This was also its first 12-inch LP release. _[The Charlie Parker Story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Charlie_Parker_Story "The Charlie Parker Story")_ was released next, which included all of the takes from the entire session. This album was the first to release the aborted first take of "Ko Ko" along with the previously released master take.

The master take of "Ko Ko" was released next on Savoy 12126: _The Jazz Hour_, a compilation of various Savoy artists; and then on Savoy SJL 2201: _Bird/The Savoy Recordings (Master Takes)_.

And, as noted above, the entire session was reissued again on the box set _Charlie Parker: The Complete Savoy Studio Recordings_.[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko-Ko#cite_note-jazzdisco-17)
