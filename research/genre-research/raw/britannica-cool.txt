## Chamber jazz and the [Modern Jazz Quartet](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Modern-Jazz-Quartet)

Perhaps in reaction to the hot, more strident, more frenetic expressions of the postwar bands, or perhaps as a direct influence of the Thornhill-Evans approach, a cool strain entered the jazz scene in the late 1940s. Generated by [Lester Young](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lester-Young) and furthered by such reed players as [Lee Konitz](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lee-Konitz) and [Gerry Mulligan](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Gerry-Mulligan), [cool jazz](https://www.britannica.com/art/cool-jazz), along with its structural corollary—contrapuntal, harmonically slimmed-down (often pianoless) chamber jazz—was suddenly in. Understatement and a more relaxed expression replaced extroversion and high-tension virtuosity. Examples abound, beginning with the Miles Davis Nonet (1948–50)—a direct offspring in instrumentation and [musical](https://www.britannica.com/art/musical) intent of the Thornhill [band](https://www.britannica.com/art/band-music). In such pieces as “Boplicity,” “Israel,” “Move,” and “Moondreams,” fine improvised solos by Davis, Konitz, and Mulligan were meaningfully [integrated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/integrated) into the arrangers’ scores. Various octets, nonets, and other small ensembles soon followed suit, as did such West Coast-based quartets and quintets as those led by Mulligan, [Chet Baker](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Chet-Baker), Shelly Manne, Shorty Rogers, Jimmy Giuffre, and Chico Hamilton.

[![Modern Jazz Quartet](https://cdn.britannica.com/42/2742-050-01076DF3/Modern-Jazz-Quartet.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/42/2742-050-01076DF3/Modern-Jazz-Quartet.jpg)

[Modern Jazz Quartet](https://cdn.britannica.com/42/2742-050-01076DF3/Modern-Jazz-Quartet.jpg) The Modern Jazz Quartet, formed in 1953, incorporated elements of classical music into their repertory.(more)

On a slightly different tack, the [Modern Jazz Quartet](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Modern-Jazz-Quartet) (made up of [John Lewis](https://www.britannica.com/biography/John-Lewis-American-musician) on piano; [Milt Jackson](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Milt-Jackson) on vibraphone; Percy Heath on bass; and [Kenny Clarke](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Kenny-Clarke), soon replaced by Connie Kay, on drums) was formed in 1953. After his years with [Dizzy Gillespie](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Dizzy-Gillespie), Lewis had been inspired further by his study of classical [music](https://www.britannica.com/art/music), especially the work of [Johann Sebastian Bach](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Johann-Sebastian-Bach). Thus, Lewis brought a new kind of compositional (often contrapuntal) [integration](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/integration) to the group’s repertory, particularly in fugal or quasi-fugal pieces, such as the early “Vendome” or the later “Three Windows” and the album-length work _The Comedy_. Above all, in these performances Lewis sought to bring [collective](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/collective) [improvisation](https://www.britannica.com/art/improvisation-music) back from earlier times; many striking examples can be heard on the recordings made by the Modern Jazz Quartet over a period of 20 years, especially in the frequent, remarkable same-register duets of Lewis and Jackson.

## Jazz meets classical and the “ [third stream](https://www.britannica.com/art/third-stream)” begins

Contributor Callout

Read _Britannica_’s biography of [Gunther Schuller](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Gunther-Schuller), the author of this article and a noted composer, performer, conductor, teacher, and writer.

It was also in the 1950s that a greater rapprochement between jazz and classical music began to emerge. Like John Lewis, many other jazz musicians were studying much of the great [classical literature](https://www.britannica.com/art/classical-literature), from Bach to [Béla Bartók](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Bela-Bartok), to expand their musical horizons. Classical musicians, too, were listening more seriously to jazz and taking a professional interest in it. The ideological and technical barriers between jazz and classical music were beginning to break down. In that climate an apparently new concept or style, termed “third stream” by [Gunther Schuller](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Gunther-Schuller), arose. But third stream music was only apparently new, since European and American composers—including [Claude Debussy](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Claude-Debussy), [Igor Stravinsky](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Igor-Stravinsky), [Charles Ives](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Charles-Edward-Ives) (using ragtime), [Darius Milhaud](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Darius-Milhaud), [Maurice Ravel](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Maurice-Ravel), [Aaron Copland](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Aaron-Copland), [John Alden Carpenter](https://www.britannica.com/biography/John-Alden-Carpenter), [Kurt Weill](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Kurt-Weill), and many others—had employed elements of jazz since early in the century. The difference in the 1950s and ’60s was that (1) the third stream amalgams began to include improvisation and (2) the traffic was now no longer on a one-way street from classical music toward jazz but was flowing in both directions. Spearheaded by Lewis and Schuller, the movement produced a wide variety of works and varying approaches to the process of cross-fertilization. Third stream began, particularly in the [cultivated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/cultivated) hands of pianist Ran Blake, to mate classical concepts and techniques with all manner of ethnic and [vernacular](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/vernacular) musics and traditions as well as with jazz.

Though the term is now seldom used, the concept of third stream remains alive and well; [Charlie Haden](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Charlie-Haden) and Carla Bley’s Liberation Music Orchestra works and [Randy Weston](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Randy-Weston) and Melba Liston’s African-influenced [compositions](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/compositions) are cases in point. Third stream music is also called by other names: crossover, fusion, or [world music](https://www.britannica.com/art/global-music) (although the first two of these terms are more often used in discussions of [jazz-rock](https://www.britannica.com/art/jazz-rock)). So lively and penetrating has the stylistic intercourse been that it is nowadays often impossible to identify a piece as jazz, classical, or ethnic, proof that the third stream ideal of a true and complete fusion (not always technically possible in the 1960s) has at least partially been achieved.

Among the [myriad](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/myriad) contributions to third stream music over the years, Robert Graettinger’s works for various [Kenton](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Stan-Kenton) orchestras are crucial. Major atonal, polyphonically complex Graettinger compositions such as “City of Glass” (first performed in 1948) and his remarkable arrangements of standard popular songs reveal a talent of astonishing originality—showing little influence of Stravinsky, [Arnold Schoenberg](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Arnold-Schoenberg), Bartók, or any major jazz figures—especially unusual for a man so young (he died at the age of 34).

The syncopated beats of [Latin American music](https://www.britannica.com/art/Latin-American-music)—or what [Jelly Roll Morton](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jelly-Roll-Morton) referred to as a “Spanish tinge”—influenced the early jazz of [New Orleans](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Orleans-Louisiana), in particular the four-beat [rhythm](https://www.britannica.com/art/rhythm-music) pattern of the Cuban habanera. During the [swing](https://www.britannica.com/art/swing-music) era, many bands began featuring Afro-Cuban dance music, such as [rumbas](https://www.britannica.com/art/rumba-dance) and congas, in their [repertoire](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/repertoire). By the 1940s [Latin jazz](https://www.britannica.com/art/Latin-jazz) had become a full-fledged [genre](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/genre) of its own, a fusion of Afro-Cuban and Caribbean sounds that coalesced in New York City with the Machito and the Afro-Cubans orchestras, led by Cuban-born trumpeter Mario Bauzá and singer and percussionist Frank (“Machito”) Grillo. Bauzá’s tune “Tanga,” one of the Machito [orchestra’s](https://www.britannica.com/art/orchestra-music) early hits, is often considered to be the first true example of Latin jazz.

Later in the decade saw combinations of big band and Afro-Cuban percussion in recordings by [Stan Kenton](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Stan-Kenton), such as “Cuban Carnival” in 1947. At the same time Dizzy Gillespie [collaborated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/collaborated) with Cuban percussionist, dancer, and composer Chano Pozo; their synthesis of [bebop](https://www.britannica.com/art/bebop) and Afro-Cuban dance rhythms, as in the 1947 hit “Manteca,” was sometimes described as “Cubop.” Prominent musicians of this style in the 1950s, when the genre began to be called Latin jazz, include arranger Arturo (“Chico”) O’Farrill and pianists Frank Emilio Flynn and Ramón (“Bebo”) Valdés, as well as pianists George Shearing and Eddie Cano, percussionists Cal Tjader and Willie Bobo, and bassist Al McKibbon on the West Coast.

Latin jazz introduced or heavily featured such instruments as conga and [bongo drums](https://www.britannica.com/art/bongo-drum) (played by such standout artists as Cándido Camero and Mongo Santamaría); maracas (the choice of Machito vocalist Grillo); and [vibraphone](https://www.britannica.com/art/vibraphone) and timbales (paired high-pitched drums), as in the Puerto Rican-flavored music of bandleader and percussionist [Tito Puente](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Tito-Puente). Cuban band Irakere, founded by pianist Jesús (“Chucho”) Valdés (son of Bebo Valdés) and arranger and conductor Armando de Sequeira Romeu, employed the full range of percussion instruments, from maracas, bongos, and congas to bata drums, guiro, [claves](https://www.britannica.com/art/claves), and cowbells.

The 1960s brought the Brazilian export [bossa nova](https://www.britannica.com/art/bossa-nova) (“new trend”) to American jazz. A syncopated, sparsely accompanied sound that evolved from a union of [samba](https://www.britannica.com/art/samba-dance) (a Brazilian dance and music) and [cool jazz](https://www.britannica.com/art/cool-jazz), bossa nova was the [innovation](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/innovation) of composer [Antônio Carlos Jobim](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Antonio-Carlos-Jobim) and guitarist João Gilberto. In the mid-1960s bossa nova began to be associated in Brazil with social protest movements, as in the work of singer Nara Leão.

For many American listeners, however, the quintessential bossa nova track is saxophonist [Stan Getz](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Stan-Getz)’s hit “The Girl from Ipanema” (1963), a collaboration with Gilberto and Jobim (and an unexpected vocal from Gilberto’s wife, actress [Astrud Gilberto](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Astrud-Gilberto), who had never before sung professionally). The genre was further popularized in the late 1960s and ’70s with the success and “easy listening” style of pianist, composer, and arranger Sérgio Mendes and his ensemble Brasil ’66, after their being signed in [Los Angeles](https://www.britannica.com/place/Los-Angeles-California) by trumpeter and A&M executive [Herb Alpert](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Herb-Alpert).

In the meantime, the jazz mainstream continually broadened and expanded through the contributions of a wide range of talents from saxophonists [Sonny Rollins](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Sonny-Rollins), [John Coltrane](https://www.britannica.com/biography/John-Coltrane), [Eric Dolphy](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Eric-Dolphy), bassist-composer [Charles Mingus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Charles-Mingus), and composer-theorist George Russell to pianists [Cecil Taylor](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Cecil-Taylor), [Bill Evans](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Bill-Evans), and [Dave Brubeck](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Dave-Brubeck). Miles Davis and Coltrane exerted the greatest influence, Coltrane especially; he inadvertently bred thousands of clones who copied his sound and turned his every move into a cliché. Much more difficult to imitate and to absorb was the music of Dolphy, who, along with his unequaled mastery of alto [saxophone](https://www.britannica.com/art/saxophone) and flute, was the first to conquer the bass [clarinet](https://www.britannica.com/art/clarinet) as a jazz instrument. “Stormy Weather” (1960), his nearly 14-minute-long duet improvisation on alto with Mingus, must be counted as one of the greatest creative efforts in all of jazz.

The great wonder of jazz is its open-endedness, allowing truly talented musicians to explore new stylistic and [conceptual](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/conceptual) avenues. Such was the case with Rollins, who—instead of merely releasing a string of unrelated musical ideas—was the first to develop thematic improvisation in such a way that themes or motifs were varied and revisited within a single performance. Equally important was the work of [Lennie Tristano](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lennie-Tristano), who not only as early as 1945 was successfully exploring the possibilities of atonal improvisation but later through his students (saxophonists [Lee Konitz](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lee-Konitz) and [Warne Marsh](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Warne-Marsh) and composer Bill Russo) created [yet](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/yet) another school of jazz playing that emphasized contrapuntal and polyphonic linearity and lean and clear textures of, at times, almost classical austerity.

A remarkably gifted musician with a deep humility regarding jazz and his art, Coltrane (probably under the influence of Davis) abandoned his earlier fascination with the burgeoning harmonic language of bop—especially Monk’s unique tonal explorations—and began experimenting with modal jazz and single chords. This led to extended improvisations, often lasting as long as an hour. Although some observers regarded this type of playing as “practicing in public,” his recordings during this phase convey work that was always proficient and often brilliant, if relatively subdued and cautious.

[Art Blakey](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Art-Blakey)’s Jazz Messengers, the most renowned and respected of the “traveling conservatories,” held forth in the world’s jazz clubs and [concert](https://www.britannica.com/art/concert) halls for more than three decades, hatching a long line of talented players ranging from [Horace Silver](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Horace-Silver), [Kenny Dorham](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Kenny-Dorham), and [Lee Morgan](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lee-Morgan) (in the 1950s) to Freddie Hubbard, [Keith Jarrett](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Keith-Jarrett), Woody Shaw, and (in the 1980s) [Wynton Marsalis](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Marsalis-family).

[![Miles Davis](https://cdn.britannica.com/22/7922-050-C6CF141C/Miles-Davis-1969.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/22/7922-050-C6CF141C/Miles-Davis-1969.jpg)

[Miles Davis](https://cdn.britannica.com/22/7922-050-C6CF141C/Miles-Davis-1969.jpg) American musician and composer Miles Davis, 1969.(more)

Initially a loyal [disciple](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/disciple) of Gillespie, Davis by the late 1950s knew that he had neither the embouchure (the position of the lips, tongue, or teeth while playing an instrument) nor the ear for Gillespie’s pyrotechnics. Under the [benign](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/benign) influence of [Gil Evans](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Gil-Evans), John Lewis, and others, he turned to an opulent, more lyrical style with which he and Evans were to make dramatic musical history in such recordings as _Miles Ahead_ (1957) and Evans’s inspired recomposing of [George Gershwin](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Gershwin)’s _Porgy and Bess_ (1958). Davis abandoned conventional major and minor harmonies for modal and pentatonic patterns (first fully aired in 1959 on the album _[Kind of Blue](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Kind-of-Blue)_), a plunge into a meandering harmonic no-man’s-land that influenced much of jazz of the mid-20th century. Modal playing, with its long [pedal points](https://www.britannica.com/art/pedal-point) (repeated or sustained bass tones) and one-chord bass [ostinatos](https://www.britannica.com/art/ostinato) (repetitions of a short melodic phrase), allowed by definition no harmonic progression or forward movement, and critics faulted the genre in a structural stasis that only the greatest improvisers could overcome. For Davis, however, this era produced some of his most soulful and emotionally powerful playing.

[![Charles Mingus](https://cdn.britannica.com/48/197648-050-1E976AB8/Charles-Mingus-1974.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/48/197648-050-1E976AB8/Charles-Mingus-1974.jpg)

[Charles Mingus](https://cdn.britannica.com/48/197648-050-1E976AB8/Charles-Mingus-1974.jpg) Jazz bassist Charles Mingus, 1974.(more)

Mingus, together with Parker and Gillespie, was among the most gifted of all the postwar giants. A major composer in the full creative sense as well as a brilliant bass virtuoso and [formidable](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/formidable) bandleader, Mingus experimented with extended forms as early as the late 1940s (“Mingus Fingers” with Lionel Hampton). His [oeuvre](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/oeuvre) ranges from early simple [blues](https://www.britannica.com/art/blues-music) and atonal free-form pieces to such poetically named jazz [instrumentals](https://www.britannica.com/art/instrumentals) as “Pithecanthropus Erectus” (1956), “Haitian Fight Song” (1957), “Fables of Faubus” (1959), and “Peggy’s Blue Skylight” (1961) to the monumental two-and-a-half-hour, posthumously premiered _Epitaph_. Accumulated between the early 1940s and 1962 and composed for 31 instruments, _Epitaph_ is a gigantic summation of everything Mingus felt and heard in music, from the gentlest lyric ballads and earthy blues to the most complex and advanced Ivesian and Stravinskian orchestral excursions.

# [Free jazz](https://www.britannica.com/art/free-jazz) and fusion: The explorations of [Ornette Coleman](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ornette-Coleman), Weather Report, and the global jazz scene

Whereas most of these postwar musicians worked out their individual styles through personal explorations within the central modern tradition, the arrival of saxophonist [Ornette Coleman](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ornette-Coleman) and trumpeter Donald Cherry [constituted](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constituted) an even more radical break from the recent past. [Eschewing](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Eschewing) conventional key and time signatures, Coleman also abandoned all the traditional jazz forms, arriving quickly at something that was to be called “ [free jazz](https://www.britannica.com/art/free-jazz).”

Although partially inspired by the Parker revolution, Coleman’s [music](https://www.britannica.com/art/music) also harkened back in its linear fragmentation, wailing [blues](https://www.britannica.com/art/blues-music) sonorities, and unconventional intonation to a much older, primitive, folklike blues and [work song](https://www.britannica.com/art/work-song-music) tradition, incidentally more or less cleansed of jazz’s earlier European borrowings. Given Coleman’s abandonment of traditional forms such as 12-bar blues and 32-bar song forms, it would be wrong to conclude that such works as “Change of the Century” (1959) or “Free Jazz” (1960) are therefore formless. Rather, they are simply subject to a new kind of organization where—in “Free Jazz,” for example—the eight players are each assigned “solo” sections accompanied by all the other players, with the various sections partitioned from each other by predetermined, collectively played motivic materials and the overall formal subdivisions thus clearly [delineated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/delineated).

[![Archie Shepp, 1982](https://cdn.britannica.com/21/163221-050-39C72C38/Archie-Shepp-1982.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/21/163221-050-39C72C38/Archie-Shepp-1982.jpg)

[Archie Shepp, 1982](https://cdn.britannica.com/21/163221-050-39C72C38/Archie-Shepp-1982.jpg) Saxophonist and free jazz pioneer Archie Shepp became known for his Afrocentric approach to music.(more)

Others who followed in Coleman’s footsteps—for example, the saxophonists [Albert Ayler](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Albert-Ayler), [Archie Shepp](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Archie-Shepp), and George Adams—sought to expand on his free-form [innovations](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/innovations), introducing such experimentations as distorted sounds or avant-garde and Afrocentric elements.

Creative shifts during the 1970s and ’80s eventually led, on the one hand, to a gigantic eclecticism where no style or [conception](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/conception) took priority and, on the other hand, to a profound sea change that dramatically altered the face of jazz. An especially important development was jazz fusion, or [jazz-rock](https://www.britannica.com/art/jazz-rock), which was more or less announced with [Miles Davis’s](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Miles-Davis) _Bitches Brew_ (1970), a landmark album that featured [electronic instruments](https://www.britannica.com/art/electronic-instrument) and experiments with [rock](https://www.britannica.com/art/rock-music) and [funk](https://www.britannica.com/art/funk). Others who joined him in exploring this direction were drummer Tony Williams, guitarist [John McLaughlin](https://www.britannica.com/biography/John-McLaughlin), saxophonist [Wayne Shorter](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Wayne-Shorter), and electric keyboardists Joe Zawinul, [Herbie Hancock](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Herbie-Hancock), Larry Young, and [Chick Corea](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Chick-Corea). Notably, Zawinul and Shorter formed Weather Report, a [seminal](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/seminal) group that initially included the classically trained Miroslav Vitouš on bass and Alphonse Mouzon on drums. Weather Report cycled through various innovative musicians in later years, such as bassist [Jaco Pastorius](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jaco-Pastorius), who lent his virtuoso technical wizardry to live and studio collaborations with Hancock. The two musicians formed a new group, Word of Mouth, with Shorter and fellow saxophonist Michael Brecker, [harmonica](https://www.britannica.com/art/harmonica) player Toots Thielemans, drummers Peter Erskine and Jack DeJohnette, and percussionist Don Alias.

By the late 20th century, fusion and free jazz had helped to considerably expand the borders of jazz to beyond the [Western Hemisphere](https://www.britannica.com/place/Western-Hemisphere). Inspired by the spiritual guru Sri Chinmoy, in 1971 McLaughlin took the name Mahavishnu and formed the Mahavishnu Orchestra, a quintet noted for radically high volume levels, complex textures, and fast modal playing. Later he favored the acoustic [guitar](https://www.britannica.com/art/guitar) for another new group, Shakti, founded with Indian violinist L. Shankar and [tabla](https://www.britannica.com/art/tabla) player [Zakir Hussain](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Zakir-Hussain). Free jazz flourished in Russia, with the folk-infused group The Ganelin Trio, and among South African musicians, with the Brotherhood of Breath incorporating kivela ( [kwela](https://www.britannica.com/art/kwela)), an urban dance music. Meanwhile, Japan produced one of the greatest 20th-century jazz artists (and one of the most important women in the genre): pianist, bandleader, and composer-arranger Toshiko Akiyoshi, who moved from [bebop](https://www.britannica.com/art/bebop) and big [band](https://www.britannica.com/art/band-music) sounds to [compositions](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/compositions) featuring Japanese harmonies and instruments, such as [taiko](https://www.britannica.com/art/taiko-musical-instrument) drums.

## Jazz at the turn of the 21st century

[![Wynton Marsalis, 2009](https://cdn.britannica.com/91/189491-050-12A00B87/Wynton-Marsalis-2009.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/91/189491-050-12A00B87/Wynton-Marsalis-2009.jpg)

[Wynton Marsalis, 2009](https://cdn.britannica.com/91/189491-050-12A00B87/Wynton-Marsalis-2009.jpg) American jazz trumpeter Wynton Marsalis became a full-fledged star by the time he was 20 years old. When he was 26, he cofounded the Jazz at Lincoln Center program, eventually earning a reputation as a national and global ambassador of jazz.(more)

By the turn of the 21st century, the personal, instantly recognizable distinctiveness of the great jazz players of the past had been replaced by an astonishing technical [assurance](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/assurance) and stylistic flexibility. Although many younger players in the 1990s may have sounded very much alike to listeners accustomed to the individualistic flourishes of earlier musicians, there were undeniable standouts such as trumpeters [Wynton Marsalis](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Marsalis-family), [Tom Harrell](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Tom-Harrell), [Randy Brecker](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Randy-Brecker), and Dave Douglas, saxophonists Steve Lacy and Joe Lovano, trombonist [Albert Mangelsdorff](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Albert-Mangelsdorff), pianist Gonzalo Rubalcaba, and bassist John Patitucci. Whereas later players functioned well in any stylistic context—even beyond jazz in global and classical realms—the earlier players, great as they were, could not reach out into other stylistic regions. The players of yore did not—could not, in most cases—go to music schools and were in essence self-taught, having learned on the job and to a large extent from each other and from their elders.

In the wake of these changes, [composition](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/composition) moved much more into the front and center of activities—as in the works of Leo Smith, [Henry Threadgill](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Henry-Threadgill), and Dave Douglas—which suggests that the long-standing conflict between [improvisation](https://www.britannica.com/art/improvisation-music) and composition may have finally been resolved. A good part of the reason for this is that most later jazz musicians went to music school—conservatories and university or college music departments—where they took theory, music history, and general music survey courses, and in most cases they also studied with teachers who were themselves major jazz figures. In addition, starting in the 1970s, the enormously expanding number of recordings made available an [infinite](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/infinite) variety of [musical](https://www.britannica.com/art/musical) traditions [encompassing](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/encompassing) all jazz styles as well as a rainbow of ethnic, popular, and [vernacular](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/vernacular) musics of all persuasions and philosophies. The younger generations took advantage of this [plethora](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/plethora) of musical and stylistic resources.

Indeed, the progeny of many earlier musicians proved themselves to be distinctive artists in their own right. Foremost among this new generation were the [Marsalis brothers](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Marsalis-family) (Wynton, Branford, Delfeayo, and Jason)—who were reared on jazz by their father, the pianist Ellis Marsalis—and singer [Norah Jones](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Norah-Jones), the daughter of Indian [sitar](https://www.britannica.com/art/sitar) virtuoso [Ravi Shankar](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ravi-Shankar).

Other developments at the turn of the 21st century included the emergence of “smooth jazz,” so-called for its polished, radio-friendly sounds and commercial appeal. Its quality ranged from bland “elevator music” to soulful and [sublime](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/sublime) recordings by such performers as American singers [Al Jarreau](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Al-Jarreau) and [Anita Baker](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Anita-Baker) and Nigerian-born British singer [Sade](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Sade). Traditional jazz singers whose popularity proved that standards never go out of style included [Harry Connick, Jr.](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Harry-Connick-Jr), and [Michael Bublé](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Michael-Buble-Canadian-singer).

Yet many more continued to experiment with global styles, as with the musical adventurousness of bassist and singer [Esperanza Spalding](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Esperanza-Spalding), or branched out into entirely new [genres](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/genres), such as [hip-hop](https://www.britannica.com/art/hip-hop) and [techno](https://www.britannica.com/art/techno-music). These crossover projects were pioneered especially by Herbie Hancock and producer, composer-arranger, and trumpeter [Quincy Jones](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Quincy-Jones) beginning in the 1980s, carried into the ’90s by the [rap](https://www.britannica.com/art/rap) groups [De La Soul](https://www.britannica.com/topic/De-La-Soul) and [A Tribe Called Quest](https://www.britannica.com/topic/A-Tribe-Called-Quest), and picked up in the new millennium by rapper [Kendrick Lamar](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Kendrick-Lamar).

Where this continuing [innovation](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/innovation) leaves jazz and where jazz goes in the future—indeed, whether jazz can endure as a distinct musical [idiom](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/idiom) or language—are unanswerable questions. The one truism about jazz is that it remains distinguishable not by what is played but by how it is played.
